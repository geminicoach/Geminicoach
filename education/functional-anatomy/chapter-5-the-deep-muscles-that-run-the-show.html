The Deep Muscles That Run the Show

Underneath the muscles you can see are the muscles that actually run the joint. This closes out the anatomy half of the course.

~18 min readInteractive: Match the deep muscleRecognition, not diagnosis

Chapter 2 gave you the superficial map, the muscles you can see and grab, the ones that show up in a mirror and in a hypertrophy program. This chapter goes one layer deeper. Underneath the deltoid, the lats, the glutes, and the abdominals sits a second set of muscles you will rarely see and often cannot palpate directly. They do not build the shape of a physique. They do something more important for a coach: they center joints, stabilize the spine, and quietly explain a huge share of the complaints your clients bring you, the cranky shoulder, the low back that "just tightens up," the tingling down a leg, the neck that never lets go.

None of what follows is diagnosis. Your job as a coach is recognition, understanding the logic of these muscles well enough to modify a program sensibly and to know when a complaint has crossed the line into something that needs a physical therapist, chiropractor, or physician. We will flag that line clearly where it matters, especially with anything involving a nerve.

Deep versus superficial, a quick refresher

In Chapter 2 you learned to read the superficial layer, the muscles closest to the skin, the ones a client's shirt hides but a mirror reveals. Deep muscles sit closer to the bone, often directly against the joint capsule itself. As a rule, the deeper a muscle sits, the smaller and more numerous its attachments tend to be, and the more its job leans toward stabilizing a joint rather than producing a big, visible movement. A superficial muscle like the deltoid can throw the arm overhead with force. A deep muscle like supraspinatus, sitting right under it, does something quieter but just as necessary, it holds the ball of the shoulder seated in its socket while the deltoid does the throwing. Lose that quiet job and the big, visible movement starts to hurt.

The rotator cuff: SITS, the shoulder's steering committee

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, and that mobility comes at a cost, the socket (the glenoid) is shallow, more like a golf tee than a golf ball resting in a cup. Something has to keep the head of the humerus centered in that shallow socket through every overhead reach, throw, and press. That job belongs to four deep muscles collectively called the rotator cuff, remembered by the acronym SITS: supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis. All four run from the scapula (shoulder blade) to the head of the humerus, wrapping around the joint like a cuff, which is exactly where the name comes from.

  • Supraspinatus sits in the small trough above the spine of the scapula (the supraspinous fossa) and runs out to the top of the humerus. Its main job is to initiate and assist raising the arm out to the side, abduction, especially the first ten to fifteen degrees before the deltoid takes over the bulk of the lift.
  • Infraspinatus fills the larger trough below the scapular spine (the infraspinous fossa) and attaches to the back of the humeral head. It is a prime external rotator, the muscle that turns your palm outward when your elbow is tucked at your side.
  • Teres minor is a small, cord-like muscle along the outer edge of the scapula, running to the humerus just below infraspinatus. It assists external rotation and helps pull the humeral head down and back during overhead motion.
  • Subscapularis is the odd one out, it sits on the front of the scapula, between the shoulder blade and the ribcage, invisible from behind and impossible to palpate directly. It is the main internal rotator, turning the palm inward, and it balances the pull of the other three from the front.

Individually, each SITS muscle produces a small rotation. Collectively, their real job is compression and centering, they co-contract to pull the humeral head snugly into the glenoid socket so that when the deltoid, the pec, and the lats produce a big, powerful motion, the joint has something stable to rotate around instead of grinding or shifting. This is why the rotator cuff matters to a coach even for clients who will never care about the names, a weak or fatigued cuff lets the humeral head drift upward during overhead work, narrowing the small space under the acromion where the supraspinatus tendon and a bursa live. That narrowing, repeated over thousands of reps, is the mechanical story behind shoulder impingement, one of the most common complaints you will hear from anyone who presses, throws, or swims.

Where recognition ends and referral begins

A client who describes a dull ache with overhead reaching, or pain sleeping on that side, is describing a pattern consistent with cuff irritation or impingement, useful for you to recognize so you can modify pressing volume, overhead angle, and warm-up work. That is different from a client who reports sharp, radiating pain, numbness, tingling, or weakness running down the arm into the hand. Nerve-type symptoms, anything that shoots, burns, or numbs along a line rather than staying local to the joint, are outside a coach's scope. Refer that client to a physical therapist or physician rather than guessing.

Quadratus lumborum: the deep low-back stabilizer

Move down to the low back and you will find quadratus lumborum, often shortened to QL, buried beneath the erector spinae group, running from the top of the pelvis (the iliac crest) up to the lowest rib and the transverse processes of the lumbar vertebrae. Its fibers run more or less vertically along the side of the spine, which tells you its action before anyone explains it, it laterally flexes the trunk, bending you sideways toward the same side, and it hikes the hip, pulling the pelvis up toward the ribcage when the leg is off the ground, exactly the motion you see when someone clears a foot over an obstacle without bending the knee.

Coaches care about QL because it is one of the most common culprits behind a nonspecific, one-sided low-back ache, especially in clients who stand or sit asymmetrically, favor one leg, or carry a bag on the same shoulder every day. A tight, overworked QL on one side often shows up as that dull, deep ache just above the belt line that a client cannot quite point to with one finger, because it sits too deep to point at precisely. It is also heavily recruited as a stabilizer during any single-leg or offset-load work, a suitcase carry, a single-arm row, a step-up, so unilateral training that a client is not used to can wake up a cranky QL that has been coasting.

Iliopsoas: the deep hip flexor tied to posture

The iliopsoas is really two muscles that merge into one tendon, psoas major, which runs from the sides of the lumbar vertebrae, and iliacus, which lines the inside bowl of the pelvis (the iliac fossa). Both converge and cross the front of the hip joint to insert on the lesser trochanter of the femur, a small bony knob on the inner thigh bone. That path, from the lumbar spine and pelvis down to the femur, makes it the body's strongest and deepest hip flexor, lifting the thigh toward the trunk, as in a high knee drive or the first phase of a sit-up.

Because psoas major actually originates on the lumbar spine itself, it does not just flex the hip, it also has a direct line of pull on the low back. A chronically shortened psoas, the kind that develops from years of long sitting, can tug the lumbar spine into more extension and tilt the pelvis forward, a pattern coaches usually describe as anterior pelvic tilt. This is why the classic image of the desk-bound client, a slight forward tilt of the pelvis and a low back that feels perpetually tight even though it is rarely the low back muscles themselves that are the problem, so often traces back to a hip flexor that has spent all day in a shortened position. It is also why hip flexor mobility work often calms complaints that seem, at first glance, to be about the low back rather than the hip.

Piriformis: deep in the glute, next to a major nerve

Piriformis is a small, pyramid-shaped muscle running from the front of the sacrum out to the top of the femur (the greater trochanter), sitting deep beneath the gluteus maximus. Its fiber direction makes it a hip external rotator, the same action infraspinatus produces at the shoulder, and with the hip flexed it can also assist hip abduction. On its own, piriformis is a minor player in raw force production. What makes it clinically relevant is its neighbor, the sciatic nerve, the thickest nerve in the body, which runs directly beneath piriformis on its way down the back of the leg, and in a meaningful minority of people actually pierces through the muscle itself.

When piriformis becomes tight, overworked, or spasmed, it can compress or irritate that nerve where it passes underneath, producing symptoms that mimic a lumbar disc problem, an ache deep in the glute that can radiate down the back of the thigh. This is worth recognizing because it explains a common client complaint, but it is also a clean example of where a coach's job stops. A local, deep glute ache that a client can reproduce by pressing on the muscle is something you can work around programmatically. Radiating symptoms, numbness, or a burning line running down the leg means the sciatic nerve is involved and the client needs a professional evaluation to sort out whether the cause is piriformis, a disc, or something else entirely.

Scalenes: accessory breathing muscles with a nerve and vessel problem of their own

On the side of the neck, running from the cervical vertebrae down to the first and second ribs, sit three small muscles called the scalenes, anterior, middle, and posterior. Their most reliable job is elevating the first two ribs during forceful inhalation, which is why they are called accessory breathing muscles, they help you breathe hard, not quietly. Most people barely use them at rest, but anyone who breathes shallowly and rapidly under stress, or who has adopted a habit of breathing from the chest rather than the diaphragm, ends up recruiting the scalenes far more than intended, and they can become chronically tight as a result.

The scalenes also matter because of what runs directly beside and between them, the brachial plexus, the bundle of nerves that supplies the entire arm, along with the subclavian artery and vein, pass through the narrow gap between the anterior and middle scalene on their way out to the shoulder and arm. That tight passageway is the anatomical basis of thoracic outlet syndrome, a condition where that neurovascular bundle gets compressed, producing tingling, weakness, or a heavy, cold feeling in the arm and hand. You do not need to diagnose thoracic outlet syndrome, but you should recognize the pattern, tight, overactive scalenes plus arm symptoms that come and go with posture or overhead position is a signal to refer out rather than stretch and hope.

Levator scapulae: the desk worker's knot

Levator scapulae runs from the transverse processes of the top four cervical vertebrae down to the upper corner of the scapula (the superior angle). As the name literally tells you, it lifts the scapula, shrugging the shoulder blade upward, and when the scapula is fixed it can tilt the neck toward the same side and rotate the chin away. In practice, this is the muscle behind one of the single most common complaints you will hear as a coach, that persistent, ropy knot at the base of the neck where it meets the top of the shoulder.

The reason it is so often the culprit is postural. Anyone who spends hours with the head forward and the shoulders rounded, looking at a screen, holding a phone between ear and shoulder, driving, is asking levator scapulae to hold a lengthened, loaded position for hours at a time, which is exactly the recipe for a muscle to become chronically tight and tender. It sits just deep enough to the upper trapezius that people often blame the trap for a knot that is actually levator scapulae underneath it, which is useful to know because the two respond to slightly different release and stretch positions.

Feel the difference between trap and levator scapulae

Have your client sit tall and drop the chin slightly toward the collarbone, then rotate the head about thirty degrees to the same side you are palpating. Place your fingers just above the top corner of the shoulder blade, close to where the neck meets the shoulder. The rope-like band you feel tighten as they gently lift that shoulder blade upward is levator scapulae, distinct from the broader, flatter upper trapezius sitting just above and behind it. Clients almost always point to this exact spot when they describe neck and shoulder tension.

Multifidus and the deep erector group: segment-by-segment control

The erector spinae you learned in Chapter 2, the long columns running up either side of the spine, produce big movements, extending and side-bending the whole trunk. Underneath that superficial group sits a deeper, shorter set of muscles, most notably multifidus, along with the rotatores and interspinales, running from vertebra to vertebra, sometimes spanning only one or two segments at a time. Where the superficial erectors move the spine as a whole, multifidus and its deep neighbors control it one joint at a time, fine-tuning the position of each vertebra relative to the next and resisting small, unwanted shears and rotations during everyday movement.

This distinction matters for coaches because of what happens when multifidus underperforms. Research on low-back pain consistently finds that people with a history of back pain show measurable atrophy and delayed activation of multifidus, even after the pain resolves, the muscle does not automatically catch back up. That gap is a large part of the logic behind so-called core stability work, planks, bird dogs, dead bugs, and slow, controlled anti-rotation and anti-extension drills are not primarily there to build a visible six-pack, they are there to re-train segmental control that a deep, hard-to-feel muscle has lost. You will rarely be able to palpate multifidus directly through the more superficial erectors, so your read on it comes mostly from movement quality, does the low back stay quiet and controlled through a hip hinge, or does one segment seem to give way or shift.

Why this pays off on the floor

Every deep muscle in this chapter follows the same coaching logic. First, recognize the pattern, the location of the ache, the movement that provokes it, the posture that feeds it. Second, connect it to what the muscle actually does, its origin-to-insertion logic tells you which position lengthens it, which position loads it, and which daily habit keeps it short. Third, know your limit, local, mechanical, movement-related complaints are yours to program around, anything radiating, numb, or vascular in nature belongs with a qualified clinician.

The takeaway

The deep muscles rarely get named in a gym conversation, but they are running the show underneath everything you can see and load. The rotator cuff centers the shoulder so the deltoid and pecs can do their work without grinding the joint. Quadratus lumborum stabilizes the low back through every offset carry and step. Iliopsoas links a full day of sitting to the tilt of the pelvis. Piriformis sits right on top of the body's largest nerve. The scalenes breathe hard and share a tight hallway with the nerves and vessels of the arm. Levator scapulae holds up a forward head for hours at a time. And multifidus, joint by joint, keeps the spine from shearing during the movements your clients do every single day. Recognizing these patterns, and knowing exactly where recognition has to hand off to a clinician, closes out the anatomy half of this course. From here, the second half turns to how you actually use all of this on the floor.
