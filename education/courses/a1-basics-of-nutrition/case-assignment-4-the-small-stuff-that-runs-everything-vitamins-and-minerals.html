Chapter 4: Reason Through a Real Complaint

Use the micronutrient chapter to explain a real complaint, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Lucia, 29
Consider Lucia, who took up distance running two years ago and now trains for half marathons. Eighteen months ago she shifted to a mostly plant-based diet she is genuinely proud of: lentils, tofu, beans, oats, leafy greens, lots of vegetables, plenty of variety, and, on paper, plenty of calories. For the last few months something has been off. Her easy runs feel hard, her race times have slipped, she is breathless on hills that used to be routine, she feels cold when her colleagues are comfortable, and she is tired in a way that sleep does not fix. She has not changed her training or her sleep, and eating more has not helped. She often has tea or coffee with meals. She wonders: "I am doing everything right, so why do I feel worse?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, what her symptom cluster points to. Which body function is failing, which nutrient sits at the center of it, and how do you know from the pattern of symptoms?
  2. Account for why this diet in particular, the one she is proud of, is ironically the mechanism. Bring in non-heme iron, her stacked risk factors, and how tea or coffee with meals fits in.
  3. Name one other nutrient worth flagging that travels with this dietary pattern, and explain why its large reserve makes it easy to miss until it surfaces.
  4. Give food-first, mechanism-aware suggestions within scope, then state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins, including why the correction should be guided by testing rather than self-prescribed.

Your Response

0 words
Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose or prescribe. A suspected deficiency is confirmed with lab testing, and correcting it, especially with supplements, which carry real toxicity risk, is a clinician's job. Iron in particular is a mineral where excess is harmful, so the dose is guided by testing, not guessed.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Links the symptom cluster (fatigue, breathlessness, cold intolerance, falling endurance) to oxygen delivery and identifies iron as the likely nutrient.
  • Explains the non-heme iron mechanism and the stacked risk factors: menstruation, endurance training, and a mostly plant-based diet, with tea or coffee cutting uptake.
  • Flags vitamin B12 as a second at-risk nutrient and uses the large-liver-reserve reasoning to explain why it stays silent.
  • Offers food-first, mechanism-aware suggestions within scope: pair non-heme iron with vitamin C, move tea and coffee between meals, add a reliable B12 source.
  • States that a suspected deficiency needs lab confirmation and a clinician, that supplements are not treatments to self-prescribe, and does not diagnose.
