Part C · Protein, Up Close

  1. In your own words, explain the difference between a complete and an incomplete protein, and say what a "limiting" amino acid is.
  2. Rice runs low on lysine; beans run low on methionine but are rich in lysine. Explain why a bowl of rice and beans covers the gap, and why you do not have to combine them in the same meal to benefit.
  3. The chapter separates the protein floor (about 0.8 g per kg of bodyweight) from a more useful target (about 1.2 to 1.6 g per kg for active or older adults). Explain why the floor is not the same as a goal, and name one group that benefits from going above it.

Part D · Carbohydrate and Its Fiber Carrier

  1. A medium apple and a glass of apple juice can carry similar total carbohydrate. Name what is physically removed in juicing, and explain how that changes how full you feel and how your gut microbes respond.
  2. Describe the "spike and crash" that fast, refined carbohydrate can cause, naming the hormone involved. Then explain how eating that carbohydrate with its fiber intact, or alongside protein and fat, changes the shape of the blood-sugar rise.
  3. The chapter says "simple equals fast, complex equals slow" is a useful first approximation but not a law. Give one example that breaks the rule and say why.

Part E · Fat, Density, and Why a Calorie Is Not Just a Calorie

  1. Fat carries 9 kcal per gram, more than double protein or carbohydrate. Name the three jobs fat does at once (beyond being a fuel store), including its structural role and the vitamins it helps you absorb.
  2. Two 200 kcal snacks: a small handful of nuts and a large bowl of grapes. Using the idea of energy density, explain which is likely to leave you fuller and which makes it easier to overshoot your calorie target.
  3. "A calorie is a calorie" is true in a bomb calorimeter but misleading for a human body. Using the three differences from the chapter (thermic cost, satiety signals, and where each macro is routed), explain why a calorie of protein and a calorie of fat are not interchangeable.
  4. Real foods are mixtures. Read the dominant macronutrient in each of these and name the supporting player: an egg, a bowl of beans, a handful of nuts.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds nutrition literacy, not a license to diagnose, prescribe a therapeutic diet, or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the macronutrients work, and treat anything that looks clinical (a diagnosed medical condition, rapid unexplained weight loss, signs of disordered eating, pregnancy, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician or registered dietitian. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
