Chapter 3: Reason Through a Real Question

Take the big three and use them to answer a real question about eating, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 20 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Sean, 29
Consider Sean, who went mostly plant-based two years ago and now trains with weights three times a week. He wants to keep building strength, but he has read online that "you cannot get complete protein without meat," and he is worried. A typical day for him: oatmeal with fruit for breakfast, a big mixed-greens salad with avocado for lunch, pasta with vegetables for dinner, plus fruit and crackers as snacks. He says he eats plenty of food and is never hungry, yet his strength has stalled and he has lost a little muscle definition. He is convinced the problem is that plant protein is somehow second-rate. He asks: "Is it true I can't build on plants, and what am I getting wrong?"

Your Tasks

  1. Run Sean's typical day through the mixtures idea from the chapter: identify the dominant macronutrient in each thing he eats, and say what is conspicuously missing.
  2. Separate the two things he might be worried about, protein quality (complete versus incomplete) and protein quantity (how much per day), and decide which one is the more likely cause of his stall, with your reasoning.
  3. Address the "you cannot get complete protein without meat" claim directly, using complete versus incomplete, the complementary-protein principle, and variety across the day.
  4. Give practical, general-education suggestions Sean could act on, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Your Response

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing or prescribing a diet. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis, therapeutic diets, and treatment belong to a qualified clinician or registered dietitian.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Distinguishes protein quantity from protein quality and identifies quantity as the likely issue in Sean's day.
  • Explains complete versus incomplete protein and the complementary-protein principle (grains plus legumes, variety across the day, soy as a complete exception).
  • Applies the mixtures idea to show his meals lack a protein anchor, and suggests concrete plant anchors plus a sensible target range (nearer 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg than the 0.8 floor).
  • Frames the completeness fear as largely unfounded without overstating it.
  • Identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose or prescribe.
