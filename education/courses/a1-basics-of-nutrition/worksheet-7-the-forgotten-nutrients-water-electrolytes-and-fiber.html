Part B · How the Body Keeps Water in Balance

The chapter describes water balance as a flow-through system, not a bucket you fill to a line. Answer each in one or two full sentences.

  1. Name the two sensors that detect when blood is getting too concentrated or blood volume is dropping, and say what each one monitors.
  2. Explain the job of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and how it changes what the kidneys do with water. Why does a dehydrated person pass a small amount of dark urine?
  3. The chapter says your output is not fixed. Explain what happens to an extra liter of water you drink but do not need, and why that means the "bucket you fill to a line" picture is wrong.

Part D · The Charge That Makes Water Work

  1. Distinguish, in a sentence each, what sodium and potassium mainly do and where each sits: which ion governs the fluid outside cells, and which does most of its work inside them?
  2. Explain what the sodium-potassium pump maintains across the cell membrane, and why that stored gradient is described as the currency of every nerve impulse and heartbeat.
  3. The chapter calls it "the sodium paradox." Explain why the general-population message is to lower sodium while a heavily sweating endurance athlete mid-event may need to add it back. Why can a single rule not serve both?
  4. Most people in industrialized diets are high in sodium and low in potassium at the same time. Name the single dietary change that improves both at once, and explain why it works on both minerals.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds nutrition literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how water, electrolytes, and fiber work, and treat anything that looks clinical (blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, a persistent change in bowel habits, fainting, confusion, or a medication interaction) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
