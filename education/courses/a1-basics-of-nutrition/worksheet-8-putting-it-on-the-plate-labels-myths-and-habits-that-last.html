Part E · Design a Habit That Holds

Use the habit science from the chapter: automaticity over motivation, a stable anchor, the trend over the day, and supporting the signals rather than fighting them.

  1. The "21 days to a habit" claim is itself a myth. Give the more accurate picture from the research (roughly how long, and how much it varies), and explain why a single missed day does not break the curve.
  2. Write one anchored habit in the form "After I [existing daily event], I will [small new behavior]." Then explain why welding the behavior to an existing cue does the work that willpower could not.
  3. Explain why judging progress on a two-to-four-week trend beats reacting to a single day on the scale, and connect this to the Chapter 1 point about daily bodyweight being mostly water and stored-carbohydrate noise.
  4. Name the three signal-supporting levers the chapter highlights (sleep, satiety, food environment) and explain, for one of them, how changing a default removes the need to spend willpower.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds label literacy, plate literacy, and habit literacy, not a license to diagnose, prescribe, or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the panel, the plate, and the habit science work, and treat anything clinical (a specific calorie or weight-loss prescription, a suspected deficiency, symptoms during a restrictive regimen, or a plan to replace medical treatment with food or supplements) as warranting the judgment of a qualified clinician or registered dietitian. General education explains mechanisms and reads labels; individualized diet prescriptions and diagnoses belong to a licensed professional. Work within your role: a coach educates and refers, a clinician diagnoses and treats within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
