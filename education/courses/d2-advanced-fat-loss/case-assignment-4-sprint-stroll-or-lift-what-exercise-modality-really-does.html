The Scenario

Scenario · Renata, 45
Renata has lifted for three years and sits at roughly 24 percent body fat. Six weeks ago she read that the "fat-burning zone" is where the body burns the highest percentage of fat, so she dropped her two weekly lifting sessions entirely and now does six days of slow incline-treadmill walking, reasoning that more time in that zone must add up to more fat lost. Her tracked deficit runs close to 600 kcal a day. Her joints are healthy and her recovery is good: she sleeps well and works a low-stress remote job. Six weeks in, her scale weight has dropped, but she says she "feels softer" than before, with less visible shape. When she tried one lifting session last week for the first time in over a month, she was sore for two days afterward, which worried her. She asks: "Should I keep maximizing my fat-burning zone time, or was dropping the lifting a mistake?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, why more time spent in the low-intensity "fat-burning zone" does not by itself guarantee more fat lost over the week.
  2. Account for why her deficit and her decision to drop resistance training might explain the "softer" feeling, even though the scale is moving.
  3. Decide whether reintroducing HIIT, adding back steady state, or something else is the better fit for her specifically, using her recovery capacity and joint health, not a claim that one modality burns more fat.
  4. Address her post-lifting soreness and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains that a higher fat percentage burned during low intensity does not decide weekly fat balance, which depends on total energy deficit over days.
  • Identifies that her deficit sits past the point where lean-mass gains are prevented, and that dropping resistance training risks the tissue that sets her metabolic floor.
  • Applies the HIIT-versus-steady-state equivalence finding correctly: reintroducing either is roughly a toss-up on fat-loss grounds, decided by her recovery and joints rather than by which one supposedly burns more.
  • Offers a practical, mechanism-grounded redesign suggestion within general-education scope.
  • Treats her post-lifting soreness as an expected adaptation response, not a red flag, and keeps boundary language proportionate to what is actually described.
