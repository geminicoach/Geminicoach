Muscle Architecture and the Line of Pull

Why a fan, a feather, and a strap all "contract," and why that single word tells you almost nothing about what any of them actually do.

29 min read 9 cited sources

Put two muscles side by side and ask them to do the same job. The sartorius runs as a single long strap diagonally across the thigh, its fibers stretching almost the entire length of the belly, origin to insertion, with barely a change in direction. The medial gastrocnemius, by contrast, packs hundreds of short fibers at a slant against a broad internal sheet of tendon, arranged the way barbs line up along the shaft of a feather. Both are, in the loose language every beginner learns first, "contractile." Both shorten when their motor units fire. Yet the gastrocnemius can generate several times the force of the sartorius, while the sartorius can shorten through a range of motion the gastrocnemius could never approach. Neither is a better muscle. They are two different answers to two different mechanical questions, built from the same raw material and arranged in almost opposite ways.

In the previous class you learned to treat bones as levers and joints as pivots: passive hardware waiting for something to drive it. This chapter opens up the engine that drives that hardware. And the first real surprise, the one that will reorganize how you look at every muscle diagram you have ever seen, is that the engine's shape, not merely its size, decides what kind of work it is built to do. Two muscles can weigh the same, sit in the same limb, and cross the same kind of joint, and still be suited to entirely different tasks because of how their fibers are arranged inside them.

A learner named Ozzie ran into this problem in the plainest way possible. Ozzie had trained for years and kept careful notes: sets, loads, how a lift felt on a given day. One week Ozzie noticed something that did not fit the mental model built up from years of trial and error. A standing calf raise felt brutally hard to move past a certain point, almost immovable, while a slow hip flexion drill felt like it could keep going forever without much force at all, sluggish rather than heavy. Ozzie had always assumed a muscle was a muscle: contract it harder, and it moves the joint faster and with more force, full stop. That assumption could not explain what the calf and the hip were doing. It could not, because it was missing the two ideas this chapter is built around: the internal architecture of a muscle, and the line along which it pulls. Once you have both, Ozzie's calf and hip stop being a mystery and become two clean, predictable cases of the same underlying rules.

Opening the muscle: fascicles, pennation, and physiological cross-sectional area

Strip a muscle down past its outer sheath and you find bundles of fibers, called fascicles, arranged in a characteristic pattern relative to the tendon that will ultimately carry their force to bone. That pattern, the layout of fascicles relative to the tendon and to each other, is what we mean by muscle architecture. Lieber and Fridén (2000) argue in their landmark review that architecture is the single most important determinant of a muscle's function, more predictive of how a muscle actually behaves under load than its fiber type, its overall mass, or the name a textbook gives it. That is a strong claim, and it holds up: two muscles with identical mass and identical fiber-type mix can still behave completely differently if their fascicles are arranged differently inside that mass. Architecture is the variable that does the real work.

Three numbers capture most of what matters, and once you can read them, you can predict a great deal about a muscle before you ever watch it move. Fascicle length, sometimes called fiber length, is the distance the contractile machinery runs before it hands its pull off to tendon. Pennation angle is the angle at which fascicles attach to the tendon, zero degrees in a strictly parallel muscle and as much as twenty to thirty degrees in a heavily feathered one. And physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA) is the total cross-sectional area of all the fibers in a muscle, measured perpendicular to their own long axis, not perpendicular to the whole muscle belly. That last distinction is the whole game, and it is easy to miss on a first read. A muscle's anatomical cross section, the slice you would see if you cut straight across the belly, is not the same thing as its physiological cross-sectional area, because in a pennate muscle the fibers run at an angle to that slice. Physiological cross-sectional area cuts perpendicular to the fibers themselves, and force is produced by sarcomeres pulling in parallel, so physiological cross-sectional area is simply a proxy for how many of those parallel force generators a muscle can fit inside a given volume.

The canonical estimate ties the three numbers together for a fixed muscle volume, and it is worth sitting with even if you never do the arithmetic by hand:

PCSA is approximately equal to (muscle volume multiplied by the cosine of the pennation angle) divided by fascicle length

Read that relationship slowly, because it is doing more work than its brevity suggests. For a fixed lump of tissue, the shorter the fascicles, the more of them a muscle can pack across its width, and the larger the physiological cross-sectional area becomes. Pennation is what lets a muscle do that packing without ballooning outward: angling the fibers lets them attach along a broad internal sheet of tendon called an aponeurosis, occupying space efficiently the way shingles overlap on a roof rather than lying end to end. This is why the strongest muscles in the human body, ranked by physiological cross-sectional area, are not the ones with the longest bellies. In Ward and colleagues' (2009) high-fidelity dissection of twenty-one human lower limbs, the soleus, gluteus medius, and vastus lateralis topped the physiological cross-sectional area rankings, and all three are short-fibered and pennate. The sartorius, gracilis, and semitendinosus, the long straps of the thigh, had the greatest excursion capacity in that same dataset and comparatively small physiological cross-sectional area. Same limb, almost opposite architecture.

Think About It

Two muscles have identical mass. Muscle A has four-centimeter fascicles set at twenty degrees of pennation. Muscle B has twelve-centimeter fascicles running parallel with no pennation at all. Before reading on, decide which one makes more force and which one shortens through a greater range. Then ask yourself which architectural number you actually used to make that call. If you reached for fascicle length and pennation angle rather than total mass, you have already grasped the core idea of this chapter.

The trade-off, stated cleanly: parallel versus series

The classic way to remember all of this comes from Wickiewicz, Roy, Powell, and Edgerton's (1983) cadaveric study of the human lower limb, which sorted muscles by their apparent design priorities: force versus speed and range. Their formulation still holds up more than four decades later, and it is worth memorizing outright: tension is a function of sarcomeres arranged in parallel; velocity and range of shortening are functions of sarcomeres arranged in series. Everything else in this chapter is commentary on that single sentence.

Here is why the sentence is true, at the level of the individual building block. Line sarcomeres up end to end along a long fiber, and each one's small shortening adds to the next one's, so the whole fiber shortens far and shortens quickly. But at any single cross-section through that fiber, only one sarcomere's worth of force is being transmitted, because the sarcomeres are in series, like links in a chain rather than strands in a rope. Now stack sarcomeres side by side instead, and their individual forces sum, because each one is pulling in parallel on the same shared point of attachment. The fiber as a whole, though, shortens only as much as a single sarcomere can shorten, since none of them are adding their excursion to a neighbor's. Every muscle in your body represents a particular budget split between these two arrangements, spent within a fixed volume of tissue. You cannot buy maximum force and maximum range from the same pool of sarcomeres. Something always gives.

Zajac (1989) formalized this budget in a model that still underlies most biomechanical simulation software used today, the so-called musculotendon actuator. His central insight was that a muscle's force-length behavior and its force-velocity behavior can be scaled almost entirely from two architectural numbers once you know them: physiological cross-sectional area and fascicle length. That is a remarkable simplification. It means you do not need to know a muscle's exact fiber-type mix or its precise metabolic profile to predict, in broad strokes, whether it will behave like a powerful, short-range engine or a fast, long-range one. You mostly need a ruler and a protractor. This is not an abstraction you will only meet in a textbook chart. It is the reason a calf muscle can hold your entire body weight balanced on the ball of one foot while moving the ankle through only a small arc, and it is the reason a hamstring can whip the shin through a wide, fast swing while yielding relatively easily under a heavy, slow load. When you watch someone lift, you are watching architecture spend its budget in real time, joint by joint.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common assumption holds that a bigger muscle, meaning a muscle with more total mass or a larger visible belly, is automatically a stronger one. Mass alone does not tell you how that tissue is arranged. A large, long-fibered muscle can have a smaller physiological cross-sectional area, and therefore a lower force ceiling, than a smaller, densely pennate one packed with short fascicles. Size is a starting clue, never the whole answer. Architecture decides what a given volume of muscle is actually for.

Pennation is a mechanical advantage, not a penalty

Look back at the physiological cross-sectional area equation and notice the cosine of the pennation angle sitting inside it. Because a pennate fiber pulls at an angle to the tendon rather than straight along it, only the component of that fiber's force running along the tendon's own line is actually transmitted onward to bone: tendon force equals fiber force multiplied by the cosine of the pennation angle. Taken in isolation, that looks like a tax on pennation. Angle your fibers, and you throw away a slice of their raw force to geometry. For decades the standard teaching stopped right there, treating pennation as a necessary evil, tolerated only for the sake of packing more tissue into a tight space.

Recent modeling work has overturned that framing almost completely. Using physiologically informed geometric muscle models, Rockenfeller, Günther, Clemente, and Dick (2024), publishing in Royal Society Open Science, showed that a pennate arrangement can allow a muscle to produce up to six times more isometric force than a non-pennate muscle occupying the exact same volume. The authors coined the term pennation mechanical advantage to resolve an old ambiguity in how physiological cross-sectional area had been interpreted for decades. The small cosine loss at each individual fiber is dwarfed by the enormous gain in the sheer number of fibers a muscle can install in parallel once they are allowed to sit at an angle rather than running straight through. Pennation is not simply the price a muscle pays for efficient packing. Pennation, on this newer accounting, is the packing, and it is a decisive net win for force production in any muscle where range of motion is not the top priority.

There is a subtlety worth carrying forward here, because it explains a persistent messiness you will meet in the literature. Pennation angle is not a fixed property stamped onto a muscle for life. Because fibers physically rotate to steeper angles as they shorten during a contraction, the pennation angle you would measure changes continuously throughout the movement, larger near full shortening than at a resting length. This is part of why Ward and colleagues (2009) opened their own study by asking a genuinely uncomfortable question: are current measurements of lower extremity muscle architecture even accurate? Much of the published data in circulation comes from formalin-fixed cadavers measured at a single, often unspecified, joint angle, frozen at whatever length the donor happened to be in. When you encounter a physiological cross-sectional area figure in a table or a diagram, it is worth asking at what muscle length it was measured, because the same muscle can report meaningfully different numbers depending on the answer.

Ozzie's calf raise is the pennation story in miniature. The gastrocnemius and soleus are both short-fibered, heavily pennate muscles built for exactly the kind of task a standing calf raise demands: moving a joint through a modest arc against very high resistance, in this case nearly the whole body's weight balanced on the forefoot. The architecture is not an accident of anatomy. It is a design suited to the job, purchased at the cost of range, which is precisely why the exercise feels heavy and short rather than long and light.

Think About It

If pennation angle changes as a muscle shortens, what does that imply about a single number you might see quoted for a muscle's physiological cross-sectional area or its force capacity? Is that number a fixed property of the muscle, or a snapshot taken at one particular length? What would you want to know before trusting a comparison between two muscles measured under different conditions?

Fiber type: shading, not switching

Architecture sets a muscle's mechanical envelope: the outer boundary of what it can possibly do given its shape. Fiber-type composition shades where inside that envelope the muscle prefers to work. We will stay strictly on the applied side of this topic here, since the underlying biochemistry of energy systems belongs to a different part of this course. What matters for reading a lift is that individual fibers differ in which myosin heavy-chain isoform they express, and that isoform sets both contraction speed and fatigue resistance. Schiaffino and Reggiani (2011), in their comprehensive review of the topic, describe the continuum found in human skeletal muscle: type I fibers, which are slow and highly fatigue-resistant; type IIa fibers, which are fast and moderately fatigue-resistant; and type IIx fibers, the fastest and most powerful of the three, and also the quickest to fatigue.

Two practical consequences follow from this, and both are worth carrying into how you read a training session. First, a muscle biased toward type I fibers, the postural soleus being a classic example, will feel almost tireless under sustained low load but will not produce force quickly or explosively no matter how hard it is asked to. A muscle rich in type II fibers can generate high force rapidly, but it pays for that capability with faster fatigue. Second, and more useful when you are actually standing at the whiteboard trying to explain a lift, is the size principle of motor-unit recruitment. Within any single muscle, the nervous system recruits smaller, slower motor units first, and calls in the larger, faster units only as the demand for force climbs higher. A single muscle therefore serves a whole range of demands, from a delicate, sustained hold to an all-out maximal effort, simply by choosing which of its own motor units to switch on at a given moment. Fiber type, in other words, is not a fixed label stamped onto a whole muscle so much as it is a distribution the nervous system reads from selectively, according to what the moment actually requires.

Ozzie's hip flexion drill fits this same picture from the opposite direction. A long, relatively parallel-fibered muscle working through a slow, controlled range at modest resistance recruits gradually, calling on fewer of its higher-threshold units, and the felt sensation is closer to a controlled burn than a hard stop. Neither sensation, the calf's hard ceiling or the hip's slow burn, tells you anything is wrong. Both are ordinary architecture and ordinary recruitment doing exactly what they are built to do.

Think About It

A learner reports that a muscle "gives out" or feels sharply different from the surrounding tissue during a specific movement, a sudden change rather than the gradual fatigue you would expect from ordinary recruitment. Fiber type and architecture help you reason about normal function, but a sudden, localized change in how a muscle performs or feels is one of the signs that warrant professional evaluation. It is not something to diagnose from a chapter, and it is not something a learner should try to work through alone.

The line of pull: a muscle is a vector

Now we reach the pivot of the whole chapter. Everything so far has been about how much force a muscle can make. The equally important question, and the one that actually determines what a muscle does at a joint, is in which direction it delivers that force to the bone. A muscle does not simply "shorten" in some generic sense. It tugs its insertion toward its origin along one specific line in space, set by the geometry of where it attaches at each end. Model that tug as a line of pull, a vector running from origin to insertion, and you can immediately ask the single question that governs everything about how a joint moves: what is the angle between that line of pull and the bone it is acting on?

That angle matters because a single pulling force splits geometrically into two separate components the instant it meets a bone that is not aligned with it. The part of the force running perpendicular to the bone is the rotary component. It swings the bone about the joint's axis, and it is the only part of the force that actually produces movement. The part of the force running parallel to the bone is the stabilizing component, sometimes called the compressive component. Directed along the shaft, it either presses the joint surfaces together or pulls them apart, but on its own it produces no rotation at all. When a muscle's line of pull happens to be exactly perpendicular to the bone, every unit of force that muscle generates becomes rotation, with nothing wasted. When the line of pull instead runs nearly along the length of the bone, almost all of that force is spent squeezing or distracting the joint, and only a small fraction is left over to actually turn it.

The deltoid is the clearest possible case, because its line of pull changes character dramatically as the arm elevates through its range. With the arm hanging down at the side, the deltoid's fibers run almost parallel to the humerus, so its line of pull is steeply aligned with the bone rather than crossing it. A large fraction of the deltoid's force at that position is compressive, pressing the head of the humerus firmly into its socket, and only a small rotary component is left over to actually initiate abduction. This is precisely why the first few degrees of raising the arm from the side feel mechanically awkward, almost stuck, and why other muscles typically help initiate that early motion. As the arm continues to rise toward horizontal, the angle between the deltoid's line of pull and the humerus opens up considerably, the rotary component grows, and the same muscle becomes a far more effective elevator of the arm. Same muscle, same nervous-system signal, same "contraction" in the loose sense Ozzie started this chapter with, and yet a completely different mechanical contribution depending on where in the range the joint happens to be.

Ozzie noticed a version of this while working through overhead pressing drills. A partial press starting from shoulder height felt smooth and controllable, while the same muscle group struggled noticeably at the very bottom of a dead-stop press from the sides. That difference is not a strength deficit that needs correcting with more sets. It is the deltoid's line of pull doing exactly what geometry predicts it will do at each joint angle: mostly compressive near the bottom of the range, increasingly rotary as the arm rises. Reading a sticking point this way, as a question about line of pull and joint angle rather than a question about raw effort, is one of the most useful habits this course is trying to build.

Think About It

Pick any pressing or pulling exercise you know well. At the joint angle where it feels hardest, is the prime mover's line of pull likely running closer to parallel with the bone, or closer to perpendicular? Sketch the two positions before you answer. The sticking point is rarely a mystery once the line of pull is drawn.

Muscles that cross two joints

The line-of-pull picture gets richer, and considerably stranger, once a muscle spans more than one joint at once. The rectus femoris crosses both the hip and the knee. The hamstrings cross both the hip and the knee. The gastrocnemius crosses both the knee and the ankle. These biarticular muscles have only a single line of pull, but two separate sets of consequences, one at each joint, and those two sets of consequences are welded together permanently because the same shortening fiber acts on both joints simultaneously. You cannot ask a biarticular muscle to affect one joint without also asking something of the other.

Zajac and Gordon (1989) made the counterintuitive core of this idea explicit in their classic review. A biarticular muscle's net action at each joint depends on the ratio of its moment arms at the two joints it crosses, and, more startling still, a muscle can actually accelerate a joint that it does not even cross, simply through the dynamic coupling of adjoining limb segments. The action you would naively predict by looking at a diagram of a muscle's origin and insertion can, in practice, reverse depending on the posture of the whole limb and on what the neighboring muscles happen to be doing at the same moment. Anatomy alone, in other words, is not a complete guide to action once more than one joint is involved.

A more tractable, everyday consequence follows from the same coupling. A two-joint muscle can drive motion powerfully at one joint while the other joint is held fixed, and it can lose much of that authority the instant the fixed joint is allowed to move. Hold the knee straight, and the gastrocnemius becomes a powerful plantarflexor of the ankle. Let the knee bend, and the gastrocnemius slackens across its own length, losing a substantial share of its ankle authority. This is why a seated calf raise loads the ankle so differently from a standing one, even though both exercises appear, on the surface, to ask the same muscle for the same job. Schumacher, Sharbafi, Seyfarth, and Rode (2020), reviewing this topic in the Journal of the Royal Society Interface, reframe what looks like an unwelcome complication as a genuine design feature instead. Because the ratio of a biarticular muscle's moment arms fixes the ratio of torques it produces at its two joints, the muscle embodies a built-in coordination pattern between them, transferring mechanical energy between joints and simplifying neural control, which is exactly why engineers keep rediscovering biarticular linkages when designing walking and jumping robots.

Ozzie's own notes, once reread with this idea in hand, made sense in a new way. A seated calf exercise had always felt notably weaker than a standing one at what seemed like the same effort level, and Ozzie had assumed this meant something was wrong with the seated version, or with the exercise selection. Nothing was wrong. The gastrocnemius simply loses mechanical advantage at the ankle when the knee is bent, because bending the knee shortens the muscle's excursion across the knee joint and changes how much length and tension it has left to spend across the ankle. The soleus, which crosses only the ankle and not the knee, is largely unaffected by knee position and takes over a greater share of the work in the seated version. Two exercises that look nearly identical from the outside can be asking two different muscles to do two different amounts of work, purely because one of them is biarticular and the other is not.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common belief holds that a stretch felt in a muscle during one movement must transfer directly to how that muscle performs in a completely different movement, since it is "the same muscle" in both cases. For a biarticular muscle this reasoning breaks down quickly. The same muscle can be lengthened at one joint while shortened at the other, and its net excursion, tension, and mechanical advantage depend on the position of both joints together, not on either one alone. A muscle's behavior at one joint cannot be read off in isolation whenever that muscle happens to cross two.

The bridge to the next chapter

Notice what the line of pull has quietly delivered over the course of this chapter, almost as a byproduct of the discussion. The perpendicular distance from a joint's axis of rotation to the muscle's line of pull is called the muscle's moment arm, and the rotary component you have been decomposing this whole chapter is nothing more than force multiplied by that geometric distance. When the deltoid's line of pull swings away from the humerus as the arm elevates, what is actually changing, underneath the surface description, is its moment arm. Chen and Franklin (2025), in a systematic review that assembled three hundred separate moment-arm datasets spanning the hip, knee, and ankle, confirm the general rule that governs all of this: moment arm varies continuously and often nonlinearly with joint angle, and even its sign can flip, turning what was a flexor into an extensor as a joint passes through certain parts of its range.

That is the seam this whole chapter has been stitching toward from the very first paragraph. Architecture tells you how much force a muscle can make. The line of pull tells you the direction in which it delivers that force. And the moment arm is the geometric lever that finally converts the two into torque, the actual turning effect felt at the joint. In the next chapter, line of pull and moment arm are multiplied together explicitly for the first time. Everything you have just learned to trace by eye becomes a single term inside an equation, one you can use to predict, joint angle by joint angle, exactly where a given lift will feel easy and where it will feel hard.

Meeting the torque equation only after tracing architecture and line of pull by hand changes what the equation means. It stops being a fact to memorize and becomes a conclusion you already saw coming. Ozzie's calf raise and overhead press are not exceptions to a rule learned by rote. They are the rule, worked out in advance, through fascicle length, pennation, and the angle a line of pull makes with a bone.

Where this understanding stops

Before moving on, it is worth being explicit about what this chapter equips a learner to do, and what it does not. Understanding muscle architecture and line of pull lets you reason about why a given exercise feels the way it feels, why two versions of a similar movement load different tissue, and why a sticking point at one joint angle is not automatically a sign of weakness. That is genuinely useful knowledge, and it changes how carefully a learner can observe their own training or the training of someone they are coaching. It does not make a learner qualified to explain away pain, a sudden loss of function, or a persistent change in how a joint or muscle behaves.

The distinction matters because the same biomechanical vocabulary that explains a sticking point can also be misused to talk someone out of getting a real problem checked. A learner who understands why the deltoid struggles near the bottom of a press has a genuine, useful mental model. That same learner is not equipped to determine whether a sharp pain in the same shoulder during the same movement reflects ordinary mechanics or something that needs a clinician's attention. Mechanism and diagnosis are two different disciplines, and this course teaches only the first one.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

This is exactly the boundary Ozzie eventually needed. A few weeks after working through this chapter, Ozzie noticed a genuinely new sensation during the same overhead press that had been merely awkward before: a sharp, localized catch near the top of the range that had not been present previously and did not match the ordinary, predictable stiffness of a rotary-component sticking point. The architecture and line-of-pull framework from this chapter was still useful here, but only in a limited way. It helped Ozzie describe the sensation precisely, note exactly where in the range it occurred, and recognize that it did not fit the pattern of an ordinary mechanical sticking point. What it could not do, and was never meant to do, was tell Ozzie whether that catch was benign or worth a professional look. Ozzie brought the description to a qualified clinician rather than guessing. That is the correct use of everything this chapter teaches: sharper observation, paired with a clear sense of where observation has to hand off to expertise.

Key Takeaways

  • Muscle architecture, meaning fascicle length, pennation angle, and physiological cross-sectional area, predicts a muscle's function better than its size or its name (Lieber and Fridén, 2000).
  • For a fixed tissue volume, force scales with sarcomeres arranged in parallel, captured by physiological cross-sectional area, while range and velocity scale with sarcomeres arranged in series, captured by fascicle length. No muscle can maximize both at once.
  • Pennation is a mechanical advantage rather than a compromise: the small cosine loss per fiber is far outweighed by the extra fibers a muscle can pack in parallel, producing up to a sixfold force gain in some geometries (Rockenfeller et al., 2024).
  • Fiber type shades a muscle toward endurance or rapid high force, and the size principle lets one muscle serve a wide range of demands by recruiting its motor units in order (Schiaffino and Reggiani, 2011).
  • A muscle is best modeled as a vector: its line of pull splits into a rotary component that produces motion and a stabilizing component that compresses the joint, and that split changes continuously with joint angle.
  • Biarticular muscles couple two joints through the ratio of their moment arms and can even influence joints they do not cross (Zajac and Gordon, 1989).
  • This framework explains mechanism, not medical status. Pain or a sudden change in how a muscle performs is a sign that warrants professional evaluation, never a puzzle to solve from a chapter alone.
Looking Ahead

You can now trace a line of pull by eye and judge whether a given architecture favors force or range. The next chapter gives that geometry a precise number. We define the moment arm formally, watch it change continuously through a joint's range, and assemble the torque equation, force multiplied by lever arm, that lets you predict, joint angle by joint angle, exactly where a lift will feel hard and where it will feel easy.
