Chapter 6 : The Hinge and the Deadlift, Load, Leverage, and the Spine

One long lever at the hip, a spine that is a stack, and a bar path that decides most of the demand.

The Core Mechanics

Moment armThe horizontal distance from a joint's axis to the bar's vertical line of gravity. External torque = bar load × moment arm.
Hinge vs. squatA hip hinge is hip-dominant rotation with little knee travel, so the hip's moment arm grows large. A squat shares work with the knee instead.
Sine relationshipThe horizontal offset of the trunk scales with the sine of torso inclination from vertical, so small angle changes near horizontal move torque a great deal (Swinton et al., 2011).
Torque climbs with loadPeak lumbar net moment rose from roughly 245 to 447 newton meters across 10 percent to 80 percent of one repetition maximum, abbreviated 1RM, in Swinton and colleagues' data.

Keeping the Load Close

  • Bar-to-body distance is the single largest lever a lifter directly controls, because it shortens every moment arm at once.
  • A few centimeters of drift can more than double the moment at a lumbar segment; the geometry did the damage, not the plates.
  • A hex bar centers the load in line with the lifter, cutting hip, spine, and ankle moments while raising demand at the knee (Swinton et al., 2011).

The Spine as a Stack

  • The spine is a column of segments, not one rigid beam; load must be described as both compression along the column and shear across it (McGill, 2015).
  • A posture that lowers one can raise the other; there is no single universally safest position (von Arx et al., 2021).
  • Heavier loads push even careful lifters toward more lumbar flexion and a posterior pelvic tilt (Shoji et al., 2025).
  • Current evidence treats spinal injury risk as multifactorial rather than reducible to one postural rule (Cherni et al., 2026).

Bracing and Intra-Abdominal Pressure

  • Bracing does not shorten the moment arm; the bar's plumb line does not move because a lifter breathes harder.
  • A stiffened trunk changes how load is transmitted through the stack, keeping segments in a more tolerable geometry.
  • Think of it as hardening the pipe the load travels through, not shrinking the pressure entering the system.

Conventional vs. Sumo

  • Sumo: wider stance, more upright torso, shorter spinal moment arm, roughly 10 percent lower L4 to L5 moment and 8 percent lower shear than conventional (Cholewicki et al., 1991).
  • Conventional: narrower stance, sagittally dominant, heavier reliance on the hamstrings and erector spinae (Hanen et al., 2025).
  • Neither style is universally better; anthropometry (femur length, torso length, hip structure) shifts which stance shortens the lever for a given lifter.
Where education ends   This builds mechanical understanding of how load moves through the hip and the spine, not a license to diagnose or treat. Pain during hinging, an uncontrollable loss of spinal position, or symptoms that track with a specific load are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
