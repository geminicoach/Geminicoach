Chapter 7: Pressing and the Shoulder

Work through the trade the shoulder makes for its range of motion (ROM), and how that trade plays out inside a loaded press.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 30 min

Answer in complete sentences. One-word answers will not earn credit here; explain the mechanism each time. There is a one-page cheat-sheet for this chapter if you want to check your reference, but try it from memory first.

Part A · The Stability-For-Mobility Trade

The glenohumeral joint gets the largest range of motion (ROM) of any joint in the body by giving something up. Explain what, and why.

  1. Describe the mismatch between the humeral head and the glenoid fossa. In a full sentence, explain how that mismatch is what gives the shoulder such an exceptional range of motion (ROM).
  2. Compare the glenohumeral joint to the hip joint. What does the hip's deep socket buy it that the shoulder's shallow socket does not, and what does the shoulder get in exchange?
  3. If bone and ligament are not doing most of the stabilizing work at the shoulder through the bulk of a press, what two systems are doing it instead, and what is each one's job?

Part B · Scapulohumeral Rhythm

The socket has to travel with the arm. Use the chapter's account of scapulohumeral rhythm to answer these.

  1. Explain, mechanically, why the scapula must upwardly rotate as the arm goes overhead. What would happen to the joint if the scapula stayed still while the arm kept rising?
  2. The classic ratio between glenohumeral and scapulothoracic motion is not fixed. Explain how that ratio changes across the arc of motion, and how adding external load changes it further.
  3. Scapular motion during elevation is three-dimensional. Name the three motions that happen together, and explain in one sentence what "scapular dyskinesis" refers to.

Part C · The Moment Arm Story

A press is a moving target for leverage. Trace how the moment arm on the shoulder changes through the movement.

  1. Define the external moment arm in the context of an overhead press, in your own words, and explain what multiplying it by the load tells you.
  2. Explain why the sticking point is a mechanics problem rather than a strength problem. Refer to what the electromyography (EMG) evidence actually showed happening in the pressing muscles during the stall.
  3. Explain how a bar path that drifts forward of the shoulder changes the external moment arm, and why that makes the bar feel heavier at that exact point, even though the load has not changed.
  4. Trace the deltoid's own internal moment arm from the bottom of a press to shoulder height. Why does the same muscle contribute mostly compression at the bottom and mostly rotation higher up?

Part D · The Rotator Cuff Force Couple

Keeping the ball centered on the socket is a full-time job, shared between two systems working as a pair.

  1. Name the four rotator cuff muscles, and describe their shared job in a single sentence.
  2. Explain concavity compression: how does the rotator cuff keep the humeral head centered against a deltoid pull that is, early in the range, mostly directed straight up into the socket?
  3. Explain why the term "force couple" is used for the deltoid and the rotator cuff together, and why the cuff's centering job does not disappear once the deltoid's leverage becomes favorable near shoulder height.

Part E · The Overhead Question

Whether, and how, a given learner should press overhead is a mechanical question with an individual answer. Reason it through.

  1. List the things a full strict overhead press asks of the system all at once (range of motion (ROM), scapular control, and rotator cuff capacity), and explain why a restriction in any one of them can change how the bar has to travel.
  2. Explain how grip width trades leverage between the elbow and the shoulder, and why a wider grip that allows more total load also asks more of the shoulder's soft-tissue restraints.
  3. If a learner has genuinely restricted overhead range of motion (ROM), name two pressing alternatives that ask less of that specific range, and explain why they can still build capacity toward a full overhead press over time.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds mechanical literacy about the shoulder, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, and treat anything that looks clinical (a catching or pinching sensation, a sense of instability or "give," or pain that persists across sessions) as a sign that warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
