Part A · The Three Moment Arms

The squat is a coordinated rotation at hip, knee, and ankle, governed by the bar's line of gravity. Answer in full sentences.

  1. Define moment arm in your own words, and explain why the bar's weight alone does not tell you how hard a joint is working.
  2. Explain why torque at the hip, knee, and ankle all tend to peak near the bottom of a squat rather than at the top.
  3. A learner's hip moment arm grows roughly four-and-a-half-fold from the top of a squat to the bottom, while the bar's weight never changes. Explain, in mechanical terms, why the lift still gets dramatically harder even though the load itself is constant.

Part C · Why Segment Lengths Change the Picture

  1. Explain, mechanically, why a learner with a long femur relative to their torso tends to squat with more forward trunk lean than a learner with a shorter femur and a longer torso, even when both are using identical technique instructions.
  2. Limited ankle dorsiflexion caps how far the knee can travel forward. Explain, step by step, how that limitation eventually shows up as increased forward lean and increased hip torque, using the mid-foot constraint to connect the steps.
  3. Kim and colleagues found that relative muscular strength and joint flexibility predicted torque distribution more strongly than the ratio of leg segment lengths alone. Explain what this means for anyone tempted to predict a learner's ideal squat from anthropometry alone.

Part D · Forward Lean: Mechanics, Not Error

  1. Explain why forward lean in a squat is, in most cases, a calculable consequence of bar height and anthropometry rather than a technical fault. Use the mid-foot principle in your answer.
  2. A learner is coached to "keep your chest up" regardless of their proportions. Explain what can go wrong with the bar path when a learner with a long femur or limited ankle mobility follows that cue too strictly.
  3. State the bar-over-mid-foot principle in your own words, and explain why it is a more reliable thing to coach than torso angle.

Part E · Dismantling One-Size-Fits-All Cueing

Use the Fry, Smith, and Schilling (2003) knee-barrier study and the chapter's broader argument to reason through the following.

  1. Summarize what happened to knee torque and hip torque when forward knee travel was physically blocked. What does this result tell you about cues that focus on a single joint in isolation?
  2. Explain why "correct squat form" is better described as a family of mechanically valid solutions rather than a single template copied from one body to another. Give at least two anthropometric or mobility factors that make this true.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds mechanical literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the load moves through the joints, and treat anything that looks clinical (new or worsening pain, joint instability, numbness, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach explains mechanics and refers out, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
