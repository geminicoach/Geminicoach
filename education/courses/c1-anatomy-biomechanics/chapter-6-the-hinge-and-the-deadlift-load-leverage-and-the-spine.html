The Hinge and the Deadlift: Load, Leverage, and the Spine

Why the deadlift is a fight against a very long external moment arm at the hips and spine, and why position, not effort alone, decides who wins it.

26 min read 9 cited sources

Picture a lifter standing over a loaded barbell. Nothing has moved yet. The plates rest on the floor, the hands are wrapped around the knurling, and the whole system is perfectly still. And yet, before a single millimeter of vertical travel, the hardest mechanical decision of the entire lift has already been made: how far in front of the hips does the bar sit, and how far has the torso tipped to reach it? Those two facts, settled in the setup and invisible to a casual observer, will determine more about the demand on the hips and the spine than the number of kilograms loaded onto the bar.

In the last chapter, the squat was a negotiation between two joints, the knee and the hip, trading torque back and forth as the load descended. The hinge is different. It is the hip's solo performance, and the deadlift is that solo performed under maximal load. To read it correctly you need only one instrument, the moment arm engine you have been building since Chapter 4, but you must point it at a structure this course has so far treated as a single rigid beam: the trunk. In this chapter the spine stops being a lever and becomes what it actually is, a stack of segments held in relationship, and the horizontal distance from that stack to the barbell becomes the single most important number in the room.

Throughout this chapter you will follow one learner, Marlon, a 37 year old who took up barbell training eight months ago after a long run of desk work left him wanting something that felt like real physical progress. Marlon can conventional deadlift a respectable load, and he is proud of it, but he has a habit his training partner keeps pointing out on video: the bar drifts a few centimeters away from his shins every time he sets up, and by the time it clears his knees it is riding well out in front of his hips. Marlon is not a patient in this course and he is not a case to be diagnosed. He is a stand in for a mechanical habit that is extremely common, extremely fixable with the right framework, and, as you will see, entirely explainable by the same moment arm logic this course has used since Chapter 4. His story is a thread, not a prescription, and we will keep that distinction clear the whole way through.

The hinge: rotation dominated by the hip

Strip a movement pattern down to its rotation and you can classify it honestly. A hip hinge is a pattern in which the bulk of the sagittal plane motion happens at the hip joint, with comparatively little at the knee and, ideally, very little at the spine. The femur stays relatively vertical or travels only modestly forward. The pelvis rotates over the femoral heads like a seesaw over its fulcrum. The torso inclines toward the floor. Contrast this with the squat, where the knee flexes deeply, the shank inclines forward over the foot, and the torso stays comparatively upright. Same primary engine, hip extension, but a completely different distribution of joint work.

This distinction matters because it tells you where the torque will accumulate. In a hinge, because the knee contributes little and the torso travels through a large angular range, the horizontal displacement of the load away from the hip becomes large. The hip extensors, the gluteal muscles and the hamstrings acting across the hip, must generate the extension torque needed to reverse that displacement, while the muscles that resist trunk flexion must hold the stack of spinal segments together against a load trying to fold it forward. The Romanian deadlift, the good morning, the kettlebell swing, and the conventional deadlift are all members of this family. They differ in load and in range, but they share a signature: a long external moment arm at the hip.

It helps to say plainly what a learner is watching for when they try to tell a hinge from a squat on video, because the two patterns are easy to blur in the middle of a heavy set. Watch the knee first. If the knee angle barely changes across the whole range of motion while the hips travel a large arc, that is a hinge. If the knee closes down substantially and the shin tips forward to track over the toes, some of the range has been handed to the squat pattern instead, and the demand at the hip will be smaller than a pure hinge would produce for the same bar height. Neither pattern is superior in the abstract. They are simply different distributions of the same total mechanical work, and a lift can slide along a continuum between them depending on stance, limb proportions, and how the lifter chooses to set up.

The external moment arm at the hip

Recall the definition this course has used from the start: the external moment about a joint equals the external force multiplied by its moment arm, the perpendicular distance from the joint's axis to the line of action of the force. For a barbell, the force is gravity acting straight down through the bar's center of mass. The line of action is therefore a vertical plumb line dropped from the bar. The moment arm at any joint is simply the horizontal distance from that joint to the plumb line.

Now watch what happens as the torso inclines. In the top position of a deadlift, the hip sits almost directly under the bar. The horizontal distance is small, and so is the hip extension torque demand. As the lifter hinges toward the floor, the torso tips forward and the hips travel backward relative to the bar. The horizontal gap between hip and plumb line grows. Because torque is force multiplied by that gap, and the force is constant for a given bar weight, the extension demand at the hip climbs in close to linear proportion to the horizontal distance. This is one reason a hinge often feels hardest not at the very bottom of a symmetric range, but at the position of greatest torso inclination combined with the load still resting on the floor.

The relationship is unforgiving because it is trigonometric rather than linear with respect to angle. The horizontal offset of the trunk's mass, and of a bar held in front of it, scales roughly with the sine of the torso's inclination away from vertical. Tilt the torso from 30 degrees to 60 degrees from vertical and the effective moment arm more than doubles, even though the torso has swept through the same 30 degree span it did on the way from vertical to 30 degrees. Small changes in torso angle near the horizontal produce outsized changes in torque. That single fact is the reason a lifter who lets the chest drop even slightly at the start of a heavy pull can feel the weight suddenly become far harder to move than the load on the bar would seem to justify.

Swinton and colleagues (2011) put numbers on the load side of this equation. Nineteen powerlifters pulling conventional deadlifts across a range from ten percent to eighty percent of one repetition maximum, abbreviated 1RM, saw peak lumbar net moment climb from roughly 245 newton meters to roughly 447 newton meters, a direct, monotonic load to moment relationship. That study varied the weight on the bar. The interactive widget below varies the geometry of the torso instead. Both levers, load and angle, feed the same product, and a lifter who only thinks about the first one is ignoring half of the equation.

A worked example makes the trigonometry concrete. Suppose a lumbar segment sits roughly 0.35 meters above the hip along the trunk. At a torso inclination of thirty degrees from vertical, the horizontal offset of that segment from the hip is 0.35 multiplied by the sine of thirty degrees, which works out to roughly 0.18 meters. Tip the same trunk to sixty degrees from vertical and the offset becomes 0.35 multiplied by the sine of sixty degrees, or roughly 0.30 meters. The torso rotated through the same thirty degree span both times, yet the horizontal offset grew by a much larger factor on the second leg of the movement than on the first. That is the entire reason a lift can feel proportionally harder in its most horizontal position than raw intuition about degrees of rotation would predict. The sine function, not the angle itself, is what a lifter's body is actually negotiating with.

It is worth pausing on why this matters practically for a learner like Marlon, whose training partner keeps flagging his setup on video. Marlon's 1RM has not changed in weeks, but his bar occasionally drifts forward at the start of a heavy single, and on those reps the pull feels noticeably harder even though the plates loaded are identical to the week before. Once a learner accepts that torque is a product of force and moment arm, and that the moment arm responds steeply to small angular shifts near horizontal, this stops being a mystery. The bar did not get heavier. The geometry did.

The spine is not a lever, it is a stack

Until now this course has quietly treated the trunk as a single rigid beam rotating about the hip. For the hip torque calculation, that simplification is fine. For understanding what happens inside the trunk, it is dangerously incomplete. The spine is a column of vertebrae separated by intervertebral discs and held together by ligaments, fascia, and muscle. It is a stack of segments, each of which can flex, extend, and shear relative to its neighbors. When this course speaks of the spinal moment arm, it means the horizontal distance from a particular motion segment, most often the L4 to L5 or L5 to S1 junction where loads peak, to the plumb line of the load.

Stuart McGill's body of work, synthesized in Low Back Disorders (2015), is the reference frame here. McGill treats the lumbar spine as a segmented column whose resistance to flexion comes from active muscular co-contraction and from passive connective tissue, and whose loading must be described in at least two independent currencies: compression along the column's axis, and shear perpendicular to it. These are not interchangeable. A posture that reduces one can increase the other, which is why a folk instruction like always lift with a straight back is more slogan than mechanics. The spine does not experience a single number called load. It experiences at least two, and they respond to posture in different directions.

Part of what makes this genuinely interesting is that the erector spinae, the long muscles running along either side of the spinal column, do not simply contract harder as flexion increases. McGill's research describes a well-documented pattern called the flexion relaxation phenomenon, in which the erector spinae progressively quiet down as the trunk approaches full flexion, handing more of the load-bearing task to passive tissue, the ligaments and the discs themselves, rather than to active muscle. This is not a malfunction. It is a normal feature of a healthy, well-coordinated spine. But it also means that near the bottom of a very forward-leaning hinge, some of the structures resisting the load are ligamentous rather than muscular, which is one more reason a stack that holds its geometric relationship matters more than raw muscular effort alone.

Von Arx and colleagues (2021) demonstrated exactly this trade-off. Using motion-capture-driven musculoskeletal modeling of thirty adults lifting a 15 kilogram box, they found that stoop lifting, a hinge pattern with a more flexed spine, produced lower total and compressive lumbar loads than squat lifting, but generally produced higher shear loads across the lumbar spine, with the exception of the L5 to S1 segment, where anterior shear was actually higher during squat lifting. There is no single, universally safest posture. There is a posture-dependent redistribution of load type. What a lifter can influence is whether the segments hold a stable relationship to one another while that load passes through them.

Shoji and colleagues (2025) sharpened the picture for the deadlift specifically. Tracking a six-region spinal model across thirteen lifters pulling from sixty percent to ninety percent of 1RM, they documented a load-dependent increase in lower-thoracic and upper-lumbar flexion, accompanied by a posterior tilt of the pelvis, as the weight on the bar rose. In plain terms, as load climbs, the stack tends to buckle toward flexion and the pelvis tucks underneath the lifter rather than staying anteriorly tilted. Their finding was squarely mechanical: under high-load conditions the participants deviated from the commonly recommended posture of a slight lumbar lordosis and an anteriorly tilted pelvis, with spinal flexion increasing as the demand rose. A flexing stack changes both the internal geometry of the segments and the shear they are asked to resist, which is exactly why heavy pulls are where technique tends to fall apart first.

This is also a good place to be honest about how the field's thinking on neutral spine has moved. A 2026 narrative review by Cherni and colleagues, examining deadlift-related low back injury prevention, argues that the traditional coaching cue to preserve a single rigid neutral spine at all costs deserves updating. The review integrates evidence on spinal flexion tolerance, load management, movement variability, and dynamic trunk control, and it lands on a more adaptive framework than the poster on a gym wall usually suggests: the spine is more tolerant of controlled flexion under moderate loads than a strict neutral-only rule implies, and risk appears to be multifactorial rather than reducible to a single postural target. None of that contradicts McGill's or Shoji's findings about compression and shear. It refines them. A stack that briefly and predictably flexes under a light load is a different mechanical event than a stack that buckles unexpectedly under a maximal one, and the honest lesson is that posture, load, and control interact rather than any single variable acting alone.

Trunk stability and effective load

Here is where the language a learner uses needs to stay precise. Bracing does not reduce the external moment arm. The barbell sits where it sits regardless of how hard the lifter breathes or how tightly the abdomen contracts. What a stable, braced trunk does is change how the load is transmitted through the stack. When the abdominal wall and the surrounding trunk musculature co-contract and intra-abdominal pressure rises, the segmented column behaves more like a single rigid unit rather than a loose chain of independently moving blocks. McGill's work documents how this co-contraction and the accompanying rise in intra-abdominal pressure, abbreviated IAP, stiffen the spine, distribute load more evenly across motion segments, and reduce the tendency of any single segment to shear or buckle under compression.

The practical consequence is that two lifters with identical external moment arms can subject their spinal structures to very different effective demands depending on whether the stack holds its position. A rigid, well-braced trunk keeps the segments in the geometric relationship the surrounding musculature is strongest in and the passive tissues tolerate best. A trunk that loses position under load, the chest caving forward or the lumbar spine rounding as in Shoji's high-load condition, changes that geometry mid-lift, shifting load toward tissues and orientations that resist it less well. This is the mechanical case for bracing, stated without exaggeration: it does not lighten the bar, but it governs how the bar's demand lands inside the body once the pull begins.

Notice what this means for how a learner should think about the cue to brace, or to take a big breath before the bar leaves the floor. It is not a magic trick that shrinks the load. It is closer to hardening the pipe that the load travels through, so that the pressure inside is distributed rather than concentrated at whichever segment happens to be weakest that day. A stiffer pipe under the same pressure holds its shape. A softer one bulges at its weakest point. The moment arm decides how much pressure enters the system. Trunk stiffness decides where inside the system that pressure gets absorbed.

The breathing pattern behind this is worth spelling out, because a big breath before the pull is not simply about oxygen. A lifter typically inhales into the abdomen before initiating the lift, then holds that breath against a closed airway while bracing the abdominal wall outward, a maneuver related to what is sometimes called a Valsalva maneuver. The trapped air acts like a cylinder of gas, raising intra-abdominal pressure and giving the trunk musculature something rigid to brace against from the inside, in addition to whatever tension the muscles themselves generate. Once the pull is complete or the sticking point has passed, the breath is released and a new one taken for the next repetition. None of this changes where the bar is. It changes how effectively the trunk resists being folded forward by a bar that has not moved an inch closer to the body.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common belief holds that bracing hard enough will let a lifter safely pull any load, as if a tight enough trunk makes the moment arm irrelevant. It does not. Bracing changes how load is distributed inside the spine. It cannot shorten the horizontal distance between the bar and the lumbar segments. A perfectly braced lifter with the bar drifting far out in front of the shins is still generating a large external moment, just one that is being transmitted through a more stable structure. Bracing is a defense against poor load transmission, not a substitute for good geometry.

Bar-to-body distance: the largest lever a learner controls

Of every variable in a deadlift, the one most directly under a lifter's control, and the one with the largest leverage over spinal and hip demand, is the horizontal distance between the barbell and the body. Because the moment arm at every joint is the horizontal gap to the bar's plumb line, letting the bar drift even a few centimeters forward of the shins lengthens that gap at the hip and at every lumbar segment simultaneously. A bar that rides against the shins and stays over the midfoot keeps every moment arm as short as a lifter's anatomy allows.

This is why the instruction to keep the bar close is not aesthetic preference. It is the single most efficient intervention available to a learner. Nobody can change the length of their own femur or the height of their own torso. Everybody can change whether the bar tracks along the body or swings away from it. Swinton and colleagues (2011) illustrated the same principle from the equipment side of the problem: the hexagonal barbell, which places the load's center of mass in line with the lifter rather than out in front of the shins, significantly reduced peak moments at the lumbar spine, the hip, and the ankle relative to a straight bar, while increasing the moment at the knee, because the more upright, squat-like posture the hex bar permits shifts work toward that joint instead. Lifters in that study also achieved a higher 1RM with the hex bar, 265 kilograms compared with 245 kilograms on average. Same load category, different geometry, different distribution of demand, which is exactly the logic this course built in Chapter 4 applied directly to a piece of hardware.

Put a number on the drift itself and the stakes become obvious. Take a 140 kilogram bar generating roughly 1,370 newtons of downward force. If that load sits 5 centimeters, or 0.05 meters, forward of a lumbar segment, it produces a moment of about 68.5 newton meters at that segment from the bar alone. Let the same bar drift to 12 centimeters, or 0.12 meters, forward of the same segment, and the moment climbs to roughly 164 newton meters, more than double, for a horizontal drift smaller than the width of a hand. Nothing about the plates changed. Seven centimeters of bar path is the entire difference, which is why this single variable outranks nearly every other coaching detail a learner might obsess over during setup.

This is the exact habit that shows up on Marlon's training video. His setup is fine. The problem appears in the first few centimeters of the pull, when the bar swings slightly away from his shins as he initiates the lift, then has to be muscled back in toward the body to finish the rep. Nothing about his strength changed between the reps where this happens and the reps where it does not. What changed is the horizontal distance the bar traveled away from his joints, and, by the logic of this chapter, that distance is not a small stylistic detail. It is a direct multiplier on the torque his hips and his lumbar spine were asked to produce and resist for that repetition.

Where this course's authority ends

Before going further into stance variations, it is worth naming the boundary that governs everything this chapter says about the spine, because the material gets more emotionally loaded the closer it gets to pain and injury. This course explains mechanics. It explains why a longer moment arm produces more torque, why the spine's response to load can be described as compression and shear, and why posture changes how that load is distributed. None of that is a diagnosis, and none of it is a treatment plan.

The reason this boundary matters is not procedural caution for its own sake. It follows directly from the biomechanics already covered in this chapter. Von Arx showed that stoop and squat lifting redistribute load between compression and shear rather than eliminating either one. Shoji showed that heavy loads push even well-intentioned lifters toward increased flexion. The 2026 review by Cherni and colleagues showed that the relationship between spinal posture and injury risk is genuinely multifactorial, involving load history, movement variability, and factors well outside any single mechanical snapshot. Given all of that complexity, a learner who reads one chapter of mechanics and then attempts to diagnose why a specific person's back hurts is working with a small slice of a much larger picture, and a slice that was never designed to support that kind of judgment.

Pain during hinging, a loss of spinal position that a lifter cannot voluntarily control, or discomfort that reliably tracks with a specific load or pattern are signals that warrant professional evaluation. They are not puzzles for a fellow learner to solve with a cue borrowed from this chapter. The mechanics you now understand make you a better observer and a more informed patient if you ever need to describe what you are experiencing to a clinician. They do not make you qualified to manage a symptomatic spine, your own or anyone else's.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Conventional, sumo, and the Romanian hinge: three answers to one problem

If the deadlift is fundamentally a fight against a long moment arm at the hip and spine, then the major deadlift variations are best understood as different solutions to that same problem, each trading one demand for another, and each suiting different bodies. This connects directly to the individual-variation themes of Chapter 5: there is no single correct stance, only stances that suit particular proportions.

Sumo: shortening the spinal moment arm

The sumo deadlift widens the stance dramatically and turns the feet out, letting the lifter drop the hips closer to the bar and keep the torso substantially more upright. Escamilla and colleagues (2000), in a landmark three-dimensional analysis of twenty-four lifters at a national championship, found sumo lifters maintained a trunk position five to ten degrees more vertical, used a far wider stance, seventy centimeters compared with thirty-two centimeters, and, because a more upright torso travels a shorter vertical bar path, performed roughly twenty-five to forty percent less mechanical work than conventional lifters moving a comparable load. The more upright torso is the mechanical heart of sumo. It shortens the horizontal distance from the lumbar spine to the bar.

Cholewicki and colleagues (1991) had already quantified the payoff at the spine. Measuring L4 to L5 loads in fifty-seven powerlifters at a national championship, with average compressive loads reaching a striking 17,192 newtons, they found the sumo style produced roughly a ten percent reduction in L4 to L5 net joint moment and about an eight percent reduction in shear compared with conventional pulling, directly attributable to the shorter horizontal bar-to-spine moment arm afforded by the more upright torso. Shoji and colleagues (2025) echoed the same mechanism, describing how a wider, more upright sumo setup reduces hip torque demand by shortening the moment arm through posture alone.

Conventional: sagittal dominance, longer arm

The conventional pull accepts a longer moment arm in exchange for a narrower, more sagittally oriented movement. Hanen and colleagues (2025), studying thirty experienced lifters at eighty-five percent of 1RM using motion capture, force plates, and electromyography, abbreviated EMG, found conventional deadlifts generated higher hip-extension moments and greater biceps femoris and erector spinae activation, while sumo produced greater frontal-plane and transverse-plane hip and knee moments, reflecting the side-to-side stabilization demanded by a wide stance. Their summary is worth remembering: conventional pulling is sagittally dominant and driven heavily by the posterior chain, while sumo trades some of that sagittal demand for multi-planar stabilization. Neither is easier in the abstract. They load different structures. Gundersen and colleagues (2024), using statistical parametric mapping to compare conventional, sumo, and hex-bar pulls in strength-trained women, added a further reminder that barbell geometry itself, meaning where the load's center of mass sits relative to the lifter, alters the external moment arm at both the hip and the lumbar spine. Equipment and stance are two dials on the same machine.

The Romanian hinge: same family, different range

The Romanian deadlift, commonly abbreviated RDL, is the hinge in its purest form. The knees stay relatively fixed, the range stops well above the floor, and the entire movement is a controlled lengthening and shortening of the hip extensors under a persistent, long moment arm. It is not simply a lighter conventional deadlift. It is a deliberate choice to keep the hip's moment arm long throughout a partial range, which is precisely why it is such an effective way to load the posterior chain. Read through the moment arm engine, the RDL is the hinge without the assistance of the knee or the floor, the hip's solo with no accompaniment at all.

Reading the hinge as a torque problem

Put the pieces together and a deadlift resolves into a single legible picture. Gravity acts straight down through the bar. The moment arm at the hip and at each lumbar segment is the horizontal gap to that plumb line. Torso inclination sets that gap, and it does so trigonometrically, so small angular changes near horizontal move the demand a great deal. Load scales the whole system in close to linear fashion, as Swinton's data showed. Bar-to-body distance is the lifter's most powerful point of control, shortening every moment arm at once. And the spine is not a beam but a stack, whose tolerance depends on whether bracing keeps its segments rigid while compression and shear pass through. Stance variations, sumo's upright torso, conventional's sagittal drive, the RDL's persistent long arm, are simply different negotiated settlements with the same geometry, and which settlement suits a given lifter depends on the individual body doing the negotiating.

A learner can now look at any hinge and ask the right questions in order. How far is the bar from the body? How inclined is the torso? How long is the moment arm to the hip and to the L4 to L5 junction? Is the stack holding its position? That sequence is the whole chapter, and it is portable. As you will see, it carries cleanly over to the shoulder in the chapters ahead.

Case study: Marlon and the bar that drifted

Case

Marlon is 37 and eight months into consistent barbell training. His conventional deadlift has climbed steadily, and he is proud of the progress. His training partner, who films most of his heavy sets, keeps pointing out the same thing on playback: the bar leaves the floor a few centimeters in front of his shins, drifts slightly further out as it clears his knees, and only swings back against his thighs near lockout. On some reps this costs him nothing he notices. On his heaviest singles, the same weight that felt manageable the week before suddenly feels like it is fighting back harder than the number on the plates should allow.

Run Marlon's situation through this chapter and the mystery resolves into a straightforward mechanical description, not a diagnosis, which is not this course's to give. Start with what changes when the bar drifts. The external moment arm at any joint is the horizontal distance from that joint to the bar's plumb line, and a bar that swings a few centimeters away from the shins lengthens that distance at the hip and at every lumbar segment simultaneously. Marlon's plates did not get heavier from one week to the next. The horizontal gap between his lumbar spine and the bar's line of action did, and torque is a direct product of force and that gap.

Move to the trigonometry from earlier in this chapter. Because the moment arm scales with the sine of the torso's inclination, a bar drifting away from the body near the bottom of the pull, where Marlon's torso is closest to horizontal, produces a disproportionately large increase in demand compared with the same drift happening near lockout, where his torso is closer to vertical. This is very likely why Marlon feels the difference most on his heaviest, lowest-off-the-floor reps and barely notices it on lighter warm-up sets, where the same few centimeters of drift matter far less in absolute torque terms.

Now consider what bracing can and cannot do for him. A tighter brace and a bigger breath before the pull will help Marlon's trunk resist folding under whatever demand the bar creates, by keeping his spinal segments in a more stable relationship to one another as force passes through them. What bracing cannot do is shorten the distance between the bar and his spine. If the bar is drifting, no amount of intra-abdominal pressure fixes the geometry that is creating the extra torque in the first place. His training partner's repeated cue, keep the bar against the shins, is targeting the actual lever in this problem, not a secondary one.

Here is where this chapter's authority stops, deliberately. Nothing above tells Marlon whether his current technique is safe for him specifically, whether the sensation he describes on heavy singles is ordinary effort or something worth having looked at, or how quickly he should try to change a setup habit that took months to build. Those are individual questions that depend on his full training history, his current symptoms if any, and factors well outside a single chapter of mechanics. What this chapter can do, and all it claims to do, is explain why a drifting bar path predictably increases torque at the hip and spine, and why closing that gap is the most direct lever available. If Marlon ever experiences pain rather than simply a harder rep, that observation stops being a coaching note and becomes a reason to see a qualified professional.

Key Takeaways

  • The hinge is a hip-dominant rotation: the knee contributes little, the torso inclines through a large range, and the hip's external moment arm grows long, which is the defining signature of the deadlift family.
  • The moment arm at any joint is the horizontal distance to the bar's vertical plumb line. Because torso offset scales with the sine of inclination, small angle changes near horizontal produce outsized torque changes.
  • The spine is a stack of segments, not a single lever. Load must be described as both compression and shear, and posture redistributes between the two rather than eliminating either (von Arx et al., 2021).
  • Bracing does not shorten the moment arm, but a rigid trunk changes how load is transmitted through the stack, keeping segments in a more tolerable geometry (McGill, 2015; Shoji et al., 2025).
  • Bar-to-body distance is the single largest lever a learner controls. Keeping the bar close shortens every moment arm at once (Swinton et al., 2011).
  • Sumo shortens the spinal moment arm through a more upright torso (Cholewicki et al., 1991; Escamilla et al., 2000), while conventional pulling is sagittally dominant (Hanen et al., 2025). Which style suits a given lifter depends on their proportions.
  • Current evidence treats spinal injury risk during lifting as multifactorial rather than reducible to a single postural rule (Cherni et al., 2026), which is one more reason mechanics education is not the same thing as clinical management.
  • Pain during hinging, an uncontrollable loss of spinal position, or symptoms that track with a specific load are signals that warrant professional evaluation. The mechanics in this chapter explain demand; they do not diagnose or treat.
Looking Ahead

This course has now spent two chapters on the hip. Next, the same moment arm engine simply relocates. In the upper-body pressing and pulling patterns, the joint changes to the shoulder, but the questions are identical: where is the load's plumb line, how long is the moment arm, and how does limb geometry set the demand? The logic built at the hip is about to pay dividends overhead.
