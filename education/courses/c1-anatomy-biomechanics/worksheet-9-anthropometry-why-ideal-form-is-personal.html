Part A · Anthropometry and the Moment-Arm Refresher

Answer in full sentences.

  1. Define anthropometry in your own words, and explain in one more sentence why it functions as an input to every torque equation in this course rather than as a side note about body type.
  2. Explain why two learners can hold the same knee moment arm at the bottom of a squat, load and depth held equal, and still end up with visibly different trunk angles once you account for femur length.
  3. State the chapter's core claim about form and proportion precisely. Explain what the claim does say and what it deliberately does not say about load, goal, mobility, and preference.

Part B · The Squat: Femur-to-Torso Ratio

  1. Explain, mechanically, why a learner with a long femur relative to their torso tends to lean further forward at the bottom of a squat than a learner with a longer torso and a shorter femur. Name the specific joint whose moment arm is driving the difference.
  2. Explain how widening the stance can buy back range of motion for a learner with a long femur. Why does this work mechanically, and what did Demers and colleagues (2018) find about stance width and joint range of motion?
  3. Straub and Powers (2024) found that forward trunk inclination and forward tibia inclination have opposite effects on the knee flexion moment. Explain what this means in plain language, and explain why judging a squat from trunk angle alone can lead you to the wrong conclusion about which joint is under strain.
  4. Two learners squat to identical depth with the bar over the midfoot in both cases. Learner A leans forward markedly through the bottom position; Learner B stays close to vertical. Name the single anthropometric measurement you would most want to know before judging either pattern, and explain what result would make each pattern the mechanically cheaper option.

Part C · The Deadlift: Spinal Moment Arm

  1. Define the spinal moment arm in the conventional deadlift in your own words, and explain specifically what makes it grow or shrink at the moment the bar breaks the floor.
  2. Explain why a relatively long torso tends to favor a sumo stance and a relatively short torso tends to favor conventional pulling. Reference Cholewa and colleagues (2019) in your answer.
  3. Explain why the claim "sumo pulling is easier on the lower back for everyone" oversimplifies the research. Use Escamilla and colleagues' (2000) finding about hip moments in your explanation.
  4. A learner has long arms and a short torso. Predict whether that learner will likely favor conventional or sumo pulling, and name the specific moment arm your prediction turns on. Then explain your reasoning using both Cholewa et al. (2019) and Lockie et al. (2018).

Part D · Pressing and Pulling: Arm-to-Torso Ratio

  1. Explain why longer arms create a longer bar path in the bench press, and explain how a wider grip can be used to manage the resulting moment arm at the shoulder and elbow.
  2. Explain why the same long arms that are a mechanical disadvantage in the bench press become a mechanical advantage in the conventional deadlift. What does this tell you about treating "long limbs" as a single, simple trait to be judged good or bad?
  3. A learner has short arms and an average torso. Predict their most likely mechanical advantage in the bench press and their most likely mechanical disadvantage in overhead pressing, and explain both using the arm-to-torso ratio.

Part E · Reading a Body: Fault or Adaptation?

  1. Given: a learner has a long femur, an average torso, and generous hip and ankle mobility. Predict their likely squat stance width and trunk lean, and explain your reasoning step by step, starting from the bar-over-midfoot constraint.
  2. Given: a learner has average limb proportions but limited ankle dorsiflexion, meaning a limited ability to bring the shin forward over the foot. Explain why this learner's squat can end up looking similar to the long-femur learner's squat even though the underlying cause is completely different.
  3. In your own words, define the difference between a technique fault and a proportion-driven adaptation. Give one original example of each, different from the examples used in the chapter.
  4. A training partner gives a learner a cue that genuinely helped a different learner move more efficiently, but for this learner it does not help and may be increasing demand at a joint. Explain, mechanically, why the same cue can help one person and hurt another.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds mechanical literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how proportion shapes technique, and treat anything that looks clinical, including new or persistent pain, joint instability, or other alarm signs, as a sign that warrants evaluation by a qualified professional. Work within your role: a training professional explains mechanics and refers out, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
