Honest Ranges of Motion: What Joints Can Actually Do

Why the textbook "normal" range is a starting point, not a verdict on your body.

18 min read 8 cited sources

Two lifters stand side by side at the squat rack. Same height, same shoe, same coaching cue. One drops smoothly until the backs of the thighs kiss the calves; the other stalls a hand's width higher, hips tucking under, lumbar spine rounding to buy those last few centimeters. The coach who assumes both bodies contain the same joint is about to give one of them a bad instruction. Because the difference between these two lifters is not effort, mobility work, or willpower. It is the shape of the bone where the femur meets the pelvis — a shape neither of them chose and neither can cue away.

In Chapter 1 we treated bones as levers. A lever, though, is only as useful as the pivot it swings around, and that pivot — the joint — has opinions about which arcs are allowed. This chapter is about learning to read those opinions honestly.

From "Normal Range" to Honest Range

Open almost any clinical reference and you will find a table: hip flexion, 120°; knee flexion, 135°; shoulder abduction, 180°. These numbers are useful, but they are population summaries, and they carry more authority in a coach's imagination than they deserve. When Roaas and Andersson (1982) actually measured passive range of motion in 210 hips of healthy men aged 30 to 40 using the AAOS-recommended technique, they found their normative values differed significantly from the reference figures cited in the standard texts of the day. They attributed the discrepancy to measurement procedure, technique difficulty, and — critically — plain inter-individual variation.

That last phrase is the whole chapter in three words. A "normal" range is the middle of a distribution, and no single person is the middle of every distribution at once. The honest range is what a specific joint, in a specific body, on a specific task, will actually permit. It is discovered, not prescribed. When we stop asking "what is the correct range?" and start asking "what is this joint's honest range, and what is limiting it?", most arguments about "proper depth" or "full range" quietly dissolve into questions about anatomy.

Four Joint Shapes, Four Sets of Permissions

Joints are not generic hinges; their geometry dictates their vocabulary of motion. A quick refresher, because the shape is the argument.

Ball-and-socket: the hip and the shoulder

Both the hip and the glenohumeral joint are ball-and-socket joints, but they represent opposite ends of a design tradeoff. The hip pairs a deep femoral head with a deep acetabulum reinforced by a labrum — a socket that swallows much of the ball. This buys enormous stability at the cost of some range. The shoulder makes the reverse bargain. Gasbarro and colleagues (2017), reviewing the anatomy of the unstable shoulder, describe a glenohumeral joint whose wide range of motion "is achieved at the cost of inherent bony stability": the glenoid is a shallow dish against a large humeral head, so the joint relies on soft-tissue stabilizers — the capsule, glenohumeral ligaments, and rotator cuff — to stay together. Same joint category, radically different permissions.

Hinge-like: the knee and the elbow

The knee and elbow are often called hinges, and to a first approximation they flex and extend in one plane. But "hinge-like" deserves the hedge. The knee's condyles roll and glide and permit a small screw-home rotation near full extension; the elbow's trochlea and capitellum yield slightly different arcs for the ulna and radius. Their end-ranges in flexion are frequently set not by bone but by the bulk of muscle and tissue being compressed — the thigh meeting the calf — which is why knee flexion range varies with limb girth.

Gliding: the intervertebral joints

Between each pair of vertebrae sit the facet joints and the disc, permitting small gliding and tilting motions. Individually tiny, they sum across the column into meaningful flexion, extension, and rotation. This matters for lifting more than it first appears, because — as we will see — the lumbar spine likes to volunteer its motion whenever a stubborn hip runs short.

What Actually Stops a Joint

When a joint reaches the end of its arc, something physically stopped it. That something falls into two broad families, and telling them apart is one of the most useful skills a movement professional can develop.

Bony (hard) end-range

The bony end-range is bone-on-bone contact, or near enough — the femoral neck contacting the acetabular rim, the olecranon seating into its fossa at full elbow extension. It feels abrupt and hard, and it does not negotiate. No stretching protocol lengthens bone. Han and colleagues (2020) made this concrete using cadaveric hips and 3D CT motion simulation across seventeen maneuvers. They found that bony impingement was the true terminator of motion in specific, predictable positions — high abduction combined with flexion, and high flexion combined with adduction and internal rotation. In those configurations, the femur simply ran into the pelvis.

Soft-tissue end-range

The soft-tissue end-range is set by tension: capsule, ligament, and muscle drawing taut. It feels springier and comes on more gradually. Han et al. cleverly defined "soft-tissue restriction" as the difference between the virtual bony-impingement range (what the skeleton alone would allow) and the range actually measured in the intact specimen. In many hip positions, that soft-tissue gap was substantial — the tissue stopped the joint well before the bones would have. Turley and colleagues (2012), using two independent experimental methods, drew a similar map: extension and flexion-with-adduction were limited primarily by soft tissue, whereas flexion and flexion-with-abduction placed the hip at risk of true osseous impingement.

The shoulder illustrates the soft-tissue mechanism beautifully. Gasbarro et al. (2017) describe a glenohumeral capsule that is lax through mid-range and then progressively tightens as the joint approaches its limit, acting as a checkrein. There is very little bone to stop the shoulder; the capsule and ligaments do the job instead. This is exactly why the two families of end-range are worth separating: a hard end-range means the arc is architecturally fixed, while a soft end-range means the limit lives in tissue that responds — over long timescales and within individual limits — to loading and adaptation.

Active Versus Passive: the Gap That Explains "Blocked"

Here is a distinction that resolves a great deal of confusion on the gym floor. Active range of motion is what your own muscles can move you through. Passive range of motion is what an external force — a partner, gravity, a strap, a clinician's hand — can move you through. Gajdosik and Bohannon (1987), in their foundational review of clinical goniometry, state the general rule plainly: active range of motion is normally less than passive range of motion.

Why should there be a gap at all? Because reaching the far edge of a passive arc requires a muscle to be strong and coordinated at a length where it has almost no mechanical advantage — and often the opposing muscle must simultaneously lengthen and relax. If your muscles cannot produce force at that extreme, or cannot let go on the other side, you stop before the tissue would have. The joint could technically go farther; you cannot take it there under your own power. That is precisely the sensation of a lift feeling "blocked" even though nothing structural has been reached: you have arrived at the end of your active range while passive range still has room.

Gajdosik and Bohannon add a caution that every coach should tattoo somewhere visible: a goniometric measurement reflects the joint arc only — it does not tell you the cause of the restriction. The same 90° reading could be produced by a tight muscle, a taut capsule, a bony block, or guarding against pain, and the number alone cannot distinguish them. Measurement gives you the "how much," never the "why." The Joint Range Explorer above lets you feel this: switch between active and passive and watch the active arc sit nested inside the larger passive one, ending sooner for reasons that have nothing to do with the bone.

Range Is Task-Dependent

So far we have spoken as if a joint has a range — a single number waiting to be discovered. It does not. Range is task-dependent: the arc available depends on what the rest of the body is doing while the joint moves. Change the position of the neighboring segments and you change the honest range.

The hip is the clearest case, and the reason cuts to the heart of squatting arguments. Recall Han et al.'s (2020) finding that bony impingement appeared specifically in flexion combined with adduction and internal rotation. Now consider what happens in a squat. As the femur flexes toward the torso, the exact combination of abduction, rotation, and pelvic tilt determines whether the femoral neck clears the acetabular rim or crashes into it. A hip that flexes gorgeously with the thigh angled out and the pelvis neutral may run out of room the instant you narrow the stance or let the pelvis posteriorly tilt. Turley et al. (2012) map the same territory: flexion-with-abduction risks osseous impingement, while other flexion tasks are governed by soft tissue. The joint has not changed between reps — the task has, and with it the limiting structure.

The pelvis borrows from the spine

Task-dependence has a partner in crime: neighboring joints quietly contribute motion you may credit to the hip. Tully, Wagh, and Galea (2002), analyzing hip flexion with computer-aided video in 22 young adults and 22 children, showed that the lumbar spine does not wait politely until the hip is exhausted. Lumbar motion occurs concurrently with hip flexion from the very outset — on average, every 3° of hip motion was accompanied by roughly 1° of lumbar motion, and the lumbar contribution grew as the hip approached its limit. Their pointed conclusion: clinical assessments of hip flexion overestimate true hip joint range because they silently pool in the spine's contribution.

Kuo, Tully, and Galea (2009) extended this to active standing hip-and-knee flexion in 34 healthy older adults and found the lumbar spine supplied 26.6% — about 29° — of total thigh movement, again moving throughout rather than only at the end. Older adults showed less hip flexion and more variable hip-to-lumbar sharing. The practical lesson lands directly on the squat: when a lifter appears to "gain depth," some of that gain may be lumbar flexion masquerading as hip flexion. This is the mechanical anatomy of the butt wink. It is not a moral failing; it is a pelvis borrowing range from a spine because the hip's honest arc, for that task, has been spent.

When the Limit Is Pain

There is a third possibility we have deliberately held back, and it changes how you should behave. Sometimes a joint stops not because of tissue tension and not because of bone contact, but because of pain — and the pain arrives before either mechanical limit. Naili and colleagues (2021) studied eight men with femoroacetabular impingement syndrome, comparing each hip's CT-simulated bony-impingement range against its clinically measured passive range. Strikingly, the observed motion limit often occurred well before the point where the bones would actually collide. The restriction was driven by pain and protective soft-tissue guarding, not by bone-on-bone contact. After surgical intervention, passive range improved.

The applied moral is firm and it is the ethical spine of this course. A restricted or painful range is a signal, not a diagnosis you are equipped to make and certainly not something to force through. When motion stops short with pain, or a range that was open closes down, or an end-feel seems wrong for the person and the task, the correct action is not more mobility drilling or heavier loading — it is referral for professional evaluation. This chapter gives you a working model of why ranges differ so you can instruct movement intelligently. It does not credential you to distinguish guarding from pathology, and confidently forcing a joint past a pain limit is exactly the mistake the model is meant to prevent.

Reading a Joint Honestly

Put the pieces together and you have a procedure, not a table. When you watch a joint reach the end of its arc, ask three questions in order. First: is this the active or the passive edge — did the person run out of strength and control, or did tissue and bone actually stop them? Second: if it is a true structural limit, is the end-feel soft (springy, tissue) or hard (abrupt, bone)? Third: is this arc even the joint's job, or is a neighbor — the lumbar spine, the pelvis — quietly contributing motion you are miscrediting? And overriding all three: if pain is the limiter, you stop asking mechanical questions and start a referral.

Notice what this procedure refuses to do. It never announces "the correct depth" or "full range" as a universal target, because those phrases assume a shared architecture that Roaas and Andersson (1982) already showed does not exist even among healthy men of the same age. It replaces a number you memorize with a question you investigate. The honest range is the one this joint, on this task, actually has — and the levers we built in Chapter 1 now have realistic arcs to swing through.

Key Takeaways

  • Textbook "normal" ranges are population midpoints; the honest range is what a specific joint permits on a specific task, discovered rather than prescribed (Roaas & Andersson, 1982).
  • Joint geometry sets the vocabulary of motion: the deep hip trades range for stability, the shallow shoulder trades stability for range (Gasbarro et al., 2017).
  • End-ranges are bony (hard, bone-on-bone, non-negotiable) or soft-tissue (springy, tension-limited); Han et al. (2020) and Turley et al. (2012) map which hip positions hit which.
  • Active range is normally smaller than passive range; the gap explains why a lift can feel "blocked" while the tissue could still go farther (Gajdosik & Bohannon, 1987).
  • Range is task-dependent, and neighbors borrow motion — the lumbar spine contributes to apparent hip flexion from the outset (Tully et al., 2002; Kuo et al., 2009).
  • When pain limits a range before any mechanical stop, treat it as a signal for professional evaluation, never as something to diagnose or force through (Naili et al., 2021).
Looking Ahead

We have established that joints have honest, individual arcs and that the hip's arc in particular is exquisitely sensitive to task.
