The Squat: Why Yours Doesn't Look Like Mine

Moment-arm logic meets the barbell, and it quietly dismantles one-size-fits-all cueing.

27 min read 8 cited sources

Put two learners under two barbells and ask them to squat to depth. One leans far forward at the hips, chest angled toward the floor, the bar riding low across the back of the shoulders. The other stays close to vertical, knees sliding well past the toes, the bar sitting high on the traps. Both reach the same depth. Both stand the weight up cleanly. And both, if a video of either one made it onto the internet, would be told by someone that they are doing it wrong.

They are not doing it wrong. They are doing different, and the difference is not a matter of style, effort, or discipline. It is mechanics, plain and traceable, once you know where to look. By the end of this chapter you will be able to watch either learner squat and explain, in the language of moment arms and torque, exactly why their movement takes the shape it does, and why it probably should.

This chapter follows a learner named Rico as a running thread. Rico is not a case to be solved or a diagnosis to be made. He is a stand-in for a very common experience: being handed a single picture of correct form and being told, implicitly or explicitly, that anything else is a mistake to be corrected. Watching Rico's situation unfold through the mechanics in this chapter will make the abstract reasoning concrete, and it will show exactly where explaining a movement pattern ends and where professional evaluation of a real person begins.

Notice, too, what the two squats at the top of this section have in common, because it is easy to miss under all the visible difference. Both bars travel in a straight vertical line. Both lifters keep that line over roughly the same patch of foot. Both descend to a depth their own hips can actually reach and stand back up without the bar drifting forward or backward. What differs is everything downstream of those shared constraints: how much the torso tips, how far the knee slides, how the load is shared between hip and knee along the way. A useful theory of the squat has to explain both the sameness and the difference at once, and that is the standard this chapter is built to meet.

One movement, three joints, one job

The squat earns an entire chapter of case study because it makes the moment-arm reasoning built across earlier chapters unavoidably visible. Strip away the cueing and the folklore, and a squat is a single mechanical event: a coordinated rotation at the hip, knee, and ankle, timed so that the whole-body center of mass (COM) stays balanced over the base of support while a loaded barbell travels down and back up.

Gravity acts straight down, so the demand a bar places on any joint is not simply its weight. It is that weight multiplied by the horizontal distance between the joint's axis of rotation and the bar's vertical line of gravity. That horizontal distance is the moment arm, and the product of load and moment arm is the external torque a joint's musculature has to overcome. Nothing about a squat stays mysterious once you commit to tracking those three moment arms, at hip, knee, and ankle, through the whole descent.

Here is the essential move to watch for. As a learner descends, the shank tilts forward and the thigh folds, and the joints travel horizontally relative to the bar's fixed vertical line. The hip drifts backward, away from that line. The knee drifts forward, toward it and often past it. Both horizontal gaps grow as depth increases, and torque climbs at every joint at once, peaking somewhere near the bottom of the movement. This is why the hole, the deepest position in a squat, is where the lift feels hardest. It is not a failure of nerve or a mysterious sticking point. It is simply the moment when the moment arms are stacked most steeply against the lifter.

Chapter 4 introduced torque as the currency every lift gets priced in. The squat spends that currency at three registers at once, and the exchange rate between them is set by only two things: how far the bar sits from the body, and how far each joint sits from the bar. Everything that follows in this chapter, stance width, bar height, segment length, depth, is really an argument about how that currency gets divided among the hip, the knee, and the ankle. No cue changes the total bill. Every cue simply decides who pays it.

It helps to make this concrete with rough numbers, because the abstraction can hide how large the effect actually is. Picture a bar loaded to twice a learner's body weight, and picture the hip's horizontal moment arm growing from roughly four centimeters near the top of the lift to roughly eighteen centimeters near the bottom, a change well within the range these mechanics actually produce. Torque at that joint is the product of the load and that distance, so a moment arm that grows four-and-a-half-fold produces a torque demand that grows by roughly the same factor, even though the bar itself never gets any heavier during the rep. The bar's weight is constant throughout the descent. The difficulty is not. That gap between constant load and rising difficulty is the entire reason a squat has a sticking region at all, and it is a purely geometric fact, not a statement about anyone's effort or willpower.

The bar wants to be over the mid-foot

Everything above rests on one anchoring fact: for the squat to stay balanced, the barbell's vertical line of gravity has to pass, closely enough, through the mid-foot for the entire lift. That is not a coaching preference dressed up as physics. It is a balance requirement. The base of support is the contact patch of the feet on the floor, and the combined COM of body and bar stays in equilibrium only when its vertical line falls inside that patch. Because a heavily loaded barbell dominates the mass of the whole body-and-bar system, keeping the bar tracking over the mid-foot is what keeps the entire system upright.

Schoenfeld (2010), synthesizing the squat kinematics literature, frames the movement in exactly these terms: a problem of keeping the combined line of gravity over the base of support while distributing the resulting load across the ankle, knee, hip, and spine. Accept that framing and a great deal falls out for free. If the bar sits high on the trapezius, the torso has to stay relatively upright to keep that high point stacked over the mid-foot, which pushes the knees forward and increases the demand on them. If the bar sits lower, across the rear of the shoulders, the torso has to tilt further forward to bring that lower point back over the mid-foot, which pushes the hips back and increases the demand on them. A learner does not choose how much their trunk leans the way they choose a rep count. Trunk lean is mostly dictated, by where the bar sits and by the unbending requirement that it track over the mid-foot.

Straub and Powers (2024), in a clinical biomechanical review aimed squarely at practitioners, make this the organizing principle for the whole exercise: the sagittal-plane orientation of the trunk and the tibia governs the external moments produced at the hip and knee. Change one and the other changes with it, because both answer to the same balance constraint. This is the sentence worth memorizing before anything else in this chapter: you cannot make a squat easier on the knees and easier on the hips at the same time. You can only move the load from one to the other.

This single constraint is also why forward lean deserves a more careful reputation than it usually gets. A learner who leans further forward than a training partner is not automatically squatting with worse technique. They may simply be solving the identical balance equation with a different set of body segments. We will return to that claim with concrete numbers once bar height and anthropometry are both on the table.

It is worth being precise about what mid-foot actually means here, because the phrase gets used loosely. It does not mean the bar sits directly above the toes, the heel, or the ball of the foot. It means the vertical line through the bar passes through roughly the middle third of the foot's length, the region that gives a learner the most room to correct small deviations forward or backward without tipping. A bar path that strays outside that window does not fail gracefully. Stability drops sharply once the combined line of gravity approaches the edge of the base of support, because at that point there is almost no margin left before the whole system tips onto the toes or rocks back onto the heels. This is also why experienced lifters talk about feeling the bar in their feet: they are, whether they use this language or not, monitoring exactly the constraint this section describes.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common belief holds that an upright torso is inherently safer than a leaned-forward one, so every learner should be coached toward vertical. But torso angle is not the constraint. Bar position over the mid-foot is. An upright torso that drags the bar forward of the mid-foot is less stable than a leaned torso that keeps the bar tracking correctly. Coaching torso angle in isolation, without reference to the bar path, treats a downstream effect as if it were the actual rule.

Hip versus knee: the load has to go somewhere

The single most clarifying study in the whole squat literature is a small one. Fry, Smith, and Schilling (2003) had seven trained men squat two ways: once letting the knees travel forward naturally, and once with a wooden barrier fixed in front of the shins that physically prevented the knees from passing the toes, the classic do not let your knees pass your toes cue turned into hardware. When forward knee travel was blocked, knee torque fell, by roughly 22 percent in their measurements. That is the result the cue promises, and it is real.

But the lifters still had to reach the same depth, and the bar still had to stay over the mid-foot. With the shins pinned close to vertical, the only way down was to shove the hips backward and pitch the trunk sharply forward, and that adjustment drove hip torque up by roughly tenfold. The total demand on the body was not eliminated. It was relocated, almost entirely, from the knee to the hip and, by extension, to the lumbar spine.

This is the mechanical center of the chapter, worth sitting with. Illmeier and Rechberger (2023), reviewing decades of anthropometric and biomechanical evidence on anterior knee displacement (AKD), reach a matching verdict: restricting how far the knee travels forward does not make a squat safer in any absolute sense. It shifts torque toward the hip and lumbar spine, and for a large share of trained individuals, some degree of natural forward knee travel is not a flaw to be coached out. It is a mechanical necessity if the squat is going to happen at all, given that particular learner's proportions. The keep the shins vertical rule is not a law of good movement. It is a decision to prefer hip loading over knee loading, made without knowing whose hips or knees are actually being discussed.

Think About It

If a learner reports knee discomfort and is told to keep their shins vertical on every rep going forward, what does the Fry et al. (2003) result predict will happen to the demand on their hips and lower back? Note that a change in where discomfort shows up is a sign that warrants professional evaluation, not proof that either version of the squat was inherently correct.

Escamilla's (2001) comprehensive review of knee biomechanics during the squat adds real reassurance here. Looking across tibiofemoral shear forces, patellofemoral compression, and muscle activation patterns, he concludes that a dynamically performed squat, kept within a learner's honest range of motion (ROM), is an effective and generally knee-friendly way to train the entire lower limb, and that it can build knee stability rather than threaten it. Forward knee travel is not a hazard to be engineered out of existence. It is part of how the mechanics are supposed to work. Where it coincides with pain, that pain is a sign that warrants professional evaluation by a qualified clinician, not a verdict on the squat pattern itself.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A popular belief holds that any forward travel of the knee past the toes is inherently dangerous and must be coached away. But the knee joint, tendons, and cartilage tolerate substantial forward travel under load, and the caution behind the original rule of thumb was narrower than the rule that eventually grew out of it. Restricting knee travel does not delete torque from the body. It moves that torque to the hip and spine. Forward knee travel, by itself, is not evidence that something has gone wrong.

It is worth understanding why the keep your knees behind your toes rule spread so widely in the first place, because the origin explains both its appeal and its limits. Illmeier and Rechberger (2023) trace the rule to older biomechanical work from the 1970s and 1980s that measured rising patellofemoral stress as the knee traveled further forward and concluded, reasonably for its time, that limiting that travel should reduce stress on the knee. The finding was not wrong. The generalization built on top of it went further than the evidence supported, because it treated a single joint in isolation and never asked where the avoided load actually went. A rule that is locally true, less forward knee travel does lower knee torque, can still be globally misleading if it ignores the rest of the kinetic chain. The squat's three joints are not independent systems. They are one mechanical chain, and a cue that only accounts for one link in that chain will always be incomplete.

High-bar, low-bar, and the geometry of the back

Nowhere is torque redistribution easier to see than in the difference between the two common back-squat styles. Glassbrook and colleagues (2017), reviewing the biomechanical comparisons between the two, describe a consistent picture. The high-bar back squat, with the bar resting on the upper trapezius, produces greater knee flexion, a more upright torso, and typically a deeper squat, along with more work for the quadriceps. The low-bar back squat, with the bar carried lower, across the rear deltoids, produces greater hip flexion and a more pronounced forward lean, along with more work through the posterior chain.

Run this back through the mid-foot constraint and the pattern falls out for free, exactly as before. Lower the bar on the back, and keeping that lower point stacked over the mid-foot requires more forward lean. More forward lean pushes the hips back, which lengthens the hip moment arm and shortens the knee moment arm. Raise the bar, and the reverse happens. Neither style is more correct than the other. They are two solutions to one balance problem that happen to distribute torque differently, which is exactly why a competitive powerlifter chasing a heavier total and a weightlifter training a catch position choose different bar placements on purpose, not by accident and not out of ignorance of the alternative.

The choice between the two is not cosmetic, and it maps directly onto why different sports settle on different defaults. Competitive powerlifting scores a squat, a bench press, and a deadlift purely by the heaviest weight lifted, with no bonus for style, so low-bar's typically shorter bar-travel distance and heavier posterior-chain involvement suit the goal directly. Olympic weightlifting asks a lifter to catch a bar overhead or at the shoulders in a deep, upright position immediately after a pull from the floor, so a high-bar-style squat pattern, with its more vertical torso and greater knee flexion, rehearses the exact position the sport actually requires. Neither community adopted its default by accident or superstition. Each picked the solution that best matches what the loaded torque distribution needs to accomplish for its specific task, which is the same logic this chapter keeps applying to individual anatomy rather than to competitive rulebooks.

Bar height is the first lever a learner can actually choose. Almost everything covered in the rest of this chapter is not a choice at all. It is the skeleton the learner showed up with.

Why individual variation is the whole point

We have been treating bar height as something a learner adjusts. But the deeper source of variation in how a squat looks is not adjustable at all. It is the skeleton a learner brought to the platform on day one. At least four anthropometric variables reshape the torque distribution before a single plate goes on the bar.

Segment lengths

A long femur relative to the torso is the clearest case. The longer the thigh bone, the further back the hips have to travel to keep the system balanced at any given depth, and the more the torso has to lean forward to keep the bar over the mid-foot. A learner with long femurs therefore tends to squat with a more horizontal torso and a hip-dominant torque profile, not because they lack core strength or discipline, but because their proportions require it. A longer torso partly offsets a long femur. A short torso amplifies it. Two learners with identical femur lengths can look completely different at the bottom of a squat if one of them also carries a noticeably shorter or longer torso.

Hip socket orientation and mobility

How deep a learner can squat while keeping their spine in a supportable position, recall Chapter 2's insistence on real, individually measured ranges of motion rather than idealized ones, depends heavily on the orientation of the acetabulum, the hip socket, and the shape of the femoral neck, both of which vary meaningfully between individuals and cannot be trained or stretched into a different shape. This is part of why some learners find a wide stance with the toes turned out opens up their depth, while others find the same stance awkward or even blocked outright. Lorenzetti and colleagues (2018), using three-dimensional motion capture and force plates across nine combinations of stance width and foot angle in both novice and experienced squatters, confirmed that both variables significantly change the moments produced at the hip and knee in the front-to-back and side-to-side planes, and that a learner's experience level changes how their knees track through the movement. There is no single stance width or foot angle that is correct for every hip, because there is no single hip shape shared by every learner.

Ankle mobility

The ankle largely sets the ceiling on how far the knee can travel forward. Limited ankle dorsiflexion, meaning the ankle cannot bend the shin far enough over the foot, caps how far the shank can tilt, and by the same mid-foot logic running through this whole chapter, that cap forces the hips back and the torso down, pushing torque toward the hip. This is the mechanical rationale behind a squat shoe with an elevated heel: raising the heel effectively extends the usable range of dorsiflexion, letting the shank tilt further, the knee travel forward, and the torso stay more upright, which shifts torque back toward the knee. The shoe is not cutting corners. It is rebalancing a system that was mechanically limited somewhere else.

It would be tidy to conclude that segment lengths alone predict how a given learner squats. The evidence resists that tidy conclusion. Kim and colleagues (2021) examined femur-to-tibia length ratios alongside hip and ankle flexibility and relative muscular strength in fifty-three learners squatting at 75 percent of one-repetition maximum (1RM), and found that net joint torques and flexion angles correlated more strongly with relative muscular strength and joint flexibility than with the ratio of leg segment lengths. Anthropometry sets the terrain a learner has to cross. Mobility and strength determine how that particular learner actually negotiates it. The honest model is multivariable, which is precisely why correct form cannot be reduced to a single template traced from someone else's body.

Three archetypes, roughly sketched

It can help to sketch a few composite patterns, not as categories to sort learners into, but as illustrations of how these variables combine. Picture a learner with proportionally short femurs, an average torso, and generous hip and ankle mobility. That combination lets the shank tilt well forward and the knee travel a long way without ever demanding much trunk lean to keep the bar over the mid-foot, which is why some lifters can squat almost straight up and down with the bar high on the back. Now picture a learner with long femurs and an average or short torso, the profile Rico shares, whose hips must travel further back at any given depth, and whose torso must lean further forward to compensate, regardless of how mobile their ankles happen to be. Finally, picture a learner with average limb proportions but limited ankle dorsiflexion, who reaches a mechanical ceiling on knee travel earlier than the other two, and who compensates the same way, hips back, torso forward, but for a mobility reason rather than a length reason. Three different bodies, three different visible squats, and, notice, the same forward-lean solution emerging from three different mechanical causes. A single photograph of any one of these squats tells you almost nothing about which cause produced it.

Think About It

A learner with long femurs and limited ankle dorsiflexion is told, in the same coaching session, to keep an upright chest and to stop the knees at the toes. Using the mid-foot constraint from earlier in this chapter, work out why these two instructions may be mechanically incompatible for this particular skeleton, and which one that learner's body is more likely to override without being asked.

The strength curve, revisited

Chapter 4 gave us the vocabulary to describe a lift's strength curve, meaning how the external torque, and therefore the perceived difficulty, changes across a full range of motion. The squat's curve more or less writes itself once the moment arms are on the table. External moment arms at the hip, knee, and ankle all grow through the descent and peak somewhere near the bottom, then shrink again on the way up. That is why the squat behaves as an ascending-difficulty movement in the region that matters most: hardest coming out of the hole, progressively easier toward lockout, where the moment arms collapse toward zero and the joints stack almost directly under the load.

Layer in Chapter 3's discussion of lines of pull and the picture sharpens further. Which muscle group dominates at any instant depends on which joint is carrying the torque at that particular joint angle, and that assignment shifts continuously through the lift. Near the bottom, the knee extensors and hip extensors are both working hard together. As the torso rises and the hip moment arm shortens faster than the knee's does, the emphasis migrates toward the knee extensors. A learner squatting low-bar, with a longer hip moment arm sustained through more of the ascent, leans more heavily on the hip extensors across the whole lift than a learner squatting high-bar with the same load on the bar. Which muscle does the squat work has no single, fixed answer, because the true answer is a function of joint angle, bar position, and skeleton, three variables this course keeps returning to, converging here on one lift.

Restricting how far the knee travels forward does not remove load from the body. It relocates that load elsewhere. The question worth asking is never whether a joint is under load, but which joint, for which learner, and whether that learner's tissues are prepared for it.

Forward lean is often mechanics, not error

It is worth stating plainly, because so much coaching language implies otherwise: forward lean in a squat is not, by itself, a technical error. It is the direct, calculable consequence of where the bar sits and how a learner's segments are proportioned, filtered through the one requirement that cannot be negotiated away, that the combined line of gravity of bar and body stays over the base of support.

A learner told to keep your chest up regardless of their proportions is being handed a cue that fights their own skeleton. For someone with a long femur, a short torso, or limited ankle dorsiflexion, forcing the torso more upright than their geometry allows does not make the squat safer. It typically means the bar drifts forward of the mid-foot, which is the actual balance failure that the chest up cue is usually trying, clumsily, to prevent. A learner who corrects one imbalance by creating another has not fixed anything. They have traded a visible problem for a less visible one.

None of this means every degree of forward lean is automatically fine, or that coaching cues are useless. A bar that drifts forward of the mid-foot at any depth is a genuine balance problem, regardless of how much the torso happens to be leaning at that moment, because it is the bar's horizontal position relative to the foot, not the angle of the torso by itself, that determines whether the lift is stable. Torso angle and bar path are related but not identical, and the bar-over-mid-foot principle is the one that should never be relaxed. Torso angle is downstream of anthropometry and bar height. Bar path over the mid-foot is the actual constraint. Keep those two ideas separate and most arguments about correct squat form collapse, because most of them are really arguments about torso angle when the only thing that was ever non-negotiable was the bar path.

A practical consequence follows directly from this distinction. If a learner wants a quick, honest check on their own squat, torso angle is the wrong thing to eyeball, because there is no fixed angle that is correct for every skeleton. The bar's path relative to the mid-foot is the right thing to watch, because that constraint does not change from learner to learner. A side-on video, a mirror at the right angle, or simply a mark on the platform can answer the useful question directly: did the bar travel in a tight vertical line over the mid-foot, or did it drift forward toward the toes or backward toward the heels. That is a question anthropometry cannot complicate, which is exactly why it is the more useful thing to coach.

Case study: Rico and the squat that looked broken

Case

Rico is 34 and has been squatting for about three years. His numbers are solid and climbing steadily, but he stopped posting his training videos after a comment thread convinced him that his squat looked broken. In the clip, his torso pitches forward well past what several commenters considered acceptable, his hips travel noticeably behind his heels, and his knees barely reach his toes at the bottom. Someone told him he was squatting like a stiff-legged hip hinge with extra steps and suggested he needed more ankle mobility work and a stricter knees-over-toes cue before he hurt his back. Rico tried both for six weeks. His bar path stayed similar, but his lower back grew more uncomfortable through the lift, and he arrived at this course wanting to know whether the commenters had been right.

Run Rico's squat through this chapter's tools and the picture changes. His proportions, once he measured them honestly rather than guessing, showed a femur noticeably longer than his torso and ankle dorsiflexion on the lower end of the typical range for a trained lifter. Both facts point in the same direction under the mid-foot constraint. A long femur pushes the hips back and the torso forward to keep the bar balanced. Limited dorsiflexion caps how far the knee can travel forward, which pushes the hips back and the torso forward again, for an entirely separate reason. Rico's forward lean was not one problem. It was two independent mechanical facts about his body, both pointing the same direction, showing up as a single visible pattern.

The knees-over-toes cue his commenters pushed on him ran directly into the second fact. With his ankle mobility already capped, restricting his knees further than they were already traveling removed one of the only degrees of freedom his lower body had left for reaching depth. Something had to give, and by the logic of Fry et al. (2003), the something that gives is hip and lumbar torque. His discomfort after six weeks of the stricter cue is not proof that his original squat was dangerous. It is consistent, exactly, with what the mechanics predict happens when a learner with his particular limitations is told to keep the shins more vertical than his ankles allow.

None of this means Rico's lower back discomfort should be waved away as normal, or explained away as mechanics and left there. Discomfort that appears or worsens after a change in technique is a sign that warrants professional evaluation, not a data point to be self-diagnosed from a mechanics chapter or a comment thread, however confident either one sounds. What this chapter can responsibly tell Rico is why his original squat, the one that drew the criticism, was a mechanically coherent solution to his own anthropometry rather than a mistake copied from a worse coach. What it cannot tell him is whether his current discomfort needs rest, a change in load, a change in depth, or a clinician's evaluation. That distinction, between explaining a pattern and clearing a person to keep training through discomfort, is exactly the line this course does not cross.

It is worth naming what a mechanically literate response to Rico's situation actually looks like, because it is easy to confuse with either extreme he encountered online. It is not you were fine all along, stop worrying, because his new discomfort is real and deserves a real answer. It is not you were right to be scared, your squat was dangerous, because nothing in his original pattern was mechanically incoherent. It is closer to this: your original squat was a reasonable, individually appropriate solution to a real balance problem, the cue you were given fights against a mobility limit you actually have, and the discomfort that followed is worth having assessed by someone qualified to examine your spine and hips directly, because a chapter about torque cannot do that examination for you.

Think About It

Rico's femur-to-torso ratio and his ankle dorsiflexion both push in the same mechanical direction. Suppose a second learner had Rico's long femur, but excellent ankle dorsiflexion instead of limited dorsiflexion. Using the logic in this chapter, would you expect that learner's squat to look more like Rico's original pattern, or more like the upright, knees-forward pattern his commenters wanted? Explain why.

When the mechanics produce discomfort

A steady discipline has run underneath this entire chapter: explain the mechanics, and stop there. Forward knee travel, a heel that lifts under load, a lower back that rounds near depth, each of these is a mechanical observation with a mechanical explanation, and each one can, in some learners, coincide with discomfort. When it does, the appropriate next step is professional evaluation by a qualified clinician, not a rule pulled from a forum thread or, for that matter, a rule pulled from this chapter. Our job here is the working model: to explain why a particular learner's knees travel forward, why their torso pitches down, why their heels want to rise, so that whoever eventually does the evaluating starts from mechanics instead of mythology.

That distinction matters more than it might first appear. Understanding that a restrictive cue relocates torque rather than deleting it is exactly the kind of insight that keeps a well-meaning training partner, or a learner coaching themselves from a phone screen, from confidently making a problem worse. It is also exactly the kind of insight that has limits. Mechanics can tell you where load goes. It cannot tell you whether a particular joint, tendon, or disc in a particular person is currently able to tolerate that load, and it cannot rule out causes that have nothing to do with technique at all. Pain and injury are signs that warrant professional evaluation, not puzzles to be solved by rereading a biomechanics chapter more carefully.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Key Takeaways

  • The squat is one coordinated rotation at hip, knee, and ankle, all subordinate to keeping the bar's line of gravity over the mid-foot for balance.
  • External torque at each joint equals bar load times that joint's horizontal moment arm; all three moment arms peak near the bottom, which is why the hole feels hardest.
  • Torque is relocated, not created or destroyed, by cueing: restricting knee travel cuts knee torque by roughly a fifth but raises hip torque roughly tenfold (Fry et al., 2003; Illmeier & Rechberger, 2023).
  • Bar height dictates trunk lean through the mid-foot constraint: high-bar loads the knee more, low-bar loads the hip more (Glassbrook et al., 2017; Straub & Powers, 2024).
  • Femur and torso length, hip and ankle mobility, stance, and strength all reshape the torque distribution; segment lengths set the terrain, but strength and flexibility often predict joint torques more strongly (Kim et al., 2021; Lorenzetti et al., 2018).
  • Forward lean is frequently a mechanical consequence of anthropometry and bar height, not a technical fault; correct squat form is a family of mechanically valid solutions, not a single template, and pain is a sign that warrants professional evaluation, never a verdict a learner should render on themselves.
