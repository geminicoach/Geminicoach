Chapter 4: Torque, the Moment Arm, and the Currency of Lifting

Work the equation by hand: define the moment arm, weigh internal against external torque, and explain why a lift's hardest inches are a matter of geometry, not sudden weakness.

Part A · The Core Equation

Write out the relationship that anchors this entire chapter, then explain the tricky part of its definition in your own words.

  1. Write the equation for torque in symbols, then in a full sentence. Name each quantity, state the unit it is measured in, and state the unit torque itself is measured in.
  2. The moment arm is not simply the distance from the joint to wherever a force is applied. Explain, in your own words, what "perpendicular distance from the joint axis to the line of action of the force" actually means, and describe a situation where the point of application and the true moment arm are clearly not the same distance.
  3. Explain why a force whose line of action passes directly through the joint axis produces zero torque, no matter how large that force is.

Part B · Internal Torque Against External Torque

  1. Explain why internal moment arms, measured in single centimeters, are so much smaller than external moment arms, which can run to tens of centimeters. What does that size mismatch mean for how much force a muscle actually has to produce to hold a load static?
  2. A learner can change their stance, grip, or bar path from one set to the next. Can that same learner change their internal moment arm just as easily? Explain why or why not, and name what actually determines an internal moment arm's length.

Part C · Why the Range of Motion Changes Everything

A lift is a rotation, not a single frozen pose, so the geometry keeps shifting from the first inch to the last.

  1. Explain how the external moment arm on a raised limb, such as in a lateral raise, changes as the limb sweeps from hanging at the side to fully horizontal. Why does the movement feel almost weightless near the bottom and hardest near horizontal?
  2. Explain what a strength curve is, and describe, in your own words, what a "sticking point" actually represents in terms of the demand curve and the strength curve. Why is it a mistake to assume a sticking point always means the muscle involved has gotten weak?
  3. A squat can be performed with a more upright torso or a more forward-leaning torso. Explain how that choice redistributes torque demand between the hip and the knee, and why neither pattern eliminates the total demand, only relocates it.

Part D · Do the Math

Show your work for each calculation. Round to one decimal place where needed.

Problem 1 · Holding a load static A barbell held motionless at the bottom of a deadlift produces an external moment arm of 0.35 meters (35 centimeters) at the lumbar spine, and the load pulling down through that moment arm is 250 newtons (N).
  1. Calculate the external torque at the lumbar spine. Show the formula and the numbers you used.
  2. If the erector spinae's internal moment arm at that same joint is 0.07 meters (7 centimeters), calculate the muscle force required to exactly balance that external torque and hold the position static.
  3. Express that muscle force as a multiple of the load itself (for example, "about ___ times the load"). What does that multiple tell you about why a modest-looking barbell can demand enormous force from the muscles crossing the spine?
Problem 2 · A moment arm that changes with angle A dumbbell lateral raise uses a 0.30 meter arm segment and a 60 newton (N) dumbbell. At a segment angle of 30 degrees from vertical, the external moment arm is 0.30 × sin(30°) = 0.15 meters. At 90 degrees (fully horizontal), the external moment arm is the full 0.30 meters.
  1. Calculate the external torque at 30 degrees, and the external torque at 90 degrees. Show both calculations.
  2. Explain, using your two answers, why the same 60 N dumbbell can feel almost weightless early in the raise and much harder as the arm approaches horizontal, even though the weight itself never changes.

Part E · Reading a Real Lift

Apply the framework from this chapter to a real technique question, the way you would if someone asked you to explain it.

  1. Coaches often cue "keep the bar close" during a deadlift or a row. Using the vocabulary of moment arms and torque, explain exactly what that cue is asking a lifter to minimize, and why following it reduces the force the low-back muscles must produce.
  2. A wider, more upright stance (sometimes called a sumo-style pull) tends to reduce the demand on the lumbar spine compared to a narrower, more forward-leaning stance, but it increases demand elsewhere. Explain this trade-off in terms of moment arms shifting between joints rather than disappearing.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds mechanical literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how torque and moment arms shape the way a lift feels, and treat anything that looks clinical, a new or sharp sensation, a change from a person's usual pattern, or any other alarm sign, as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician.
