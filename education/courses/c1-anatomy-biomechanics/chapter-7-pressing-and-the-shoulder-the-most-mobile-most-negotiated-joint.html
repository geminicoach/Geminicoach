Pressing and the Shoulder: The Most Mobile, Most Negotiated Joint

Why the overhead press is a moment-arm rollercoaster, and why the socket has to be steered under the arm.

19 min read 9 cited sources

Stand in front of a loaded barbell resting on your shoulders and press it overhead. Somewhere between your chin and the crown of your head, the bar will slow. Not because you got tired — the whole rep takes under two seconds — and not because your muscles suddenly quit firing. They are, in fact, screaming at full volume. The bar slows because you have pressed it into the single worst mechanical position of the entire lift. Push through, and it accelerates again toward lockout as if the load had lightened. It didn't. What changed was the geometry.

That region of stall has a name every experienced lifter knows by feel — the sticking point — and it is the clearest, most tangible payoff of the line-of-pull thinking we built in Chapter 3. This chapter moves the moment-arm engine upstairs, to the joint where geometry matters most and hides least: the shoulder.

The shoulder is not a joint. It is a negotiation.

We speak casually of "the shoulder joint" as though the arm hangs from a single hinge. Mechanically, this is a convenient lie. The glenohumeral joint — the ball of the humeral head sitting in the shallow socket of the glenoid — is the obvious rotating element, and it is astonishingly mobile. That mobility is precisely its problem. The socket is small, shallow, and covers only a fraction of the ball at any instant. A joint built for range is, by construction, a joint that has traded away inherent stability. The bones alone do not hold it together; soft tissue and active muscular coordination do.

Which means the socket cannot stay still. For the arm to elevate through its full arc while keeping the humeral head well-seated, the scapula — the shoulder blade — must travel with it, tilting and rotating on the back of the ribcage to keep the glenoid pointed underneath the moving arm. This coordinated dance is scapulohumeral rhythm, and it is the reason we treat the shoulder as a multi-joint system rather than a ball in a cup.

The quantitative story begins with Inman, Saunders, and Abbott (1944), whose roentgenographic and bone-pin work established the classic figure taught for eighty years: a roughly 2:1 ratio of glenohumeral to scapulothoracic motion. For every three degrees the arm rises relative to the trunk, about two degrees come from the ball-and-socket and one from the blade sliding on the ribs. It was a landmark abstraction — the first evidence that the shoulder is a linked chain, not a lone hinge.

But a fixed ratio is too clean to be true, and later instrumentation showed it. McQuade and Smidt (1998), using electromagnetic tracking on 25 subjects, found the rhythm is anything but constant: during passive elevation it fell from nearly 8:1 to about 2:1 across the arc, and — crucially for us — loading changed it. Under heavy resistance the ratio shifted from roughly 1.9:1 early to 4.5:1 later in the range. In other words, how much the blade contributes depends on where you are in the movement and how heavy the load is. Add weight to a press and the scapular contribution reorganizes itself. McClure and colleagues (2001), fixing sensors directly to bone pins, went further and showed scapular motion is not even single-axis: during scapular-plane elevation the blade upwardly rotated about 50°, tilted posteriorly about 30°, and externally rotated about 24°. The socket is being steered in three dimensions at once.

Think About It

If the scapulohumeral ratio rises under heavy load, what is the scapula doing more of, late in a maximal press, compared to a light warm-up rep? Why might that matter for keeping the glenoid "under" the bar?

For our applied purposes, the takeaway is not a number to memorize but a principle: the position of the socket is an active, load-dependent variable. When that coordination degrades — the blade fails to rotate and tilt on schedule — the consensus literature calls it scapular dyskinesis. Kibler and colleagues (2013), in the "scapular summit" consensus, were careful to frame dyskinesis as a potential impairment to shoulder function, not a diagnosis, and noted that impingement-type symptoms are particularly sensitive to it. We will honor that framing throughout: altered scapular motion is a signal, not a verdict. We flag it and refer it; we do not diagnose it, and we certainly do not press through it. We will make the scapula the star of Chapter 8. Here, keep it in the back of your mind as the thing that must move well for everything downstream to make sense.

One muscle, many jobs: the deltoid's changing line of pull

Recall the core lesson of Chapter 3: a muscle's usefulness at a joint is governed by its line of pull relative to the joint's axis — specifically, by the perpendicular distance from that axis to the line of the muscle's force, its internal moment arm. A muscle with a long moment arm turns its tension efficiently into rotation. A muscle whose line of pull runs nearly through the joint has almost no moment arm, so most of its force does something other than rotate the bone — it compresses the joint surfaces together.

Nowhere in the body does this play out more dramatically than at the shoulder, because the arm sweeps through such a large arc that a muscle's geometry relative to the joint is completely rewritten as the limb moves. The deltoid is the case study. Hik and Ackland (2018), in a systematic review consolidating moment-arm data across the deltoid's three sub-regions, the pectoralis major, latissimus, teres major, and all four rotator cuff muscles, reported that the anterior and middle deltoid possess the largest elevator moment arms of any muscle crossing the glenohumeral joint during coronal- and scapular-plane abduction and during flexion. The deltoid is the prime elevator. But — and this is the whole point — those moment-arm magnitudes "vary substantially with joint angle."

Consider the arm hanging at your side and the middle deltoid trying to lift it out sideways. At that low starting angle, the muscle's fibers run almost vertically, nearly parallel to the humerus — its line of pull points largely up the bone into the socket. The moment arm is short. Most of the deltoid's tension jams the humeral head into the glenoid rather than rotating the arm upward. This is exactly why the first few degrees of a lateral raise feel oddly weak and why the supraspinatus of the rotator cuff is so important early: the geometry is against the big mover. As the arm rises toward horizontal, the fiber angle changes, the moment arm lengthens toward its peak, and the same tension now produces far more rotation. Hughes and colleagues (1997), computing instantaneous moment arms for ten shoulder muscles across sagittal, scapular, and coronal planes, mapped exactly this: the deltoid carries the largest elevation moment arm, with the anterior head dominant in anterior planes and the middle head dominant in scapular and coronal planes — the plane you press in determines which sub-region is doing the geometric heavy lifting.

Think About It

A lateral raise "burns" hardest around the point where the arm reaches shoulder height, not at the bottom. Given what you just read about moment arms, is that because the muscle works harder there, or because the geometry finally lets its effort count? Hold your answer — the dial below will test it.

The same reasoning explains the depressors. Hughes et al. (1997) and Hik and Ackland (2018) both found the pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and teres major carry the largest depressor moment arms during elevation — they are geometrically built to pull the arm back down and forward. In a horizontal press (the bench), the pectoralis's line of pull is favorably oriented to drive the arm across the body; in an overhead press it becomes a brake on the last portion of the range. Same muscles, opposite roles, decided entirely by where the arm is in space.

The external side of the ledger: mapping the sticking point

So far we have talked about the internal moment arm — the muscle's leverage. The sticking point lives on the other side of the ledger: the external moment arm, the perpendicular distance from the joint axis to the line of gravity running straight down through the bar. External moment arm times load equals the external moment — the demand your muscles must overcome. And in a press, that external moment arm is not fixed. It changes every instant the bar moves, because the bar and the shoulder do not travel in the same vertical line.

The definitive experimental picture comes from Elliott, Wilson, and Kerr (1989), who put ten elite powerlifters under 3D cinematography and surface EMG during bench pressing at 80% of 1-RM, at maximal, and at supramaximal loads. Two findings anchor everything we say about sticking points. First, the EMG of the prime movers — the sternal pectoralis, anterior deltoid, and long head of triceps — peaked at the very start of the ascent and stayed high. Muscular effort did not dip during the sticking region. The lifters were trying just as hard. Second, and this is the mechanical heart of it, while the resultant moment arm about the shoulder axis decreased through the ascent, the moment arm about the elbow reached a minimum precisely during the sticking region before increasing again.

The sticking region reflects a biomechanically poor mechanical position — not a drop in muscular effort.

after Elliott, Wilson & Kerr (1989)

Read that carefully, because it overturns the intuitive story. The bar does not slow because you weaken. It slows because you arrive at the configuration where leverage is worst — where the external demand relative to your available leverage peaks. Effort is maxed the whole time; only the geometry varies. This is the single most important reframe in the chapter.

The overhead press is an even more vivid case than the bench because the sticking point is so reliably located just above the collarbones and in front of the face. Here the villain is bar path. To clear your head, the bar has to travel a slightly curved path — out and around the chin, then back over the crown at lockout. Any portion of that path where the bar sits forward of the shoulder joint stretches the external moment arm at the shoulder. The farther the bar drifts in front of the mid-foot and the joint, the longer that lever, and the larger the external moment your deltoids must fight. A forward-drifting bar path is, quite literally, a self-inflicted increase in the load your shoulders feel.

Evangelista and colleagues (2025) formalized this by modeling both the bench and overhead press as a kinematic chain of rigid links, with single- and two-joint muscles constrained by the barbell, and deriving the equations of motion in closed form. Their model reproduces the origin of the sticking region and — importantly — quantifies how shoulder and elbow torques trade contribution across the phases of the lift, all shaped by the barbell constraint. It is a satisfying confirmation that the sticking point is not a mysterious failure of will but a predictable output of geometry: give a solver the link lengths and the bar's path, and the stall falls out of the math.

Grip width: trading moment arm against range

If bar path controls the external moment arm at the shoulder, grip width controls how the demand is distributed between the shoulder and the elbow — and how much horizontal force the arms have to manage. This is where the bench press becomes a clean natural experiment, and Larsen and colleagues (2021) ran it: fourteen trained males performed 1-RM bench presses at wide, medium, and narrow grips while the researchers tracked kinematics, horizontal kinetics, and muscle activity through the sticking region.

The results map cleanly onto our moment-arm logic. A wider grip shortens the vertical distance the bar must travel — less range — and orients the upper arm more out to the side, which as we saw maximizes the pectoralis and middle-deltoid demand while shortening the elbow's effective contribution. A narrower grip lengthens the range, tucks the elbows, and shifts more of the work onto the triceps. Larsen et al. (2021) found that triceps medialis activity was greater with medium and narrow grips — the elbow extensors doing more as their share of the lever grows. Wide and medium grips produced greater horizontal shoulder moments than the narrow grip in the sticking region, and — the practical bottom line — wide and medium grips allowed more load to be lifted than narrow grip.

But there is a cost hiding in the kinetics. The wide grip generated substantial lateral forces during the sticking region — on the order of 13 to 15% of the load pushing outward — where medium and narrow grips produced more medially directed or balanced forces. That out-to-the-side arm position that maximizes leverage is also the position that loads the shoulder's soft-tissue restraints hardest. This is the recurring theme of the whole chapter made concrete: the shoulder's mobility lets you chase leverage by opening the arm wide, but every degree of that trade spends stability you can only get back from tissue and coordination.

Think About It

Two lifters bench the same load. One uses a wide grip, one narrow. Which one is asking more of the triceps, and which is asking more of the pectoralis and the shoulder's passive restraints? Frame each answer in terms of moment arm and range — not preference.

Notice what "honest range" (Chapter 2) means here. Because the glenohumeral joint gives up stability for mobility, its usable range in a press is not a fixed anatomical fact — it is heavily dependent on soft tissue and on how well the scapula is steering the socket. A range that is safe and strong for one lifter's coordinated, well-positioned shoulder may be provocative for another's. When a grip width, a bar path, or an elevation angle produces pain, a pinching sensation, or a feeling of the joint slipping, that is not a cue to grind harder. It is information. In the framing of Kibler et al. (2013), it may reflect an impairment in how the shoulder is functioning — and it warrants evaluation by a qualified professional, not a heavier set. Our job as an applied working model is to explain the mechanics clearly enough that you can recognize when the mechanics are telling you to stop and ask.

Putting the system back together

Step back and the shoulder resolves into a coherent picture. It is the most mobile joint precisely because it is the least bony-stable, which forces it to be the most negotiated — the scapula continuously repositioning the socket (Inman et al., 1944; McQuade & Smidt, 1998; McClure et al., 2001) so the deltoid and its neighbors can work from favorable angles. The internal moment arms of those muscles rewrite themselves through the pressing arc (Hik & Ackland, 2018; Hughes et al., 1997), so the same deltoid that mostly compresses the joint at the bottom of a raise becomes the arm's dominant elevator at shoulder height. And the external moment arm — the demand — swings just as hard, peaking wherever the bar drifts farthest forward and producing the sticking region that maximal effort cannot smooth away (Elliott et al., 1989; Evangelista et al., 2025). Grip and bar path are the two dials you actually turn to redistribute all of it (Larsen et al., 2021).

You should now be able to look at any press — overhead, flat, incline, wide, narrow — and reason about it as a moment-arm problem in motion: where is the bar relative to the joint, how long is the external lever there, which muscle's line of pull is favorable at that angle, and where, therefore, will the lift be hardest. That is the entire toolkit. The only element we have deliberately left underdeveloped is the shoulder blade itself — the quiet partner steering the socket through every rep above.

Key Takeaways

  • The shoulder is a multi-joint system: the glenohumeral ball-and-socket rotates the arm while the scapula rotates and tilts to keep the socket positioned under it (scapulohumeral rhythm).
  • The classic 2:1 rhythm is an average, not a law — it shifts with joint angle and with load (McQuade & Smidt, 1998), and scapular motion is three-dimensional (McClure et al., 2001).
  • A muscle's internal moment arm changes through the pressing arc. The deltoid mostly compresses the joint at low elevation and becomes the dominant elevator near shoulder height (Hik & Ackland, 2018; Hughes et al., 1997).
  • The sticking point is a geometry problem, not an effort problem: EMG of prime movers stays high while the mechanical position temporarily worsens (Elliott et al., 1989).
  • A forward-drifting bar path lengthens the external moment arm at the shoulder and increases demand; a tight, stacked path minimizes it.
  • Grip width trades moment arm against range and redistributes work between pectoralis/deltoid and triceps — while a wide grip also raises lateral force on the shoulder (Larsen et al., 2021).
  • Because mobility is bought with instability, pressing pain, pinching, or slipping is a signal warranting professional evaluation — never something to push through.
Looking Ahead

We kept the scapula in the wings this chapter, invoking it whenever the socket needed steering but never letting it take the stage. In Chapter 8 that changes. Pulling movements — rows, pull-downs, deadlift lockouts — put the shoulder blade center stage, because pulling is the scapula's job. We'll trace how upward and downward rotation, protraction and retraction, turn the blade from a passive base into the prime mover, and why a pull is only as strong as the scapular control underneath it.
