Part A · The Five-Move Read

The chapter names five moves, run in sequence, that let you read any lift by eye, plus a related question they set up: where the likely sticking point falls. Answer in full sentences.

  1. In your own words, state what each move asks you to look at: (1) identify the pattern, (2) locate the load's gravity line relative to each joint, (3) find where the external moment arm peaks, (4) trace the best-positioned muscles, (5) check the read against proportions and honest ranges of motion. Then explain, with one specific example, why starting at move three and skipping move one produces an unreliable read.
  2. Explain what a likely sticking point is, mechanically, and why it is not a fixed location stamped onto every performance of a lift.
  3. Explain, in your own words, what inverse dynamics names, and what a laboratory has (motion capture, force plates, a segment model) that your eye reading a lift on a gym floor does not. What does your eye still get right, even without those instruments?

Part B · One Framework, Nine Chapters

Each move draws on earlier material in this course. Connect them explicitly.

  1. Explain how identifying a lift's pattern (move one) depends on recognizing joint actions and lever classes from earlier chapters. Give one example of a lift whose pattern would be easy to misname without that background.
  2. Explain how tracing the best-positioned muscles (move four) depends on muscle architecture and line of pull. Why can a muscle that looks large on an anatomy chart still be the wrong answer for a given joint angle?
  3. Explain why checking the read against proportions (move five) requires honest ranges of motion and anthropometry together, and describe what could go wrong if you checked only one of the two.
  4. The chapter treats torque and the moment arm as the currency the whole method is priced in. Explain what this means in your own words, and why a lift's name (squat, hinge, press, pull) is a starting classification rather than the actual answer to how demanding it is.

Part C · Run the Method on a Lift You Have Never Been Taught

Apply the five moves to the Zercher squat, a squat performed with the bar cradled in the crooks of the elbows and held tight against the ribs, rather than resting on the back or held at the front of the shoulders. Nothing in this course has named this lift directly.

  1. Pattern: classify the Zercher squat's joint pattern, and explain what does and does not change relative to a back squat.
  2. Gravity line: describe roughly where the bar's line of gravity falls relative to the hip, knee, and ankle when it is held at the elbow crease against the ribs, and how that differs from a bar resting higher on the back.
  3. Moment arm and likely sticking point: predict where the external moment arm is likely to peak, and identify the phase of the lift where you would expect the sticking point to fall. Explain your reasoning.
  4. Muscles: name the muscles best positioned to meet the demand you identified, including any demand this bar position creates that a back squat does not.
  5. Proportion check: explain how a lifter with notably long forearms, or one with a deep chest, might experience this lift differently even at an identical depth and load.

Part D · Peak Demand Migrates

Demand is rarely fixed to one spot in a lift. Use the chapter's hip thrust, squat, and press examples to reason through the following.

  1. In a barbell hip thrust, hip extensor demand is highest early in the movement and decreases toward lockout, which surprises most beginning readers. Explain the moment arm geometry that produces this pattern.
  2. In a squat, trunk inclination and tibia inclination have opposite effects on the knee moment. Explain what happens if you read only one of the two variables, and describe a case where they could combine to produce a similar knee moment in two lifters whose squats look visibly different.
  3. Explain why a press's sticking region is a geometric event rather than a fixed point on the bar path, and explain how changing grip width could relocate it.

Part E · Mechanically-Expected Variation, or Something Else?

For each feature below, decide whether it reads as a mechanically expected variation or as something that warrants professional evaluation, and justify the call using the chapter's logic. There is no credit for a bare label; the justification is the answer.

FeatureYour callJustification
A lifter with a long femur and limited ankle dorsiflexion range of motion shows a pronounced forward lean at squat depth.  
A lifter's bar path drifts forward and their torso pitches only during the final repetition of a heavy set, as fatigue sets in.  
A lifter shows a sudden, one-sided shift in bar path partway through a press, along with new discomfort that was not present in earlier sets.  
A lifter using a wide stance shows their knees tracking outward over the toes throughout a squat, consistently, without discomfort.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds mechanical literacy, the ability to read where demand concentrates in a lift and why, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the load moves through the joints, and treat anything that looks clinical (new or worsening discomfort, sudden asymmetry, numbness, a change under load that cueing cannot resolve, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: an educator explains mechanics and refers out, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
