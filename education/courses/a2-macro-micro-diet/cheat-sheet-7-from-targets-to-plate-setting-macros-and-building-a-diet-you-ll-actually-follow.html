Chapter 7 : From Targets to Plate

Numbers to plate in four steps: size the goal, set macros in order, build a followable plate, and engineer adherence.

Step 1 : Size the Goal from Maintenance

Fat lossA deficit of about 15 to 25 percent below maintenance (often 300 to 600 kcal). Moderate defends lean mass; aggressive costs muscle, sleep, mood, and adherence.
MaintenanceEnergy-matched intake. The right call more often than people think: after a cut, in a stressful season, or when the real problem is consistency.
GainA modest surplus of about 5 to 15 percent (often 200 to 400 kcal). Bigger builds fat faster, not muscle faster.
  • The target is a starting hypothesis, not a verdict. Check it against the scale and mirror over two to four weeks, then adjust in small steps.

Step 2 : Macros in Priority Order

  • Protein first : about 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg (set in grams of bodyweight, not a percentage). Rises in a deficit; spares lean mass and drives satiety.
  • Fat to a floor : about 0.5 to 0.8 g/kg (roughly 20 to 30 percent of calories). A floor, not a target: below it you lose hormones, vitamin absorption, and flavor.
  • Carbohydrate = remainder : whatever calories are left. The flexible macro absorbs the day-to-day variation.
  • Exchange rate : protein and carbohydrate about 4 kcal/g, fat about 9 kcal/g.

The Remainder Math

  • Lock protein (g x 4) and fat floor (g x 9), then carbohydrate = leftover kcal / 4.
  • Example, 2,000 kcal at 75 kg: protein 135 g (540 kcal), fat 53 g (475 kcal), carbohydrate about 246 g.
  • Cut to 1,600 kcal: protein and fat hold, carbohydrate alone falls to about 146 g.
  • State every target as a range, not a fragile exact number. Labels and TDEE carry real error.

How the Person Tracks

  • Weigh and log : most precise, most demanding. For a time-limited phase or a few weeks of learning portions.
  • Hand portions : palm of protein, cupped hand of carbohydrate, thumb of fat, fist of vegetables. No equipment; right neighborhood, not exact address.
  • Habit-based : structural rules, no counting. Lowest friction, least feedback.
  • Rule : pick the least demanding method that still gives the feedback the goal needs. Consistency is the active ingredient.

Step 3 : Build the Plate

  • Protein anchors : a protein source at every meal (about 25 to 45 g each). Spreading it across meals may use it better than one big dinner.
  • Meal frequency : fit the schedule, not the dogma. Protein amount drives satiety; three versus six meals does not.
  • Satiety levers : fiber and water lower energy density (more food per calorie); fat is dense and easy to overeat. Favor foods you chew over liquid calories.
  • Room for enjoyment : reserve a deliberate slice for foods chosen for pleasure. This is the load-bearing wall of adherence.

Step 4 : Adherence Wins

  • Macro ratio barely predicts results; sticking to the plan does (Sacks 2009; Dansinger 2005).
  • Flexible control beats rigid: a deviation is a data point, not a blown day.
  • No clean foods, no dirty foods. Use "smart sources" and "balance," not "clean" and "cheat."
  • Design for the human who exists, then never blame the human for a plan that ignored their life.
Where education ends   This builds diet-design reasoning, not a license to diagnose, prescribe, or treat. A diagnosed disease, medication or lab management, or a possible eating disorder are signs that warrant a qualified professional (a physician, a registered dietitian, or a mental-health clinician). Work within your role, whatever it is.
