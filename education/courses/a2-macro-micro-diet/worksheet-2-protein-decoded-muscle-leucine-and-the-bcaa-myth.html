Part A · Map the Mechanism

Muscle is in constant turnover. Show that you can track the two sides of the ledger.

  1. Write the net-balance equation in words (what is subtracted from what), and explain what has to be true over days and weeks for muscle to be gained rather than lost.
  2. Protein has no dedicated storage depot the way carbohydrate and fat do. In two or three sentences, explain what the body draws on when protein intake falls short, and why that fact makes the dose and timing of protein matter more than they do for carbohydrate.
  3. The fasted state (overnight, between meals) is described as a small negative balance that is normal and reversible. Explain when that normal state turns into a real problem, and why the scale can hide it.

Part B · The Leucine Threshold

The dose-response of muscle protein synthesis (MPS) is closer to a threshold than to "more is always better."

  1. In the Moore (2009) and Witard (2014) studies, roughly what per-meal dose of quality protein maximally stimulated MPS in young men, and what happened to the protein above that dose? Explain in a sentence, not just a number.
  2. Explain the difference between leucine as a signal and the other amino acids as materials. Why does a protein's leucine content, not just its gram count, help decide whether a meal crosses the threshold?
  3. Older adults show anabolic resistance. State roughly how their per-meal protein and leucine target differs from a young adult's, and explain in your own words what "the doorbell gets harder to hear" means mechanically.

Part C · Spread It Across the Day

Use the Areta (2013) distribution logic.

  1. Areta gave the same 80 grams of protein in three patterns: two large boluses, four medium doses, or eight small ones. Say which pattern stimulated MPS best over 12 hours, and explain why the other two fell short (name the specific failure of each).
  2. An older adult eats 25 grams of protein as 5 g at breakfast, 5 g at lunch, and 15 g at dinner. Explain why this can stimulate MPS poorly all day even though the daily number "looks acceptable," and describe how you would reorganize the same food.
  3. The chapter says total daily protein is the heavyweight variable and distribution is the finishing move. Explain what it means to "polish a foundation that is not there," and who distribution matters most for.

Part D · Bust the BCAA Myth

Reason from the brick model, not from brand loyalty. Match each claim to whether it is true, and where the logic breaks.

The pitch, in three clauses A. Leucine triggers MPS   B. BCAA supplements contain leucine   C. Therefore BCAA supplements build muscle
  1. Which of clauses A and B are correct, and exactly why does conclusion C not follow? Use the idea that triggering a build and supplying the materials to finish it are two different jobs.
  2. A new muscle protein needs all nine essential amino acids. BCAAs supply three. Explain where the missing six come from when someone takes BCAAs on an empty stomach, and why that makes the result "a wash at best" (Wolfe, 2017).
  3. Someone already eats 130 grams of mixed, high-quality protein per day across four meals. Mechanistically, what does adding a 10-gram scoop of BCAA powder contribute that their food has not already delivered, leucine included? Give the honest verdict and the sentence you would actually say.
  4. An EAA (all nine essential amino acids) blend is mechanistically more defensible than a BCAA blend. Explain why, and then explain why it is still usually redundant for a healthy person who eats enough complete protein.

Part F · Set a Defensible Target

Turn the mechanism into numbers. Show your arithmetic.

  1. A 70-kilogram, 64-year-old wants to preserve muscle while losing a little fat. Choose a defensible g/kg target for their age and deficit, calculate the daily grams, divide across four meals, and confirm each meal clears the older-adult threshold. Show every step.
  2. Explain why the old RDA of 0.8 g/kg is called a "floor" rather than a target, and why the daily range (~1.6 to 2.2 g/kg) and the per-meal threshold "point to the same plate."

Where education ends
This worksheet builds nutrition literacy, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe a diet for a medical condition, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how protein physiology works, and treat anything that looks clinical (kidney or liver disease, an eating disorder, unexplained muscle loss or weakness, or medically supervised restriction) as warranting evaluation by a qualified professional. Work within your role: a coach or trainer educates and refers, a registered dietitian or physician manages medical nutrition, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
