Part A · The Two Numbers People Confuse

Glycemic index and glycemic load sound alike and answer different questions. Show that you can keep them apart.

  1. In one or two sentences each, state what the glycemic index (GI) measures and what the glycemic load (GL) measures. Name the one variable GL adds that GI holds constant.
  2. Watermelon has a GI near 76, and a 120 gram serving carries about 6 grams of available carbohydrate. Using GL = (GI × grams of available carbohydrate) ÷ 100, compute its glycemic load and say, in a sentence, whether that lands low, medium, or high.
  3. A slice of white bread has a similar GI but roughly 14 grams of available carbohydrate. Explain in your own words why its glycemic load comes out two to three times higher than watermelon's despite the near-identical index.

Part C · Fiber, the Part You Do Not Digest

  1. Distinguish soluble from insoluble fiber by what each one physically does in the gut, and give one food example of each.
  2. When gut bacteria ferment certain fibers they produce short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate. Explain, in a sentence or two, one job those short-chain fatty acids do and why "eat a variety of plants" beats "take a fiber supplement."
  3. The chapter calls fiber the highest-leverage carbohydrate lever. State the daily fiber range where the outcome benefits cluster, and explain why ramping fiber gradually with water matters.

Part D · Carbohydrate as Fuel

Open the two glycogen tanks and reason about when timing is a real lever.

  1. The body stores glucose as glycogen in two depots. Contrast the liver tank and the muscle tank by what each one feeds, and explain why your muscle cannot lend its glycogen to your blood.
  2. Roughly how long can a typical adult's muscle glycogen sustain hard continuous endurance work, and why does that number mean a single lifting session or a 30 minute jog does not seriously empty the tank?
  3. Define carbohydrate availability in a sentence, and use it to explain why timing genuinely matters for an endurance cyclist three hours into a ride but is a rounding error for a desk worker before a 20 minute walk.

Part E · Refined vs Whole, and the Priority Order

  1. A kernel of wheat has bran, germ, and endosperm. Explain at the mechanism level what refining removes and why finely milled starch produces a taller glucose curve than the intact grain, from the identical grams of carbohydrate.
  2. The chapter argues the popular conversation has the priority order almost inverted. List the three priorities in the correct order for a general learner, and name which one carries the strongest outcome evidence.
  3. Someone says a birthday cake is a moral failure. Rewrite that as a structural statement, without shame, that still keeps the daily pattern honest.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds carbohydrate literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the numbers, the fiber, and the fuel work, and treat anything that looks clinical (a diabetes diagnosis, medication or insulin dosing, unexplained weight loss, constant thirst, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: an educator refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
