Part B · Set the Targets for Real People

Population defaults are the price of admission, not the goal. For each person, set a reasonable starting protein target and explain the reasoning. Give the numbers and the "why," never a bare figure.

  1. A sedentary 70 kg adult. What is a reasonable starting protein target, and why is the RDA a floor rather than a target here?
  2. A 70 kg person who lifts hard four mornings a week but is not a competitive athlete. Give a protein range and explain how you would scale carbohydrate to their actual workload rather than to how "athletic" they feel.
  3. Fat and hydration do not disappear as training rises. State the practical fat floor from the chapter, and explain why fat should not be crushed to make room for performance carbohydrate.

Part C · Life Stage Sharpens Old Levers

Life stage does not add brand-new rules. It takes levers you already know and gives them sharper teeth.

  1. Define anabolic resistance and the leucine threshold, and explain why the same meal that maximizes a 25-year-old's response can leave a 75-year-old's response submaximal.
  2. An older person eats toast, soup, and a large protein-rich dinner, totaling 1.0 g/kg of protein for the day. The daily total looks acceptable. Explain why the distribution may still be failing their muscle, and name the cheapest change you could make.
  3. Why does pregnancy narrow the general-education lane, and what does responsible support of an already-prescribed clinical plan look like?

Part D · Pattern Changes the Route, Not the Goal

An omnivorous, a vegetarian, and a vegan pattern can all be excellent. Pattern does not change the goals; it changes which levers you pull first, because it changes the bioavailability math.

  1. Name the plant-based pressure-point nutrients, and mark the one that "just eat a varied diet" genuinely cannot solve. Explain why it is a hard requirement rather than a preference.
  2. Give two changes to how someone eats iron-containing plant foods (not what they eat) that raise the iron actually absorbed, and name the mechanism each one works through.
  3. Protein quality is not just grams. Explain why "120 grams of protein" is a slightly different instruction for a plant-based eater than for an omnivore, referencing digestibility (DIAAS/PDCAAS) and complementary proteins.

Part E · The Life the Plan Has to Fit

The best plan is the best one a person will actually execute in the life they really live. Constraints are design inputs, not problems to argue away.

  1. Religious or scheduled fasting (for example, Ramadan) compresses the day's intake into a smaller window. Name the two levers the evidence says are most likely to slip, and explain how you would route the same protein total through two meals instead of four.
  2. Explain why a "B-plus" plan followed for a year usually beats an "A-plus" plan abandoned in three weeks. Tie your answer to how body-composition and health adaptations accumulate.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds nutrition literacy and the judgment to individualize a general framework, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the levers work and adjust food for healthy people, and treat the off-ramps as clinician territory: diagnosed disease requiring medical nutrition therapy, clinical management of pregnancy, any sign of disordered eating, and interpretation of lab values. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
