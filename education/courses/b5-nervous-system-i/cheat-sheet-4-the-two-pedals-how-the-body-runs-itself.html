Chapter 4 : The Two Pedals

One control layer, two branches on the same organs: sympathetic (mobilize) and parasympathetic (restore), blended, not switched.

Organ by Organ: The Two Pedals

HeartSympathetic: faster, harder beat (positive chronotropy and inotropy). Parasympathetic: slower beat, via vagal brake on the sinoatrial node.
AirwaysSympathetic: bronchi relax, airways widen (mostly via circulating epinephrine). Parasympathetic: airways narrow toward baseline.
PupilsSympathetic: dilate (radial muscle). Parasympathetic: constrict (circular muscle).
Gut / digestionSympathetic: motility and secretion drop, blood flow redirected away. Parasympathetic: motility and secretion resume, blood flow returns.
Blood vesselsSympathetic: narrows vessels to gut and skin, widens vessels to working skeletal muscle. Vessels are mostly sympathetic-only, no strong parasympathetic counterpart.
Sweat glandsSympathetic only, and the one common exception: this pathway is cholinergic (acetylcholine), not noradrenergic.

The Two-Neuron Chain

  • Preganglionic neuron: brainstem or spinal cord to a ganglion.
  • Postganglionic neuron: ganglion to the organ.
  • Sympathetic: short preganglionic, ganglia in a chain near the spine, long postganglionic fanning out broadly. Thoracolumbar outflow.
  • Parasympathetic: long preganglionic (largely via the vagus nerve), ganglion near or inside the organ, short postganglionic. Craniosacral outflow.

The Chemistry

  • Acetylcholine: released by preganglionic neurons of both branches at the ganglion (nicotinic receptors).
  • Acetylcholine: also the parasympathetic branch's final signal at the organ (muscarinic receptors).
  • Norepinephrine: the sympathetic branch's final signal at most organs (adrenergic receptors).
  • Adrenal medulla: a modified sympathetic ganglion; releases mostly epinephrine, plus some norepinephrine, straight into the blood. A hormonal amplifier that broadcasts body-wide and lingers after the nerves go quiet.

Dynamic Balance, Not a Switch

  • Tone: both branches maintain constant baseline activity. Rest is an active command, not the absence of one.
  • Resting heart rate (about 60 to 70) sits below the heart's own pacemaker rate (about 100 to 110) because the parasympathetic brake is continuously applied.
  • Dual innervation: most organs receive both inputs, and the organ's state is the running balance between them.
  • Heart rate variability (HRV): beat-to-beat fluctuation; higher, more flexible variability reflects a well-regulated system, not weakness.
  • Chronic imbalance and lost flexibility, not sympathetic activity itself, are what research links to elevated risk.

Reading a Whole-Body Pattern

  • Sympathetic pattern: heart rate up, airways open, pupils wide, gut paused, sweat glands active, blood shifted toward muscle.
  • Parasympathetic pattern: heart rate down, airways at baseline, pupils constricted, gut active, sweat low, blood returned to digestive organs.
  • A perceived threat (public speaking, a tense meeting) can trigger the full sympathetic pattern with no physical danger present.
  • The organs never change identity. Only the balance between the two branches changes.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how the autonomic nervous system works, not a license to diagnose or treat. A heart rate that will not settle, fainting, chest pain, or racing episodes with no clear trigger are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
