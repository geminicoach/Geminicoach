Cheat-Sheet · B5 Nervous System I

Chapter 1 : The Spark That Starts Everything

One cell, one repeated trick: charge up, sum inputs, fire a fixed spike, encode strength in rate.

The Neuron in Five Moves

1. InputBranching dendrites receive signals from many other neurons at once.
2. IntegrateThe soma (cell body) sums excitatory and inhibitory input continuously.
3. ThresholdAt the axon initial segment, if the summed signal crosses about negative 55 millivolts (mV), a spike fires.
4. OutputThe action potential travels down the axon at a fixed, all-or-nothing size to the axon terminals.
5. ResetThe refractory period briefly blocks re-firing, enforcing one-way travel and a maximum firing rate.

Resting Membrane Potential

  • ~-70 mV : the resting charge, inside negative relative to outside.
  • Sodium (Na+) : concentrated outside the cell.
  • Potassium (K+) : concentrated inside the cell; its outward leak sets the baseline.
  • Sodium-potassium pump : spends energy to push 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ in, maintaining the gradients.

The Action Potential

  • Threshold : about -55 mV; the tipping point that opens voltage-gated sodium channels.
  • Depolarization : sodium floods in, positive feedback drives voltage up to about +40 mV.
  • Repolarization : sodium channels inactivate, potassium channels open, voltage falls back down.
  • All-or-nothing : once threshold is crossed, every spike is the same size, no matter how strong the trigger.

Refractory Period

  • Absolute refractory : sodium channels are inactivated; the neuron cannot fire again no matter what.
  • Relative refractory : firing is possible again but needs an unusually strong push.
  • Job 1: forces the signal to travel only forward, never backward.
  • Job 2: caps the maximum rate a neuron can fire.

Myelin & Speed

  • Myelin : fatty insulation from glial cells, wrapped in segments along the axon.
  • Nodes of Ranvier : the small bare gaps between myelin segments.
  • Saltatory conduction : the spike leaps node to node instead of crawling continuously.
  • Unmyelinated: roughly 0.5 to 10 meters per second. Myelinated: up to roughly 150 meters per second.

Frequency Coding: The Answer to the Puzzle

  • Every spike is identical in size, so strength cannot be written in spike amplitude.
  • Frequency coding (rate coding) : stimulus intensity is written in how often a neuron fires, not how big any one spike is.
  • A light touch produces a slow trickle of spikes; a hard press produces a rapid, sustained barrage, at the same fixed spike size.
  • Population coding : more intense stimuli also recruit more sensory fibers firing at once.
  • The central principle: neural signals are digital in strength, all-or-nothing, but graded in frequency.
