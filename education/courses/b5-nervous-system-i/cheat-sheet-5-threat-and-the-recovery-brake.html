Chapter 5 : Threat and the Recovery Brake

One threat, two waves (fast nerve, slow hormone), and an active brake that brings the body home.

The Cascade, Step by Step

1. AppraisalA limbic-prefrontal network (amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex) decides whether to fire the cascade; driven by perceived threat, not only objective danger.
2. Fast waveSympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) axis: sympathetic nerves plus the adrenal medulla release adrenaline and noradrenaline within seconds; mobilizes the body for action.
3. Slow waveHypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis: hypothalamus to pituitary to adrenal cortex, ending in cortisol; peaks in about 15 to 30 minutes, lingers over an hour; sustains and helps end the response.
4. RecoveryThe parasympathetic brake, carried chiefly by the vagus nerve, actively re-engages: restores vagal tone, slows the heart, and quiets sympathetic outflow.

Key Terms

  • SAM axis : sympathetic nerves plus adrenal medulla; fast, seconds to peak.
  • HPA axis : hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal cortex; slow, minutes to an hour; ends in cortisol.
  • Vagus nerve : the tenth cranial nerve; the main parasympathetic brake line to heart, lungs, and gut.
  • HRV : heart rate variability; beat-to-beat variation used as an index of vagal brake strength.
  • Allostasis : stability through change (McEwen); how the body varies internal settings to meet demand.
  • Allostatic load : the cumulative cost of a response that fails to habituate or shut off.

Four Routes to Allostatic Load

  • Too many stressors : repeated demands stack up.
  • Failure to habituate : same trigger, no dampening over time.
  • Failure to shut off : the response outlasts the threat.
  • Inadequate response : one system under, another compensates and overworks.

Recovery and Flexibility

  • Resting heart rate is not passive: vagal tone actively holds it down below the heart's own intrinsic pace.
  • Autonomic flexibility: fast, appropriate shifting between mobilization and recovery is the real marker of a resilient system.
  • A chronically activated system loses range: it gets stuck high, HRV blunts, and the resting baseline creeps upward over time.

The Voluntary Handle: Slow Exhalation

  • Vagal activity surges on exhalation and eases on inhalation (respiratory sinus arrhythmia).
  • Slow, exhale-weighted breathing is a manual lever on the parasympathetic brake, a working-model principle, not a clinical protocol.
  • It can ease the fast sympathetic wave; it does not clear the slower cortisol tide on demand.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of the stress response and the recovery brake, not a license to diagnose or treat. A racing heart or palpitations without a clear trigger, chest tightness, shortness of breath, or a wired feeling that never resolves are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
