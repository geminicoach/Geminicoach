Chapter 2: Reason Through a Real Complaint

Take the map of the nervous system and use it to explain a real, everyday complaint, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 20 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Delia, 34
Consider Delia, who spent a long video call sitting cross-legged on the floor. When she finally stood up, her foot felt strange: first numb, then a rush of tingling, and for a few seconds her foot would not quite obey her, landing a little clumsily before it settled back to normal within a minute or so. It has happened before, always after sitting the same way for a while, and it always resolves completely once she moves around. Delia wonders: "What was actually happening in my foot just then, and should I be worried that it does not respond right away?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, what is happening in Delia's foot using the central nervous system (CNS) / peripheral nervous system (PNS) map and the afferent / efferent vocabulary from the chapter. Be specific about which territory and which kind of traffic is involved.
  2. Explain why a single compressed nerve can produce both the tingling (a sensory, afferent effect) and the momentary clumsiness (a motor, efferent effect) at the same time, using the mixed nerve concept from the chapter.
  3. Decide whether this reads as normal, transient nerve compression or something that needs clinical evaluation, and say how you know.
  4. Give one practical, general-education suggestion, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Your Response

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Correctly locates the sensation in the peripheral nervous system (PNS), not the central nervous system (CNS), and explains why.
  • Explains that the affected nerve is a mixed nerve carrying both afferent and efferent fibers together, so one compression event affects both incoming sensation and outgoing motor control in that body region.
  • Frames brief, position-related tingling and clumsiness that fully resolves as normal physiology, not a disorder.
  • Offers a practical, general-education suggestion within scope (e.g., changing position periodically).
  • Identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose.
