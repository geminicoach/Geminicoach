Class 6 · Module 2 — The Sensing Side

Listening from the Inside

Transduction, adaptation, and the quiet river of signals your body sends about itself.

60 min read 8 cited sources

Sit still for a moment and try to feel your heartbeat without touching your wrist or your chest. No pulse-taking — just listen. Some of you will sense a steady thud almost immediately. Others will strain and catch nothing at all, or perhaps mistake the tick of a clock or the pressure of your own attention for a beat. Now try something easier: notice the chair pressing against your back. There it is, obvious — except that a second ago, before I mentioned it, you were not feeling it at all, even though the pressure never changed.

These two small experiments open the door to this chapter. One reveals how strangely dim our sense of our own insides can be. The other reveals that even a strong, constant signal simply disappears from awareness. Both are clues to how the nervous system decides what is worth reporting — and both reframe what "sensation" actually is.

Turning the Arrows Around

For the last several chapters we followed signals outward. A decision became a command, a command became a muscle contraction, an autonomic setpoint became a change in heart rate. We traced the efferent half of the map from Chapter 2 — the pathways that carry orders from center to periphery. Now we turn every arrow around and follow the far larger flow moving the other way: the afferent half, the news arriving from the body and the world.

The volume of that inbound traffic is staggering. At every instant your skin, eyes, ears, joints, gut, blood vessels, and airways are generating streams of signals. The nervous system's problem is not scarcity of information; it is a flood. Most of this reading is about two elegant solutions to that flood — transduction, the universal trick for getting a physical event into the neural language of Chapter 1, and adaptation, the trick for ignoring almost all of it so the important parts can be heard.

Then we make a turn that reorganizes the whole picture. Instead of marching through the classic five senses, we split sensation along a different seam: the difference between sensing the world outside you (exteroception) and sensing the state of the body that is you (interoception). That second sense — the one you were straining to use when you hunted for your heartbeat — turns out to be the raw material of emotion, effort, and the plain feeling of being alive. It is also, as we will see, the sensory partner to the autonomic and stress systems we built in Chapters 4 and 5. You cannot pump a brake you cannot feel.

Think About It

In the outward-signaling chapters, a "command" ended at a muscle or gland. Where does an afferent signal end? Hold that question — the answer is less obvious than it sounds, and it drives the second half of this chapter.

The Universal Grammar of Sensation: Transduction

Here is a fact that sounds trivial but is not: your brain has never touched the world. It sits in the dark, in fluid, inside bone. Light, sound pressure, warmth, the stretch of a full stomach — none of these physical things enter the skull. What enters is only ever one currency: the action potentials of Chapter 1. Every sensory experience you have ever had was assembled from trains of these identical electrical spikes.

The process that makes this possible is transduction — the conversion of a physical stimulus into a change in a neuron's membrane potential, and ultimately into a pattern of action potentials. A sensory receptor is, at bottom, a specialized transducer: a cell (or a nerve ending) built to be exquisitely sensitive to one form of energy and to translate it into the only thing neurons can pass along.

A worked example: pressure on the skin

Consider what happens when you press a fingertip against the edge of this device. The physical event is mechanical — a deformation of the skin. Buried in that skin are mechanoreceptors whose membranes contain mechanically gated ion channels. When the membrane is stretched or indented, these channels physically pop open. Among the molecular actors here are the Piezo channels, whose discovery clarified how a bending force becomes an electrical event (Hao et al., 2014). Open channels let positive ions rush in; the local membrane potential shifts toward threshold. This graded shift is the receptor potential.

Crucially, the receptor potential is not yet an action potential. It is a smooth, analog voltage change whose size scales with the strength of the stimulus — press harder, and more channels open, and the receptor potential grows. But the axon that carries the message to the spinal cord speaks only in all-or-nothing spikes. So the graded receptor potential is encoded: a stronger stimulus produces a larger receptor potential, which produces a higher frequency of action potentials. This is the heart of sensory coding. Intensity is written in rate — spikes per second — not in the size of any single spike (Hao et al., 2014).

The same logic, in different molecular clothing, plays out everywhere. In the retina, photons trigger a chemical cascade that changes membrane voltage. In the cochlea, sound-driven fluid motion bends hair cells and opens mechanical channels. In the gut wall, stretch from a filling stomach opens stretch-sensitive channels on visceral afferents. Different energies, different receptors, one destination language. If you understand transduction as "physical stimulus → receptor potential → frequency of spikes," you understand the grammar shared by every sense you have.

Think About It

If every stimulus arrives as the same kind of spike, how does the brain ever know whether a signal means "light" or "warmth" or "a full stomach"? (Hint: it is not written in the spike. Think about which wire the spike arrives on.)

That hint deserves a name. Because all spikes look alike, the meaning of a sensory signal is carried largely by labeled lines — by which pathway a spike travels. Spikes arriving on the optic nerve are interpreted as light no matter what caused them, which is why pressing gently on a closed eye produces a spot of "light" that was never there. The brain reads the return address, not the message.

Adaptation: Why You Notice Change, Not Constancy

Return to that chair pressing on your back. The pressure was there the whole time, generating receptor potentials the whole time — yet it vanished from awareness within seconds. This is sensory adaptation: the tendency of receptors and neural pathways to reduce their response to an unchanging stimulus. Adaptation is not fatigue or failure. It is a strategy, and a brilliant one.

The logic is informational. A signal that never changes carries almost no new information. If a receptor fires at a constant rate to report "still pressing, still pressing, still pressing," it wastes energy and clogs the channel with redundancy. Far more useful is a system that shouts at the onset of a stimulus, quiets down when the stimulus proves boring and constant, and then shouts again the moment anything changes. Wark and colleagues (2007) frame adaptation precisely this way: as a mechanism for efficient neural coding, in which the nervous system continually retunes itself to the current statistics of its inputs, spending its limited bandwidth on what is new rather than on what is settled.

This is why adaptation operates across an enormous range of timescales — from milliseconds in individual channels to seconds and minutes in whole pathways (Wark et al., 2007). Your eyes adapt to a dark room over many minutes; a single touch receptor stops responding to steady pressure in under a second. Different jobs, same principle: track the change, discard the constant.

Fast and slow: two ways to report a stimulus

Not all receptors adapt at the same rate, and the difference is functional. Fast-adapting (or phasic) receptors respond briskly to the onset and offset of a stimulus and fall silent in between — they are change-detectors, ideal for vibration and texture. Slow-adapting (or tonic) receptors keep firing throughout a sustained stimulus, though at a declining rate — they are better for reporting an ongoing state, like the position of a joint or a steady pressure you need to keep tracking (Hao et al., 2014).

You have a felt example of both right now. A fast-adapting population is why you feel the instant you put on a wristwatch and then forget it entirely; the "on" is loud, the "still on" is silent. A slow-adapting population is why, if you consciously attend, you can still recover a faint ongoing sense of the watch's weight. Hold onto this idea of a system tuned to change and blind to constancy. It will explain, in the second half of this chapter, one of the most consequential features of the human mind — that a body humming along in a steady state generates almost no felt signal, which is exactly why so many people are strangers to their own interiors.

Two Directions of Sensing: Exteroception and Interoception

Now the reorganizing move. The textbook tradition sorts sensation by modality — vision, hearing, touch, taste, smell. Useful, but it hides a deeper split that the modern literature treats as fundamental: the difference in direction. Some senses point outward, at the world. Others point inward, at the body itself.

Exteroception is sensing the external environment: the light bouncing off this page, the sound of a room, an object under your fingers. These are the senses we usually mean by "the five senses," and they are built for high spatial and temporal resolution — you can localize a touch to a millimeter and hear a click within milliseconds. Exteroception answers the question what is out there?

Interoception answers a different question: what is going on in here? It is the sense of the internal physiological condition of the body — heart rate, breathing, gut fullness and motility, temperature, blood chemistry, the burn of muscles, the urge to breathe, the pang of hunger or thirst. Chen and colleagues (2021), in an NIH-coordinated overview of the field, treat this as a distinct sensory domain with its own receptors, its own ascending pathways, and its own central destinations — and they draw a sharp line between it and exteroception.

Craig's reframing: interoception as the whole physiological condition of the body

For much of the twentieth century, "interoception" meant little more than crude visceral sensation — the dull ache of a distended organ. The influential reframing came from A. D. Craig (2002), whose landmark review redefined interoception as the sense of the entire physiological condition of the body: not just gut and bladder, but temperature, pain, itch, muscle and joint state, hunger, thirst, air hunger — the full readout of the flesh. Craig proposed that this afferent representation is the substrate of subjective feelings and, ultimately, of self-awareness. He called the body-as-sensed "the material me."

Craig also gave the reframing an anatomical spine. He identified a lamina I spinothalamocortical pathway — a dedicated line carrying signals about the body's condition up through the spinal cord and thalamus to the cortex — as the structural foundation of interoception (Craig, 2002). The point that matters for our working model is not the Latin. It is that interoception is not a vague afterthought hanging off the "real" senses. It is a full-fledged afferent system with its own wiring, and its cortical destination is telling.

The Vagus and the Ascending Route

Where does interoceptive news travel? A great deal of it rides the vagus nerve — the same nerve we met in Chapter 4 as the highway of the parasympathetic "rest and digest" system. But there is a fact about the vagus that most people, even people who invoke it constantly, get backwards. The vagus is not mainly a command line running down to the organs. It is overwhelmingly a sensory nerve: roughly 80% of its fibers are afferent, carrying signals up from the heart, lungs, and gut to the brain (Canna et al., 2020).

This single statistic reorganizes the autonomic story. In Chapter 4 we treated the vagus as the parasympathetic brake — an outbound cable. That is real, but it is the minority traffic. The dominant flow is the reverse: the body constantly telling the brain how it is doing. The vagus is first and foremost the nervous system's chief interoceptive conduit (Canna et al., 2020).

These afferents arrive first at the nucleus tractus solitarii (the NTS) in the medulla — a brainstem hub that is, in effect, the reception desk for visceral news. From there, signals relay through the parabrachial nucleus and thalamus, then upward to the hypothalamus, the insular cortex, and the anterior cingulate cortex (Chen et al., 2021). The insula deserves special attention: it is where the many strands of bodily signal are integrated into a unified sense of the body's condition — the neural home, if there is one, of Craig's "material me" (Craig, 2002). Notice that this destination is not a motor area and not a warehouse. It is where feeling gets built.

Think About It

We spent Chapter 5 on the recovery brake — the parasympathetic shift that ends a stress response. If most of the vagus is afferent, what does that imply about your ability to deliberately engage recovery? Can you steer a system whose current state you can barely perceive?

That question is not rhetorical. It is the practical hinge of the whole module. Recovery, self-regulation, the down-regulation of a stress response — all of them depend on the brain having an accurate read of the body's state. If the afferent report is faint or distorted, regulation is operating half-blind. This is why the sensing chapter had to come after the stress chapters, not before: sensing is the closing of a loop we left open.

Why Inner Signals Are Vague — and the Brain Predicts

Here is where interoception gets genuinely strange, and genuinely important. Exteroceptive senses are sharp; interoceptive ones are often maddeningly blurry. Most people cannot reliably report their own heart rate from feeling alone. Hunger, anxiety, fatigue, and even the urge to urinate can be confidently misidentified. Why is the sense that matters most for emotion so bad?

Part of the answer is adaptation, which we can now cash in. A healthy body in a steady state — heart beating regularly, breath even, temperature stable — is a constant stimulus. And we have already seen what happens to constant stimuli: receptors adapt, and the signal fades from awareness (Wark et al., 2007). The very homeostasis that keeps you alive also makes your interior quiet. You feel your heart when it changes — pounding after stairs, skipping in fear — far more than when it ticks along.

But there is a deeper answer, and it is the current frontier of the field. Anil Seth (2013) proposed that emotional feelings do not arise from a direct "readout" of bodily signals at all. Instead, the brain runs a predictive model of what its body should be doing, generates expectations, and compares those expectations against the noisy afferent stream. What you feel is the brain's best inference about the causes of its interoceptive signals — not the signals themselves. Seth called this interoceptive inference.

This idea, extended with Karl Friston into a framework of active inference (Seth & Friston, 2016), flips the classic model of perception. The brain is not a passive receiver waiting to be told how the body feels. It is a prediction engine that is mostly generating expectations and only updating them when the incoming prediction error is large enough to matter. On this view, even autonomic reflexes are partly "enslaved" to descending predictions: the brain predicts a heart rate and the body is nudged to fulfill the prediction, with mismatch resolved either by revising the belief or by acting to change the body (Seth & Friston, 2016).

Two consequences follow that are worth sitting with. First, feeling is constructed, not received — which is why the same racing heart can be labeled "excitement" before a performance and "terror" during a panic attack. The bodily signal is ambiguous; the interpretation does the work. Second, when the predictive model is chronically miscalibrated — when the brain expects danger and reads every twinge as threat — you get the felt signature of conditions like anxiety, in which the interoceptive prediction error is resolved not by calming the body but by escalating the alarm (Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016).

Closing the Loop — and Why It's Trainable

Step back and look at the whole architecture of the course so far. Chapter 1 gave us the spike. Chapter 2 gave us the map with its two directions. Chapters 4 and 5 built the autonomic and stress systems as outbound regulation. This chapter supplies the missing return path: the afferent stream that tells the regulating brain what state the body is actually in. Sensing–deciding–acting is now a closed loop, not a one-way arrow.

And here is the encouraging part. Because feeling is a skill of interpretation as much as a fact of wiring, interoceptive ability can be improved. Lazzarelli and colleagues (2024), reviewing mind–body interventions, treat the capacity to detect, interpret, and integrate bodily signals as trainable — reachable through top-down attentional practices (mindful attention to breath and body) and bottom-up movement and expressive practices. Their central claim connects everything in this chapter to the module's larger aim: because interoception is the raw material of emotion, sharpening it improves emotion regulation. You regulate what you can feel. And what you can feel, you can learn to feel better.

This is also why the recovery story from Chapter 5 stays unfinished without this chapter. Engaging a parasympathetic brake is not only a matter of outbound signaling; it depends on an inbound sense of the body's rising and falling arousal. A person with clearer interoception has, in effect, a more accurate gauge on the dashboard — and a gauge you can read is a system you can steer.

Key Takeaways

  • Transduction converts any physical stimulus into a graded receptor potential, which is encoded as the frequency of action potentials — intensity lives in rate, and meaning lives in which labeled line the spike travels.
  • Adaptation is a coding strategy, not a failure: receptors quiet down to unchanging stimuli so the system spends its limited bandwidth reporting change. Fast-adapting receptors flag onset/offset; slow-adapting receptors track ongoing state.
  • Exteroception senses the outside world with high resolution; interoception senses the body's own physiological condition — heart, breath, gut, temperature, chemistry — and is the substrate of feeling and self-awareness (Craig, 2002).
  • The vagus nerve is ~80% afferent: its dominant traffic is bodily news traveling up to the NTS and on to the insula, making it the chief interoceptive conduit (Canna et al., 2020).
  • Interoceptive signals are often vague because a homeostatic body is a constant, adapted-away stimulus. The brain compensates by predicting — you feel its best inference about the body, not a raw readout (Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016).
  • You cannot engage a recovery brake you cannot feel. Interoceptive ability is trainable, and sharpening it improves emotion regulation (Lazzarelli et al., 2024).
Looking Ahead

Now that both directions of the loop are in place, Chapter 7 returns to the outbound side to build the motor system in earnest — and you will see immediately why it belongs after this chapter. Movement that is not guided by a continuous stream of sensory feedback is clumsy, drifting, and blind. The action we studied at the start of the module turns out to lean on the sensing we studied at the end of it.
