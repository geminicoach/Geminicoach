The Scenario

Scenario · Gideon, 29
Consider Gideon, who has taken up rock climbing seriously this year. He has noticed that when he first grabs a small hold and squeezes hard, his forearm and fingers visibly shake for a second or two before the grip steadies out and holds firm for the rest of the move. On easier, low-force holds, there is no shake at all. It only ever happens at the start of a hard, high-effort grip, and it always settles quickly on its own. Gideon wonders: "Is this shaking a sign that something is wrong with my nerves or my grip strength? Why does it only happen when I grip really hard?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, how Gideon's forearm produces enough force for a hard grip, using motor unit recruitment (the size principle) and rate coding from the chapter, and explain what determines whether a contraction feels rough or smooth.
  2. Account for why the same shake does not appear on lower-force holds, referencing the size principle and how many motor units a gentle grip actually requires.
  3. Decide whether this reads as normal physiology or something to be concerned about, and explain how you know, referencing the closed loop of proprioceptive feedback and reflex correction.
  4. Give one practical, general-education framing point, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains that meeting a high force demand recruits additional motor units, in order from small to large under the size principle, and drives already-active units to fire faster (rate coding).
  • Explains that force smoothness depends on how completely motor unit twitches fuse, unfused, lower-rate firing produces a rougher contraction while faster, fused firing (tetanus) produces a smooth, steady hold, and that proprioceptive feedback and reflex correction help the grip settle.
  • Explains why lower-force holds do not shake, referencing the size principle and the small number of already-fused, low-threshold motor units a gentle grip needs.
  • Frames the shake as a normal, expected feature of motor unit recruitment and rate coding under closed-loop control, not inherently a disorder.
  • Identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose.
