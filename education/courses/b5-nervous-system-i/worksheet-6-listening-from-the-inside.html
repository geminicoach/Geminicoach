Part A · Sensory Transduction

Transduction is the process that turns a physical stimulus into action potentials, the electrical spikes that are the only language the nervous system speaks.

  1. Using the worked example of pressure on the skin, describe the chain of events from a finger pressing on a table to a train of action potentials heading toward the spinal cord. Name the receptor potential and explain what it is.
  2. The receptor potential is graded (an analog voltage), but the nerve fiber only fires all-or-nothing spikes. Explain how stimulus intensity gets encoded once the message has to travel as spikes rather than as a smooth wave.
  3. If every action potential looks essentially identical no matter which receptor produced it, how does the brain ever know whether an incoming spike train means light, warmth, or a full stomach? Explain the idea of a labeled line in your own words, and give one everyday example of what happens when that system is tricked (for instance, pressing gently on a closed eyelid).

Part B · Sensory Adaptation

Adaptation is a coding strategy, not a malfunction: a system that reports change is more useful than one that reports the same unchanging fact forever.

  1. Explain, in mechanism terms rather than just definition, why a constant stimulus (like the chair pressing against you right now) tends to fade from awareness even though the physical stimulus itself never weakens.
  2. Distinguish fast adapting (phasic) receptors from slow adapting (tonic) receptors. Give one everyday example of each and explain why that receptor type is well suited to the job it does.
  3. Adaptation has both a benefit and a cost. Describe one situation where losing sensitivity to a constant stimulus is clearly useful, and one situation where that same tendency could be a genuine problem.

Part C · Exteroception vs. Interoception

Sensation can be sorted by direction: outward toward the world, or inward toward the body's own state.

  1. Define exteroception and interoception in your own words, and explain the difference in the kind of question each one answers.
  2. Craig (2002) reframed interoception as the sense of the entire physiological condition of the body, not just the gut. List three signals besides "stomach fullness" that count as interoceptive under this broader definition, and explain why each one qualifies.
  3. Explain why exteroception tends to be sharp and precise (you can localize a touch to within a millimeter) while interoception tends to be comparatively vague. What does this difference suggest about how each sense evolved to be used?

Part D · The Vagus, Interoception, and the Stress System

Connect this chapter back to the autonomic and stress systems covered earlier in the course.

  1. Most descriptions of the vagus nerve treat it as an outbound "calm down" switch. Explain what is incomplete about that picture, using the roughly eighty percent afferent figure from the chapter.
  2. Trace the ascending route in your own words: where do vagal and other visceral afferents first arrive in the brainstem, and where does that information ultimately get integrated into a unified sense of the body's condition?
  3. Explain, using the idea of a closed loop, why a person cannot reliably steer a recovery response (the parasympathetic brake) if their interoceptive sense of their own arousal is faint or inaccurate.

Part E · Why Interoceptive Signals Are Vague, and How the Brain Predicts

This is the strangest and most important idea in the chapter: what you feel is not a raw readout of the body, it is the brain's best guess.

  1. Explain, using adaptation, why a healthy body sitting in a stable state generates so little felt signal, even though the underlying physiology is active the whole time.
  2. Summarize Seth's concept of interoceptive inference in your own words. According to this model, where does a felt emotion actually come from, and why is it not simply a passive readout of the body?
  3. Garfinkel and colleagues (2015) separated interoceptive accuracy from interoceptive awareness. Explain the difference between these two, and explain why a person can be very confident about a bodily signal while being largely wrong about it.
  4. Apply this to a concrete case: the same racing heart can be labeled excitement before a performance and labeled dread during a panic. Using interoceptive inference, explain how the identical bodily signal ends up with two different felt meanings.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds an accurate model of how sensing works, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. A persistent or worrying bodily signal, a racing heart with no clear trigger, or a pattern that concerns you is not something this course can sort out from the outside. Treat any such signal as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
