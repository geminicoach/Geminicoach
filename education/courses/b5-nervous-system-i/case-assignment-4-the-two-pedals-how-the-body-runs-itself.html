Chapter 4: Reason Through a Real Pattern

Take the two-pedal model and use it to explain a real, everyday pattern, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Imani, 29
Consider Imani, who tracks her stress using a phone app paired with a wearable. On days when a tense moment arrives, most often a deadline announced mid-meeting, the app flags the rise in her heart rate and tells her she is "stuck in fight-or-flight," recommending breathing exercises to force her body back into a calm state as fast as possible. Imani has started to worry that any rise in her heart rate during the day is a sign that something is wrong with her nervous system. When she actually looks at her data, though, she notices a consistent pattern: her heart rate climbs quickly when the deadline is announced, stays elevated through the tense part of the meeting, and then settles back to its usual resting rate within two or three minutes once the meeting ends and the pressure passes. She wants to know whether this pattern is something to be concerned about, and what, if anything, she should do differently.

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, what is happening in Imani's body when her heart rate rises during the tense part of the meeting and falls afterward. Name the branches involved, the neurotransmitters at work, and how dual innervation of the heart makes both directions possible.
  2. Explain why a quick rise followed by a quick return to baseline is different from a nervous system that is "stuck," using the concept of dynamic balance or heart rate variability to support your answer.
  3. Identify one feature of Imani's pattern that is reassuring, and describe one hypothetical change to that pattern that would warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician, explaining why.
  4. Give one practical, general-education response to Imani that explains the physiology without diagnosing her, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Identifies sympathetic mobilization during the meeting and parasympathetic (vagal) return afterward, referencing dual innervation of the heart.
  • Names the correct neurotransmitters (norepinephrine and acetylcholine) and describes the two-neuron chain accurately.
  • Explains why a quick rise-and-return reflects dynamic balance and flexibility rather than a "stuck" system, referencing heart rate variability or an equivalent flexibility concept.
  • Distinguishes Imani's ordinary pattern from a hypothetical red-flag pattern that would warrant clinician evaluation, and explains why.
  • Offers a practical, general-education suggestion and clearly states where general education ends and clinical care begins, without diagnosing.
