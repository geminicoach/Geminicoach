The Chemical Handshake

How an electrical spike becomes a chemical whisper — and then a spike again.

60 min read 8 cited sources

Here is a fact that should bother you more than it usually does: your neurons almost never touch. In Chapter 1 we watched a neuron gather its inputs, reach threshold, and fire a clean electrical spike down its axon. In Chapter 2 we mapped how those neurons wire into circuits. But the wiring diagram hides a secret. At nearly every connection, there is a gap — about twenty billionths of a meter of salty water — and the electrical spike simply cannot jump it.

So the nervous system does something audacious. At the edge of the gap, it converts the electrical signal into a burst of chemistry, squirts molecules across the void, and rebuilds an electrical signal on the far side. This happens in under a millisecond, hundreds of times a second, at trillions of connections, right now, as you read this sentence. This chapter is about that conversion — the chemical handshake that turns one cell's decision into another cell's input. It is the single most important trick in the entire nervous system, and once you understand it, a great deal of pharmacology, psychology, and physiology stops being a list of facts and starts being a story.

The gap that makes everything possible

The place where two neurons communicate is called the synapse. The neuron sending the signal is presynaptic; the one receiving it is postsynaptic. Between them lies the synaptic cleft — the gap. It is tempting to think of this gap as a design flaw, a bit of sloppy engineering that evolution never got around to fixing. It is the opposite. The gap is the feature.

Consider what a purely electrical, hard-wired nervous system would be. Every connection would be a soldered joint. A signal arriving at that joint would pass through unchanged — the same size, the same sign, every time. There would be no way to make a connection stronger or weaker, no way to turn it off, no way to let one signal excite a cell while an identical signal calms it. The chemical synapse breaks the direct electrical connection on purpose, and in doing so it inserts a decision point into every link of every circuit. That decision point is where drugs act, where learning is stored, and where the difference between calm and panic is negotiated.

The core sequence — and this is the spine of the whole chapter, so hold onto it — goes like this. A spike arrives at the presynaptic terminal. The spike opens calcium channels, and calcium floods in. Calcium triggers little membrane-wrapped packets called vesicles to fuse with the cell membrane and dump their cargo of neurotransmitter molecules into the cleft. Those molecules drift across and dock onto receptors on the postsynaptic membrane. The docking opens channels or launches chemical cascades that nudge the receiving cell's voltage up toward its own threshold, or down away from it. And then — critically — the neurotransmitter is cleared away, so the signal can end and the synapse can reset for the next one.

Electrical, then chemical, then electrical again. A digital spike becomes an analog chemical cloud becomes, on the far side, a graded electrical nudge. Let us walk through each stage slowly, because the details matter and because each one turns out to be a place where the real world — caffeine, antidepressants, nerve agents, snake venom — reaches in and grabs the machinery.

Stage one: the spike arrives and calcium answers

The action potential travels down the axon as a self-regenerating wave of voltage — the same all-or-nothing spike from Chapter 1. When it reaches the presynaptic terminal, it finds a specialized patch of membrane called the active zone, and clustered there are voltage-gated calcium channels. These channels are shut at rest. The arriving spike depolarizes the terminal, the calcium channels sense the voltage change and pop open, and because calcium is far more concentrated outside the cell than inside, Ca²⁺ ions rush in.

This is the first conversion. The universal currency of the axon — a change in membrane voltage — has just been translated into a chemical signal inside the terminal: a sudden spike in local calcium concentration. And it is local in a way that matters enormously. Thomas Südhof, who won a share of the 2013 Nobel Prize for working out this machinery, emphasized in his Nobel Lecture that the calcium channels sit almost on top of the release machinery, so that when calcium enters it does not have to diffuse far to act (Südhof, 2014). The whole point is speed. Fast chemical synaptic transmission happens in well under a millisecond, and it can only be that fast because the trigger (calcium) is delivered essentially at the doorstep of the thing it triggers.

Why calcium, of all ions? Because calcium is an unusually good signal. Cells work hard to keep internal calcium extremely low, so any influx is a dramatic, unambiguous change — a signal with a high signal-to-noise ratio. Evolution recruited calcium as the master trigger not just here but throughout cell biology precisely because its silence at baseline makes its arrival meaningful.

The action potential is all-or-nothing — every spike is the same size. Yet the amount of neurotransmitter released can vary. Where in the sequence so far could that variability enter? (Hint: what determines how much calcium gets in?)

Stage two: vesicles fuse and release

Inside the terminal, neurotransmitter is not floating around loose. It is packaged into synaptic vesicles — tiny spheres of membrane, each holding a few thousand molecules of transmitter. A subset of these vesicles is "docked" and "primed" at the active zone, sitting ready like buckets on the lip of a well. When calcium floods in, it binds to a sensor protein on the vesicle, and the vesicle fuses with the terminal membrane, spilling its contents into the cleft. This process is called exocytosis.

Südhof's landmark 2004 review, "The Synaptic Vesicle Cycle," laid out the molecular cast in detail (Südhof, 2004). Two players are worth naming even in a working model, because they explain the two things you most want to explain: the fusion and its calcium-sensitivity. The SNARE proteins are the fusion machinery — long protein strands on the vesicle and on the target membrane that zip together like a coiled zipper, dragging the two membranes into contact. The calcium sensor is a protein called synaptotagmin, which sits on the vesicle and holds the primed fusion in a state of restraint until calcium arrives; when Ca²⁺ binds synaptotagmin, the restraint is released and fusion completes almost instantly. You do not need to memorize these names for this course, but you should carry away the concept: there is a loaded, cocked mechanism, and calcium is the finger on the trigger.

Once emptied, the vesicle membrane is recovered and recycled — pulled back into the terminal, refilled with transmitter, and made ready to fire again. This recycling (the endocytosis half of the "vesicle cycle") is what allows a terminal to keep releasing during sustained activity without simply running out of packets. Südhof (2004) framed the whole terminal as a small, self-sustaining factory that turns over its own machinery on a timescale of seconds.

Notice a consequence of the packaging. Because transmitter comes in vesicles, it is released in quanta — discrete gulps, one vesicle's worth at a time. A weak stimulus might release a handful of vesicles; a strong, high-calcium event might release many. This is one of the places the graded, analog quality of chemical signaling comes from. The spike that triggered release was all-or-nothing, but the amount of chemistry it produces is tunable.

Stage three: molecules cross and dock on receptors

Now the transmitter is in the cleft. It does not swim purposefully anywhere — it simply diffuses, and because the cleft is so absurdly thin, diffusion carries it to the opposite membrane in microseconds. There it meets the receptors: proteins embedded in the postsynaptic membrane, each shaped to recognize a particular transmitter.

This is the lock-and-key principle, and it is worth stating carefully because a subtle version of it is the intellectual centerpiece of this chapter. The neurotransmitter is the key. The receptor is the lock. A key that does not fit a lock does nothing — glutamate will rattle uselessly against a receptor shaped for GABA. Fit is everything, which is what makes the whole system addressable: a terminal can release its transmitter into a crowded cleft and only cells bearing the matching receptor will respond.

But here is the twist that the naïve lock-and-key story misses, and it is the twist you must internalize: a key does not carry its own meaning. The meaning is built into the lock. Turning a key might open a door or it might sound an alarm, depending entirely on what the lock is wired to do. In exactly the same way, a neurotransmitter does not "mean" excitation or inhibition. What it does depends on the receptor it binds. We will spend a whole section and a whole widget on this, because it is the single most common misconception in popular neuroscience.

Two families of locks: ionotropic and metabotropic

Receptors come in two broad architectures, and the difference between them is essentially a difference in speed and reach (Purves et al., 2001; Kandel et al., 2021).

An ionotropic receptor is a receptor and an ion channel fused into one protein. When transmitter binds, the channel opens directly, and ions flow. This is fast — a fraction of a millisecond from binding to current — and it is local and brief. Ionotropic receptors are the workhorses of fast point-to-point signaling: this is how the excitatory and inhibitory inputs from Chapter 1's threshold simulator actually get delivered. Each binding event opens a tiny channel, a tiny current flows, and the postsynaptic voltage twitches up or down.

A metabotropic receptor does not contain a channel at all. When transmitter binds, the receptor activates an internal signaling cascade — a G-protein and a chain of second messengers inside the cell — that then, indirectly, opens or closes channels elsewhere, changes the cell's metabolism, or even alters gene expression. This is slower (tens of milliseconds to seconds to minutes) but far more powerful in its reach: a single binding event can amplify into thousands of downstream molecular actions and can change how the cell responds to everything else. Metabotropic signaling is the basis of neuromodulation — the slow tuning of a cell's overall state rather than the fast delivery of a discrete message.

Keep these two families in mind as we meet the transmitters. As a rough heuristic: the fast "accelerator and brake" transmitters (glutamate and GABA) act largely through ionotropic receptors to deliver quick, discrete signals, while the "state and motivation" transmitters (dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine) act largely through metabotropic receptors to tune the system slowly. It is only a heuristic — every one of them uses both kinds of receptors somewhere — but it will keep you oriented.

Think About It

A drug reaches the brain that binds a metabotropic serotonin receptor. Would you expect its effects to appear within a millisecond, or to build over seconds and minutes? Why does the receptor architecture, not the transmitter, tell you the answer?

Stage four: the receiving cell edges toward or away from threshold

When a receptor opens a channel, ions flow, and the local membrane voltage changes. Two outcomes are possible, and this is where we cash out Chapter 1's language of threshold.

If the opened channel lets in positive ions (sodium, say), the local voltage moves up, toward threshold. This is an excitatory postsynaptic potential, or EPSP — a small nudge that makes the receiving cell more likely to fire. If the opened channel lets in negative ions (chloride) or lets positive ions out (potassium), the local voltage moves down, away from threshold. This is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential, or IPSP — a nudge that makes firing less likely.

No single synapse decides anything on its own. The postsynaptic neuron is doing exactly what the Chapter 1 simulator did: summing thousands of these little EPSPs and IPSPs across its dendrites and cell body, moment by moment. If the running total reaches threshold, the cell fires its own spike, and the whole handshake begins again at the next synapse downstream. The synapse, then, is not a wire. It is a vote — a graded, weighted, chemically-delivered vote — and the neuron tallies the votes.

Now watch the full conversion snap into place. A discrete electrical spike arrived. It became a graded chemical signal (a variable-sized puff of transmitter). That chemical signal became a graded electrical signal again (an EPSP or IPSP of variable size). And only when enough of those graded signals sum will a new discrete spike be born. Digital in, analog across, digital out. The synapse is the translator that lets a spiking, all-or-nothing nervous system also be a smoothly graded, tunable one.

Meeting the players — by what they do, not what they're called

Textbooks often introduce neurotransmitters as a pharmacology roll-call: names, chemical classes, receptor subtype tables. For a working mental model that is exactly backwards. Let us meet the major transmitters by function — by the job they do in the running system — and treat the chemistry as supporting detail. Iversen and colleagues (2009) give the full pharmacological treatment for those who want it; here we want the story.

Glutamate — the accelerator

Glutamate is the brain's principal excitatory transmitter. The overwhelming majority of fast excitatory synapses in the central nervous system use it, acting through ionotropic receptors to deliver quick EPSPs. When you think "one neuron pushing another toward firing," think glutamate. It is the accelerator pedal of the brain — and, foreshadowing Chapter 8, its receptors are central to how synapses strengthen during learning. Too much glutamate signaling, unchecked, is genuinely dangerous (a state called excitotoxicity), which is one reason the brake is so important.

GABA — the brake

GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) is the principal inhibitory transmitter of the mature brain. Acting largely through ionotropic receptors that admit chloride, it delivers IPSPs — nudging cells away from threshold. If glutamate is the accelerator, GABA is the brake, and a nervous system is only stable because the two are held in tension. A great many calming and sedating substances work by enhancing GABA's action; when the brake is boosted, the whole system quiets. The glutamate/GABA balance is, in a real sense, the excitation/inhibition balance that the Chapter 1 simulator was summing all along.

Dopamine — motivation and prediction

Dopamine is not, despite the popular slogan, a "pleasure molecule." Its best-supported computational role is as a reward prediction error signal: dopamine neurons fire not simply when something good happens, but when something is better than expected, and they pause when something is worse than expected (Glimcher, 2011). This teaching signal tells the rest of the brain how to update its predictions — the machinery of learning what to pursue. Dopamine acts through metabotropic receptors (the D1 and D2 families), and here again the receptor determines the effect: the same dopamine signal drives one population of target cells one way through D1 receptors and another population the opposite way through D2 receptors (Glimcher, 2011). Motivation, not pleasure; and a modulator, not a fast messenger.

Serotonin — mood and state

Serotonin is a broad modulator of mood, arousal, appetite, and overall behavioral state. It arises from a small cluster of nuclei that project widely across the brain, and it acts through a large family of mostly metabotropic receptors — which is why serotonergic drugs tend to have slow, diffuse, state-changing effects rather than fast point-to-point ones. When we get to Chapter 9 on sleep, serotonin will reappear as one of the systems that shift the entire brain between states.

Acetylcholine — muscles and the autonomic switchboard

Acetylcholine is the transmitter at the junction between nerve and muscle — every voluntary movement you make is, at the last step, acetylcholine opening ionotropic receptors on muscle fibers (Kandel et al., 2021). It is also a central player in the autonomic nervous system, the involuntary control of heart, gut, and glands. Hold onto acetylcholine especially, because Chapters 4 and 5 will make it the chemical signature of the parasympathetic "rest and digest" branch. And acetylcholine is the textbook case for our central lesson: it uses both ionotropic (nicotinic) and metabotropic (muscarinic) receptors, and its effect flips depending on which one it meets.

Norepinephrine — alertness and arousal

Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) is the transmitter of alertness, vigilance, and arousal. In the central nervous system it sets the gain on attention and wakefulness; in the autonomic system it is the chemical signature of the sympathetic "fight or flight" branch, the counterpart to acetylcholine's parasympathetic role in Chapters 4 and 5. Norepinephrine and dopamine are chemically close cousins — dopamine is actually a synthetic precursor of norepinephrine — and their systems overlap in interesting ways. Ranjbar-Slamloo and Fazlali (2020) review the tangled relationship, noting shared receptor families and even the fact that norepinephrine transporters can clear dopamine in some brain regions, so the two systems are not as cleanly separable as older accounts implied.

Think About It

Two of the six transmitters above are the fast accelerator and brake; four are slower modulators of state and motivation. Can you sort them without looking back? Now predict: which group would you expect to act mostly through ionotropic receptors, and which through metabotropic?

The message is in the lock, not just the key

We have arrived at the idea the chapter has been circling. State it as bluntly as possible: a neurotransmitter has no fixed meaning. Excitation and inhibition are properties of the receptor, not the transmitter. The same molecule can excite one cell and inhibit another, because the two cells bear different locks (Purves et al., 2001; Kandel et al., 2021).

Acetylcholine is the cleanest demonstration, and it is worth walking through because it will pay off directly in the autonomic chapters. Acetylcholine released onto skeletal muscle binds nicotinic (ionotropic) receptors, opens cation channels, and drives the muscle toward contraction — pure excitation. That same acetylcholine, released onto the pacemaker cells of the heart, binds muscarinic (metabotropic) receptors, which through a G-protein cascade open potassium channels — and the heart cell is inhibited, its rate slowed. One molecule. Two receptors. Opposite outcomes: speed a muscle, slow a heart.

This resolves a puzzle that would otherwise be baffling. How can a single transmitter system, using a single chemical, carry out the coordinated slowing-and-relaxing job of the parasympathetic branch across dozens of organs, each needing a different response? The answer is that the coordination lives in the receptors distributed across the target tissues, not in the messenger. The nervous system achieves specificity not by inventing a unique molecule for every message — it has only a handful of transmitters — but by installing the right lock on the right door. The postsynaptic cell, in effect, decides what an incoming signal means.

This is also why so much of pharmacology is receptor pharmacology. A drug that targets one receptor subtype can produce excitation-like or inhibition-like effects in one tissue while leaving others untouched, precisely because it is choosing among locks. Once you hold the "message is in the lock" principle firmly, a great deal of otherwise-arbitrary drug behavior becomes predictable.

Clearance: a signal that never stops is as useless as one that never starts

We have one stage left, and it is the one beginners forget. The handshake is not complete when the transmitter binds — it is complete when the transmitter is removed. Imagine a doorbell that, once pressed, kept ringing forever. It would tell you nothing after the first instant, because a signal that cannot turn off cannot carry information. The nervous system faces exactly this constraint: for a synapse to send a second message, it must first erase the first one. This is clearance, and there are two main mechanisms.

Reuptake is the vacuum cleaner. Specialized transporter proteins in the presynaptic membrane pump the transmitter back into the terminal, ending its action in the cleft and, conveniently, recovering the molecules for reuse. This is the dominant clearance route for serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. Enzymatic breakdown is the shredder. An enzyme in the cleft chemically chops the transmitter into inactive fragments. Acetylcholine is the classic case: an enzyme called acetylcholinesterase splits it almost the instant it unbinds, which is why signals at the nerve-muscle junction can be so crisp and repeatable.

Now here is why clearance belongs at the center of this course rather than as a footnote: blocking clearance changes signaling, and an enormous fraction of psychoactive substances work exactly this way. If you block the reuptake transporter, the transmitter lingers in the cleft, keeps binding receptors, and its signal is amplified and prolonged. This is precisely the mechanism of the reuptake-inhibitor class of antidepressants (which prolong serotonin's presence) and of stimulants like cocaine (which prolong dopamine's). If you block the breakdown enzyme, the same thing happens through the other route — this is how acetylcholinesterase inhibitors work, a category that ranges from medications for certain memory disorders to, at the lethal extreme, nerve agents and many insecticides, which flood the neuromuscular junction with acetylcholine until muscles can no longer function.

You saw both of these in the "Synapse in Motion" widget. Toggle reuptake off, and the transmitter meter refuses to fall; toggle the breakdown enzyme off, and the same thing happens by the other mechanism; the postsynaptic voltage stays elevated, over-stimulated, unable to reset. That lingering, over-stimulated state is not an abstraction — it is the shared logic behind a startling range of drugs, medicines, and toxins. Understand clearance and you understand, at a stroke, why blocking it is such a common and powerful way to reach into the nervous system.

Think About It

A reuptake inhibitor doesn't add any new transmitter to the system — the terminal releases exactly as much as before. So why does blocking reuptake strengthen the signal? Answer in terms of how long the transmitter stays bound to receptors.

Putting the handshake back together

Step back and look at the whole thing. A spike — the digital, all-or-nothing output of Chapter 1 — arrives at a terminal. Voltage opens calcium channels; calcium is the trigger. The trigger fuses vesicles, releasing a graded puff of transmitter. The transmitter diffuses across the cleft and docks on receptors, where the lock, not the key, decides whether the receiving cell is nudged toward firing or away from it. Those nudges sum, exactly as in the Chapter 1 simulator, and may or may not produce a new spike. And finally clearance erases the message, resetting the synapse for the next handshake and giving drugs their most common point of entry.

This chapter is a hinge in the course. Backward, it explains the actual delivery mechanism behind the excitatory and inhibitory inputs you summed in Chapter 1 — those were EPSPs and IPSPs, chemically delivered, receptor-defined. Forward, it hands you the two transmitters that will define the autonomic branches in Chapters 4 and 5: acetylcholine for parasympathetic "rest and digest," norepinephrine for sympathetic "fight or flight." It foreshadows Chapter 8, where the strength of these very synapses will change with experience — the physical trace of learning. And it foreshadows Chapter 9, where the modulatory transmitters will shift the entire brain between waking and sleep. The chemical handshake is not one topic among many. It is the mechanism through which nearly everything else in this course actually happens.

The speed of neurotransmitter release is astounding: it is initiated in less than a millisecond after Ca²⁺ enters a presynaptic terminal… no other cellular signaling process comes even close.

Thomas Südhof, Nobel Lecture (2014)

Key Takeaways

  • Neurons almost never touch. At the synapse, an electrical spike is converted to a chemical signal, ferried across the cleft, and converted back to an electrical signal — digital in, analog across, digital out.
  • The trigger for release is calcium: the spike opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, and calcium binds a sensor (synaptotagmin) that lets primed vesicles fuse and dump transmitter into the cleft.
  • Receptors follow a lock-and-key logic, but the meaning is in the lock, not the key: the same neurotransmitter excites one cell and inhibits another depending on the receptor it binds.
  • Ionotropic receptors are fast, local channels; metabotropic receptors are slower G-protein cascades with wide reach — the basis of neuromodulation.
  • Think by function: glutamate is the accelerator, GABA the brake, dopamine encodes prediction/motivation, serotonin sets mood and state, acetylcholine drives muscle and parasympathetic signaling, norepinephrine drives alertness and sympathetic signaling.
  • Clearance ends the signal — by reuptake or by enzymatic breakdown. Blocking clearance makes transmitter linger and over-stimulate the receiving cell, which is exactly how many antidepressants, stimulants, and nerve agents work.
Looking Ahead

We now have the full working neuron: it integrates inputs, reaches threshold, fires, and hands its decision chemically to the next cell. In Chapter 4 we zoom out from the single synapse to a whole control system — the autonomic nervous system — and meet the two branches that govern your heart, gut, and glands without your permission. You already know their chemical signatures: acetylcholine and norepinephrine, the very transmitters you just watched cross the cleft.
