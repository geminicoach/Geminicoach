Part C · The Motor Unit

Answer each question in full sentences.

  1. Define a motor unit in your own words, and explain why it, rather than a single muscle fiber, is the smallest element of movement the nervous system can actually command.
  2. Explain the size principle: in what order are motor units normally recruited, and why does that order make the system more useful than recruiting units randomly?
  3. Compare a small motor unit in a muscle that aims the eyes to a large motor unit in the calf muscles. Describe how each is built for its job, referencing fiber type, speed, force, and fatigue resistance.

Part D · Grading Force: Recruitment and Rate Coding

Answer each question in full sentences.

  1. Explain recruitment as a way of grading force, and explain rate coding as the second way. How do the two levers work together across a typical contraction?
  2. Define tetanus, and explain how firing an already recruited motor unit faster turns a series of individual twitches into a smooth, stronger contraction.
  3. Every motor neuron spike is all or nothing, and every muscle fiber twitch is all or nothing. Explain how the nervous system still manages to produce a smoothly graded range of force, from a light touch to a maximal effort, out of these strictly on-or-off building blocks.

Part E · Proprioceptive Feedback and Reflex Correction

Answer each question in full sentences.

  1. Explain the difference between an open loop system and a closed loop system for controlling movement. Which one describes real human movement, and why does that matter for accuracy?
  2. Name the two sensor types that supply proprioceptive feedback from a moving limb, and describe what each one reports.
  3. Describe the stretch reflex: what triggers it, roughly how fast does it correct an error, and why does that speed matter compared with a correction routed through the cortex?
  4. Explain why the nervous system keeps both a fast spinal reflex loop and a slower cortical or cerebellar loop running at the same time during a single movement, rather than relying on just one.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds understanding of how a command becomes a movement, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, and treat anything that looks clinical (sudden loss of movement or speech, progressive or use-dependent weakness, uncontrolled tremor, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
