The Spark That Starts Everything

How one cell, by briefly flipping its charge, becomes the atom of thought, movement, and sensation.

60 min read 8 cited sources

Put your hand flat on a table and press down until you feel the pressure in your fingertips. In the fraction of a second it took you to notice that pressure, a chain of tiny electrical events raced up your arm, hopped between cells, crossed gaps, and arrived in your brain as the plain fact of contact. Nothing about that experience feels electrical. It feels like touch. But underneath, the entire event was assembled out of one repeated trick performed by billions of cells: a brief, sharp flip of voltage across a membrane, passed along a wire, then reset.

That flip is called an action potential, and it is the single most important thing to understand in this entire course. Every chapter that follows — circuits, chemical messengers, reflexes, sensation, movement, learning, even sleep — is built from this one event repeated in different arrangements. So we are going to take our time with it. By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain, in plain language, how a neuron decides to fire, why that firing is "all-or-nothing," and how a single cell manages to tell your brain the difference between a gentle touch and a crushing grip.

The neuron as a decision-maker

It is tempting to open a chapter like this with an anatomical diagram covered in arrows, asking you to memorize dendrite, soma, axon, terminal. We will meet those parts, but the labels are not the point. The point is what the parts do. A neuron is not a static structure to be annotated; it is a tiny device that repeatedly answers one question: given everything I'm hearing right now, should I fire or not?

Read the neuron left to right and it tells a three-word story: input, integrate, output. Signals arrive at the branching dendrites. They are summed at the cell body (or soma). And if the sum is large enough, a spike is launched down the long output cable, the axon, toward the next cell in line. This "integrate-and-fire" logic is so central that neuroscientists have distilled it into a canonical mathematical model — the integrate-and-fire neuron — precisely because it captures the essential behavior with almost nothing else attached: potential accumulates until a threshold is crossed, then a spike is emitted and the counter resets (Burkitt, 2006).

Hold onto the decision-maker metaphor. Everything technical we add — ions, gates, voltages, myelin — is just detail hung on that frame. A neuron gathers evidence and casts a vote.

The parts, briefly

Let's name the cast so we can use the words freely. The dendrites are the receiving surface — a bushy tree that dramatically increases how many other cells can talk to this one. The soma houses the nucleus and acts as the tallying point. The axon initial segment, right where the axon leaves the soma, is the actual polling station: this is where the fire/no-fire decision is physically made. The axon itself is the transmission line, sometimes microns long, sometimes a meter long (the axons running from your spinal cord to your toe are famously the longest cells in your body). And the axon terminals are where this neuron hands its message to the next one — the subject of Chapter 3.

Notice already an asymmetry that matters: many inputs, one output. A single neuron may receive thousands of incoming contacts across its dendritic tree but has only one axon carrying one kind of answer. That convergence — many voices in, one decision out — is the physical basis of the "integration" step, and it is where the interesting computation happens.

The membrane is a charged battery

Before a neuron can fire, it has to be ready to fire — and readiness means being charged, like a battery held at tension, waiting. This baseline state is the resting membrane potential, and in most neurons it sits at roughly −70 millivolts (mV). That number means the inside of the cell is about 70 thousandths of a volt more negative than the outside. It is a small voltage in absolute terms, but across a membrane only a few nanometers thick, the electric field is enormous.

Where does that voltage come from? From ion gradients — differences in the concentration of charged particles across the membrane — and from the selective doors that let some ions cross more easily than others. Two players dominate the story: sodium (Na⁺), which is packed outside the cell, and potassium (K⁺), which is concentrated inside. A tireless molecular pump, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, spends metabolic energy to maintain these gradients, pushing sodium out and hauling potassium in against their tendencies to even out (Purves et al., 2001a).

Here is the subtle part. At rest, the membrane is far more permeable to potassium than to sodium — potassium's channels are the ones standing open. So potassium, abundant inside, tends to leak outward down its concentration gradient. But every positive potassium ion that leaves makes the inside slightly more negative, and that growing negativity pulls back on the positively charged potassium, resisting further loss. The membrane settles at the voltage where those two forces — the chemical push outward and the electrical pull inward — balance. That equilibrium is close to potassium's own preferred voltage, which is why the resting potential lands near −70 mV. The Nernst and Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equations formalize this balance, but the intuition is enough for us: the resting potential is the truce struck between concentration gradients and electrical attraction, weighted by which doors happen to be open (Purves et al., 2001a).

Think About It

If the resting membrane is mostly permeable to potassium, and potassium is concentrated inside the cell, which direction does potassium tend to move — and why does that outward movement stop before the cell empties of potassium? Answer it in your own words before reading on.

The reason we call this a battery is that it stores potential energy the cell can release in an instant. A charged battery is not doing anything, but it is poised to. The neuron spends energy constantly, second by second, to keep itself charged and tense — precisely so that, when the moment comes, it can discharge explosively. That explosion is the action potential.

The spike: all-or-nothing

Now release the tension. Suppose enough excitatory input arrives to nudge the membrane at the axon initial segment upward from −70 mV toward zero. This upward movement is called depolarization. For small nudges, nothing dramatic happens — the membrane drifts up a little and then leaks back down. But there is a tipping point, typically around −55 mV, called the threshold. Cross it, and the behavior of the membrane changes completely.

At threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels snap open. Sodium, which has been dammed up outside the cell, floods inward, and because sodium is positive, it drives the voltage rapidly upward — which opens still more sodium channels, which lets in still more sodium. This is positive feedback, a self-amplifying rush. The membrane voltage shoots from −55 mV up past zero to around +40 mV in about a millisecond. That upstroke is the spike.

Almost immediately, two things end the party. The sodium channels inactivate — they close automatically after opening, regardless of voltage. And slower voltage-gated potassium channels open, letting potassium rush outward and pulling the voltage back down. This downstroke is repolarization. The membrane briefly overshoots into extra negativity (hyperpolarization) before the resting machinery restores −70 mV. This entire choreographed sequence — the ionic mechanism of the spike — was worked out with extraordinary precision by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley in a series of experiments on the giant axon of the squid, culminating in a mathematical model so accurate it still anchors the field seventy years later (Hodgkin & Huxley, 1952).

Why "all-or-nothing" is the load-bearing idea

Here is the concept the whole course leans on. Once threshold is crossed, the spike that results is always the same size. A signal that barely crosses threshold produces exactly the same amplitude spike as a signal that blasts past it. There is no "bigger" action potential. This is what we mean by all-or-nothing: like a toilet flush, it either goes completely or it doesn't go at all. Pressing the handle harder does not produce a more powerful flush.

Why should biology work this way? Because it makes signaling reliable over distance. If spikes could shrink, a signal traveling a meter down your leg would fade to nothing before it arrived, the way a shout dies over a canyon. Instead, each spike is regenerated at full strength as it travels — which brings us to the domino metaphor. A falling domino does not push the next one over with its own momentum; it merely tips it, and the next domino supplies its own fall. Whether you nudge the first domino gently or violently, every domino down the line falls with identical force. The action potential propagates the same way: each patch of membrane triggers the next to fire from its own stored charge, so the signal never weakens. The catch — dominoes cannot be knocked "harder" — is exactly the constraint the nervous system had to solve.

Think About It

If every action potential is the same size no matter how strong the stimulus, how could your nervous system possibly tell the difference between a whisper and a shout, or a warm cup and a scalding one? Hold that puzzle — we resolve it at the end of the chapter.

The refractory period: a built-in reset

Right after a spike, the neuron cannot immediately fire again. During the absolute refractory period, the sodium channels are still inactivated and simply will not open, no matter how strong the input. Then comes a relative refractory period, during which firing is possible but requires an unusually strong push. This forced pause is not a flaw — it does two useful jobs. First, it enforces a direction: because the patch of axon just behind the spike is refractory, the wave can only propagate forward, never backward. Second, it caps the maximum firing rate, setting an upper speed limit on how fast a neuron can send spikes. That limit will matter enormously when we get to frequency coding.

Decades of work reviewed by Stuart and Spruston (2015) show that real dendrites do this integration in ways far richer than passive addition — with active, voltage-sensitive dendritic events that can boost or gate inputs depending on where on the tree they arrive — but the core idea holds: the axon initial segment fires only when the integrated signal crosses threshold.

How fast is a thought? Signal speed and myelin

An all-or-nothing spike that never weakens is reliable, but reliability says nothing about speed. If your hand touches a hot stove, you want the withdrawal command to arrive before the burn does its damage. So evolution had a second problem to solve: getting spikes down long axons quickly.

There are two levers. The first is axon diameter. A wider axon offers less internal resistance to the flow of current, so the spike propagates faster — which is exactly why the squid, needing a lightning-fast escape reflex, evolved a "giant axon" up to a millimeter across, the very axon Hodgkin and Huxley later exploited. But building wide axons is expensive in space and material; you cannot pack a human brain with squid-sized cables.

The second lever, the elegant one, is myelin. Myelin is a fatty, insulating wrapping — produced by glial cells — that sheaths the axon in segments, like sausage links, with small bare gaps between them called the nodes of Ranvier. Insulation stops current from leaking out across the wrapped stretches, so instead of the spike laboriously regenerating at every point along the membrane, the electrical signal jumps almost instantly from node to node, only pausing to regenerate at the bare gaps. This node-hopping is called saltatory conduction (from the Latin saltare, "to leap").

The speed difference is not subtle. Unmyelinated axons conduct at roughly 0.5 to 10 meters per second; myelinated axons can reach up to 150 meters per second (Purves et al., 2001b) — the difference between a stroll and a race car. This is why the loss of myelin in demyelinating conditions is so devastating: the wires are intact, but the signals crawl or scatter.

Solving the puzzle: frequency coding

Return now to the puzzle we planted earlier. If every spike is identical in size, how does a neuron communicate strength — the difference between a whisper and a shout? The answer is one of the most beautiful ideas in physiology, and it was discovered nearly a century ago by Edgar Adrian, recording from single sensory fibers. Adrian found that stronger stimuli did not produce bigger spikes — they produced more frequent ones. A light touch might fire a few spikes per second; a heavy press fires a rapid barrage. The amplitude is fixed, but the firing rate carries the message (Adrian & Zotterman, 1926).

This is frequency coding (or rate coding), and it is the nervous system's elegant workaround for the all-or-nothing constraint. Think of it like Morse code: every dot is the same loudness, but the pattern and rate of dots carries the meaning. Stimulus intensity is translated into spike frequency; the digital, fixed-size spike becomes an analog signal by varying how often it repeats. And remember the refractory period from earlier — it is exactly what sets the ceiling on this rate, defining the loudest "shout" a neuron can produce.

So we can now state the two-part principle that this chapter has been building toward, the principle you will carry into every remaining chapter of the course:

Neural signals are digital in strength — every spike is all-or-nothing, identical in size — but graded in frequency. Intensity is not written in how big a spike is, but in how many arrive, and how fast.

The central principle of neural coding

With that, the neuron stops being a diagram to label and becomes something you can reason about. It charges itself into readiness (resting potential), listens to a chorus of excitation and inhibition (integration), casts an all-or-nothing vote when the tally crosses threshold (the spike), and encodes the strength of what it detected in the rhythm of its firing (frequency coding) — sending that answer racing down an insulated cable to the next cell in the chain.

Key Takeaways

  • A neuron is a decision-maker running an input → integrate → output loop: dendrites receive, the soma sums, and the axon initial segment fires if the total crosses threshold.
  • The resting membrane potential (~−70 mV) is a charged battery, maintained by ion gradients and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, with potassium's outward leak setting the baseline.
  • The action potential is a self-amplifying sodium rush past threshold (~−55 mV), followed by potassium-driven repolarization and a refractory reset — the mechanism mapped by Hodgkin and Huxley (1952).
  • Spikes are all-or-nothing: always the same size, regenerated at full strength like falling dominoes, so signals never weaken over distance.
  • Myelin and axon diameter set conduction speed; saltatory conduction lets myelinated axons reach ~150 m/s versus ~0.5–10 m/s for bare ones.
  • Frequency coding resolves the all-or-nothing paradox: stimulus strength is encoded in firing rate, not spike size (Adrian & Zotterman, 1926).
Looking Ahead

We now have the atom of the course: one cell, one spike, one decision. In Chapter 2 we start assembling atoms into molecules. What happens when the output of one neuron becomes the input of thousands, and thousands feed back onto one? We turn from the single decision-maker to the circuit — where excitation, inhibition, and feedback loops give rise to behaviors no single neuron could produce alone.
