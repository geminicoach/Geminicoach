Cheat-Sheet · B5 Nervous System I

Chapter 2 : Wiring the Body

One nervous system, two territories, traffic that runs in two directions, and a cord that does more than relay.

The Two Territories

Central (CNS)Brain and spinal cord. Sealed in bone, floated in cerebrospinal fluid, guarded by a blood-brain barrier. This is where integration, weighing many inputs to produce a coherent output, is concentrated.
Peripheral (PNS)Cranial and spinal nerves reaching every muscle, gland, and patch of skin. Exposed, repairable, but not armored. Peripheral axons can regrow after injury; central circuits largely cannot.
The boundaryPhysical, not just conceptual. Central axons are myelinated by oligodendrocytes; the moment an axon exits the cord it is myelinated instead by Schwann cells, at a discrete transition zone.

Traffic Direction

  • Afferent : traveling toward the CNS. Incoming, sensory. "Afferent = arriving."
  • Efferent : traveling away from the CNS. Outgoing, motor. "Efferent = exiting."
  • Direction only, never importance. A pleasant sensation and a painful one can both be afferent.
  • Most nerves are mixed cables: afferent and efferent axons bundled side by side in one sheath.

The Reflex Arc

  • 1. Receptor : detects the stimulus, converts it to a signal.
  • 2. Afferent neuron : carries the signal in, enters the dorsal (back) root.
  • 3. Interneuron : the local decision-maker, inside the cord.
  • 4. Efferent neuron : carries the command out, exits the ventral (front) root.
  • 5. Effector : the muscle that acts, roughly 100 to 150 ms total.
  • Some reflexes (the knee jerk) skip the interneuron for an even faster loop.

Somatic vs. Autonomic

  • Somatic : voluntary commands to skeletal muscle. "I meant to do that."
  • Autonomic : automatic commands to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands.
  • Autonomic splits into sympathetic and parasympathetic branches, previewed here, covered in full later.
  • Most organs answer to both branches at once; the result is a shifting balance, not an on/off switch.

The Cord: More Than a Cable

  • Full of inhibitory interneurons that actively filter and route signals, not passive relay.
  • Generates rhythmic patterns (central pattern generators) that produce basic stepping on their own.
  • Organized segment by segment: each segment maps to a strip of skin (dermatome) and muscles (myotome).
  • That segmental map is how a reflex test localizes a finding to a specific level of the cord.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how the nervous system is organized, not a license to diagnose or treat. Sudden weakness, slurred speech, or numbness that persists or spreads are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
