Part B · The Math That Lies

  1. Explain why the 3,500-calorie rule (a 500 kcal daily deficit equals a pound a week, forever) is a static model, and name at least two reasons real daily expenditure drifts downward as a person loses weight.
  2. Because expenditure falls toward intake over time, real fat loss traces a curve, not a line. Describe what that curve looks like early versus late, and explain what a multi-week plateau actually represents.
  3. The dynamic model gives a better rule of thumb than 3,500. Roughly how much does daily maintenance fall per pound lost, and what does that mean for someone who has already lost 20 pounds and wants to keep losing?

Part D · Size the Deficit (worked calculation)

Devin is 35, weighs 210 pounds (about 95 kg), carries roughly 28% body fat, sits at a desk, and lifts twice a week. He wants to lose fat and hold on to muscle. Work the steps and show your reasoning at each one.

  1. Choose a weekly rate of loss for Devin as a percentage of bodyweight, and justify it from his body fat and training status.
  2. Convert that rate into pounds per week, then into an estimated daily calorie deficit. Show the arithmetic.
  3. Set his daily protein target in grams before adjusting anything else, and explain why protein is set first.
  4. Describe the feedback loop Devin should run over the next two to three weeks, and what he should do if he is losing much faster or much slower than planned.

Part E · The Load-Bearing Wall

  1. Protein and resistance training are described as one muscle-preservation system, not two separate tips. Explain what each contributes, and why neither works nearly as well alone.
  2. The general-population number of 0.8 g/kg is called a floor, not a target. Give an evidence-aligned protein range for a general dieter, and explain why the requirement climbs the leaner and more energy-restricted a person becomes.
  3. In the Longland (2016) study, men in a roughly 40% deficit on high protein gained lean mass while losing fat. Explain why that result is surprising, and what it says about the usual "rules" of dieting.

Part F · Surplus, Recomposition, and Realistic Rates

  1. Building muscle usually needs a surplus, but the chapter insists it be modest. Explain why a large surplus does not build muscle faster, and what the extra calories become instead.
  2. Recomposition (losing fat and gaining muscle at once) is easy for some people and a slow grind for others. Name two of the four dials that decide recomp potential, and describe the profile for whom recomposition is the expected outcome.
  3. Fat is lost far faster than muscle is built. Explain this asymmetry with rough numbers, and say how it should change the way a gaining phase is judged (what to measure, over what time frame).

Where education ends
This worksheet builds nutrition literacy and planning skill, not a license to diagnose, prescribe, or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how energy balance and the math of change work, and treat anything that looks clinical (very low intake with signs of under-fuelling, loss of menstrual periods, hair loss, disordered-eating patterns, a managed medical condition, or unexplained weight change) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
