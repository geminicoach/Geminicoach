Part A · The Average Hides the Person

A reported result is almost always a mean, the center of a distribution that no single participant has to actually experience.

  1. In the PREDICT 1 study (Berry et al., 2020), more than a thousand adults ate identical standardized meals. The blood-fat response varied by roughly 103 percent between people, glucose by about 68 percent, and insulin by about 59 percent. Explain what that spread means, and why it makes "this is a healthy meal" a statement about a population rather than a promise to any one person.
  2. Someone tells you, "I tried that approach and it did nothing for me." Using the idea of a distribution, explain why that can be literally true without the person failing or the recommendation being wrong, and describe how you would treat the general recommendation from that point forward.

Part C · When the Details Finally Matter

Nutrient timing sits low in the hierarchy for most people. Its payoff rises only for a specific kind of person, and for a reason.

  1. State where nutrient timing sits in the hierarchy for a general-population person, and explain why chasing a precise post-workout window buys them almost nothing (Kerksick et al., 2017).
  2. Mori (2014) fed trained and untrained men identical protein and carbohydrate either immediately after resistance exercise or six hours later. The trained men showed higher nitrogen balance with immediate feeding; the untrained men showed no difference. Explain the mechanism that flips the practical conclusion between the two groups.
  3. One person trains four times a week with five years of lifting behind them; another has not trained in years. Both ask whether they should bother with peri-workout timing. Give each a different, reasoned answer, and say what would have to become true before the second person should start caring about timing.

Part D · Calibrate to Life Stage

Aging and female-specific physiology move the defaults for mechanism-backed reasons. Keep your answers at the level of general physiology; anything that looks clinical belongs to a qualified clinician.

  1. Define anabolic resistance (Breen & Phillips, 2011) and explain why it is best described as a dimmed dial rather than a broken wire. Then give the two interventions that belong together for an older adult, the per-kilogram protein range they point to, and why neither one works nearly as well alone (Deutz et al., 2014; Rogeri et al., 2021).
  2. Explain why protein needs are not flat across the menstrual cycle. Name the hormone and the phase that raise them, give the upper protein range for active women, and say why the honest position is informed flexibility rather than a rigid day-by-day template (Sims et al., 2023).
  3. Define energy availability in plain words. Then, using the REDs spectrum from adaptable to problematic (Mountjoy et al., 2023), explain why a lost or disrupted menstrual cycle is a warning light rather than a sign of discipline, and mark where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Part E · Design a Plant-Based Plan

A well-built plant-based diet reaches parity for health and performance. A poorly built one does not. The difference is deliberate design, not virtue.

  1. Using PDCAAS and DIAAS scoring (Hertzler et al., 2020), explain why "lower-scoring" plant proteins are not the same as "inadequate," and name the three levers that close the gap.
  2. The old folklore said you must combine complementary proteins within a single meal or the protein is wasted. Say what is a myth and what is still sound here, using the free amino acid pool and the grain/legume lysine-and-methionine pattern.
  3. A person is adopting a fully plant-based diet for ethical reasons and asks what they actually have to plan for. Where education ends
This worksheet builds nutrition literacy, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe, in yourself or anyone else. Individual variation bends the defaults; it does not delete the fundamentals, and it does not make anyone qualified to read a medical test, manage a condition, or dose a medication. Treat anything that looks clinical (a disrupted or absent menstrual cycle, a suspected medical condition, the interpretation of genetic or metabolic test results, or the starting and dosing of any medication or supplement regimen) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
