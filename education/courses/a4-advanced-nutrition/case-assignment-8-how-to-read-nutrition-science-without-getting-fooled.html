Chapter 8: Run the Checklist on a Viral Headline

Take the capstone skill and use it on a real headline: read the evidence honestly, size the effect, and stay inside the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Dom, 38
Consider Dom, who is scrolling before bed and sees a post from a wellness account with 200,000 followers: "New study: this common spice slashes type 2 diabetes risk by 40 percent." The post links to a real, peer-reviewed journal, shows a photo of a smiling researcher, and has been shared eleven thousand times. Dom's mother has type 2 diabetes, so the headline lands hard. Clicking through, Dom finds the study is a cohort that recorded thousands of people's eating habits and tracked who developed diabetes over several years; the people who used the most of this spice did indeed develop diabetes less often. The account, it turns out, sells a spice-extract supplement. Dom is ready to buy a bottle tonight and messages you: "This is legit, right? Should my mom swap it for her medication?"

Your Tasks

  1. Place this study on the hierarchy of evidence and explain what a study of this design can and cannot establish about cause and effect.
  2. Name at least one plausible confounder or reverse-causation explanation for the correlation, and explain why it undercuts the "slashes risk" claim.
  3. Take apart the "40 percent." Explain the difference between relative and absolute risk and why the headline number can be technically true yet deeply misleading.
  4. Identify the hype signals in how this reached Dom (the viral framing, the single-nutrient promise, the seller behind the post), then state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins, especially regarding Dom's mother and her medication.

Your Response

0 words
Where education ends
You are demonstrating how to read evidence and communicate it plainly, not diagnosing, prescribing, or advising anyone to change a treatment. A responsible response explains the reasoning, identifies what warrants professional evaluation, and does not tell Dom's mother to alter or stop her medication. Decisions about diagnosed conditions and prescriptions belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Correctly identifies the study as observational and places it on the hierarchy of evidence.
  • Names a plausible confounder or reverse-causation explanation for the correlation.
  • Distinguishes relative risk from absolute risk and explains why the headline percentage misleads.
  • Flags the hype signals: viral framing, single-nutrient claim, and the seller behind the post.
  • Stays within scope: educates without diagnosing or advising a medication change, and refers the clinical decision to a clinician.
