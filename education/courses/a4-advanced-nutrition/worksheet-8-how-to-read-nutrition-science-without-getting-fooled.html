Part C · Size the Effect

A headline reads: "This eating pattern cuts your risk of a certain cancer by 40 percent." The underlying study finds the ten-year baseline risk in the control group is 5 in 1,000.

  1. Translate the "40 percent" into an absolute risk. What does the risk move from, and to? Show your reasoning.
  2. Explain, in plain language a non-specialist could follow, why the "40 percent" and your absolute number are both true yet tell very different stories.
  3. The study also reports the result was "statistically significant (p < 0.05)." Explain why that does not, by itself, mean the finding is large or practically important.

Part E · Run the Checklist

A supplement ad claims: "In a clinical study, our berry extract boosted fat metabolism by 30 percent." Walk it through the chapter's checklist and record what you would want to know at each step before you believe or repeat it.

  1. What kind of study is it, and where does that put it on the ladder?
  2. Mechanism or real-world outcome, and how can you tell which was actually measured?
  3. If a number like "30 percent" is quoted, what is it a percentage of, and what would you ask to find out?
  4. Who funded or sells it, and why does that matter here?
  5. State your bottom line: believe it, doubt it, or suspend judgment, and justify the call in one sentence.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds the ability to read evidence critically, not a license to diagnose, prescribe, or overrule medical care, in yourself or anyone else. Explain what a study can and cannot support, and treat anything clinical (stopping or changing a medication, new or worrying symptoms, or decisions about treating a diagnosed condition) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: an educator explains the evidence, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-prescribe.
