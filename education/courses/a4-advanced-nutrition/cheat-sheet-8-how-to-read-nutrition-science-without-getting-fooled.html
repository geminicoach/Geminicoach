Chapter 8 : How to Read Nutrition Science Without Getting Fooled

One skill above all the others: judge the strength of a claim before you believe it, repeat it, or act on it.

The Hierarchy of Evidence

MechanisticCells and animals. Shows a plausible pathway, not a real-world result. A hypothesis, not advice.
ObservationalWatches people over time. Can find correlations, but cannot prove cause. A suspect, not a verdict.
Randomized trialRandomly assigns the intervention. The strongest single test of cause and effect, within its limits.
Review / meta-analysisPools many studies. The top of the ladder only if the underlying studies are sound.

Correlation Is Not Causation

  • A causes B : the headline's story, and only one of four.
  • Reverse causation : maybe B causes A.
  • Confounder : a third factor C drives both.
  • Chance : test enough foods and something hits.
  • If it is observational, name a confounder in five seconds.

Effect Size & Risk

  • Relative risk : "cuts risk 40%" sounds huge, but relative to what?
  • Absolute risk : the real move, e.g. 5 in 1,000 down to 3 in 1,000.
  • Significant ≠ important : p < 0.05 says "probably real," not "large" or "useful."
  • Always ask: a percentage of what starting number?

How False Positives Are Made

  • Multiple comparisons : test many foods, report only the hit.
  • P-hacking : slice the data until something crosses the line.
  • Forking paths : many small choices bend a result toward "significant."
  • Sample and population : ask who the finding is actually about.

Why Nutrition Is Harder

  • You cannot blind a diet : people know what they eat.
  • Adherence drift : the diet on paper is not the diet on the plate.
  • Recall error : food-frequency questionnaires are rough guesses.
  • Follow the money and remember publication bias: the studies you never see.

The 90-Second Checklist

  • 1. What kind of study is it? Place it on the ladder.
  • 2. Mechanism or outcome? Was the real-world result actually measured?
  • 3. Correlation or causation? If observational, name a confounder.
  • 4. How big is the effect, in absolute terms, not just relative?
  • 5. Who paid for it or profits from it, and which studies might be missing?
  • 6. Who was studied, and does the finding apply to this person?
Where education ends   This builds the ability to read evidence critically, not a license to diagnose, prescribe, or overrule medical care. Changing or stopping a medication, treating a diagnosed condition, or acting on new or worrying symptoms are matters for a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
