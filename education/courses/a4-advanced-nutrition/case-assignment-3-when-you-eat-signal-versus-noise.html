Chapter 3: Sort the Signal From the Noise

Take the hierarchy of impact and use it to untangle a well-meaning but upside-down routine, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Chris, 32
Consider Chris, who lifts four mornings a week before work, fasted, because that is the only time available. Chris has read the supplement marketing and runs everything around it: a protein shake slammed within ten minutes of the last set, a hard rule against "carbs at night," breakfast skipped on the theory that it keeps the metabolism sharp, and six small snacks a day to "keep the fire stoked." After two years of consistent training, Chris has barely added muscle and is frustrated. A week of honest food logging shows the picture: total daily protein averages about 95 grams (Chris weighs roughly 85 kg), most of it dumped into a large dinner, with thin, low-protein meals scattered through the rest of the day. Chris asks: "I am so disciplined about timing. Why isn't it working?"

Your Tasks

  1. Run Chris through the hierarchy of impact from the top, and identify where the real problem actually sits. Use numbers: compare Chris's total protein to the roughly 1.6 g/kg/day where fat-free mass gains plateau.
  2. Redesign Chris's protein around distribution: give a concrete plan of roughly four meals near 0.4 g/kg each, show the arithmetic, and explain why this beats the current dinner-heavy pattern.
  3. Go through Chris's four rituals (immediate post-workout shake, no night carbs, skipped breakfast, six small meals). Say which are noise and why, and which single habit genuinely applies to Chris's situation and should be kept.
  4. State clearly where general nutrition education ends and where a question would need a qualified professional.

Your Response

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing or prescribing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation (for example a managed medical condition or signs of disordered eating), and does not diagnose or prescribe. Diagnosis, treatment, and individualized medical nutrition therapy belong to a qualified professional.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Runs Chris through the hierarchy from the top and names low, dinner-skewed total protein as the real issue, not the timing rituals.
  • Compares Chris's roughly 1.1 g/kg total to the 1.6 g/kg/day plateau (Morton et al., 2017) and restructures to about 0.4 g/kg across four meals with correct arithmetic.
  • Labels the noise correctly: frequency does not change metabolic rate (Schoenfeld et al., 2015), breakfast-skipping does not slow metabolism (Sievert et al., 2019), and a night-carb ban has no fat-balance penalty.
  • Keeps the one habit that fits a fasted trainer, the post-workout protein, and explains why the window tightens for Chris (Aragon & Schoenfeld, 2013; Kerksick et al., 2017).
  • Separates general education from what needs a qualified professional, and does not diagnose or prescribe.
