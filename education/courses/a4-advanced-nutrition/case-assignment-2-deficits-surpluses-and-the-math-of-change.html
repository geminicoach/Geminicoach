Chapter 2: Size the Deficit, Defend the Rate

Take the math of change and use it to fix a too-aggressive plan, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Bella, 34
Consider Bella, who weighs about 185 pounds (roughly 84 kg), carries around 30% body fat, sits at a desk all day, and started lifting twice a week a month ago. She wants to lose about 25 pounds before an event ten weeks out, so she decided to "just go hard." Using the old rule that 500 calories a day equals a pound a week, she reasoned that if she doubled the gap she would lose twice as fast, and cut herself down to about 1,100 calories a day. Protein was an afterthought; most days land near 70 grams. The scale dropped fast for the first two weeks, then crawled, and by week five it has barely moved. Bella is frustrated and hungry, and her plan is to cut even lower to "break through." She asks: "Is my metabolism broken? Should I just eat less?"

Your Tasks

  1. Re-size Bella's plan the right way around: pick a defensible weekly rate of loss as a percentage of her bodyweight, justify it from her body fat and training status, then convert that rate into pounds per week and into a starting daily deficit. Show the arithmetic.
  2. Explain the week-five stall using the static versus dynamic model. Say why the 3,500-calorie rule overpredicts her loss, roughly how much daily maintenance falls per pound she loses, why fat loss is a curve rather than a line, and answer her question about whether her metabolism is "broken."
  3. Address her plan to cut lower. Name the three bills a steeper deficit charges, explain why her low protein makes the muscle bill worse, set her protein target first in grams and show the math, and give her a better next move than slashing calories (a feedback loop or a structured diet break).
  4. State clearly where general nutrition education ends and where Bella's situation would need a qualified professional.

Your Response

0 words
Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing or prescribing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation (for example a very low intake with signs of under-fuelling, disordered-eating patterns, or a managed medical condition), and does not diagnose or prescribe. Diagnosis, treatment, and individualized medical nutrition therapy belong to a qualified professional.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Works rate-first: picks a defensible weekly rate (about 0.75% of bodyweight for Bella, roughly 1.4 lb/week) and converts it to a starting deficit near 600 to 750 kcal/day with correct arithmetic, instead of accepting the 1,100 kcal crash.
  • Explains the stall with the dynamic model: maintenance falls by about 10 kcal per pound lost, so the paper deficit shrinks, fat loss decelerates into a curve, and a plateau is the predicted endpoint, not a broken metabolism.
  • Rejects cutting lower by naming the three bills (more lean-mass loss, harder adaptation, collapsing adherence), notes low protein worsens the muscle bill, and sets protein first at about 1.6 to 2.0 g/kg (roughly 135 to 170 g/day) with a feedback loop or diet break as the next move.
  • Communicates in plain, non-alarming language and shows the reasoning and numbers.
  • Separates general education from what needs a qualified professional, flags the very low intake, and does not diagnose or prescribe.
