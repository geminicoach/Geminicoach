Chapter 6: Grade the Gut Cure

Take the evidence-grading discipline of the chapter and apply it to a real pile of products and a real week that did not stick, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Rae, 38
Consider Rae, who got excited reading about the gut microbiome and decided to overhaul hers in a week. Rae bought a bottle of a generic probiotic, a mail-in "personalized microbiome" test kit, and a tub of inulin powder, then ran a seven-day plan: a big scoop of inulin every morning, the probiotic, and mostly the same few healthy meals. By day two Rae was bloated and gassy and figured the plan was "detoxing." By day five it settled and felt like a win. Two weeks later, back to the usual lower-fiber routine, digestion felt exactly as it had before. The test kit report graded Rae's microbiome "below optimal" and recommended a paid supplement bundle. Rae saw an ad promising a product that "heals leaky gut, reduces inflammation, and rebalances your microbiome in 7 days" and now wants to know what actually happened, what was worth it, and whether that next product is worth buying. There is no blood in the stool, no unexplained weight loss, and no persistent pain.

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, what the day-two bloating actually was and why it settled, using ramp rate and substrate-specific tolerance. Say what you would change about the method rather than the goal.
  2. Explain why the seven-day frame was the real mistake, using how fast the microbiome shifts and the fact that it tracks a habitual pattern and drifts back to baseline.
  3. Grade the advertised product's three claims one at a time ("heals leaky gut in 7 days," "reduces inflammation," "rebalances your microbiome"), separating any real underlying mechanism from the overreaching claim, and grade the mail-in test kit as well.
  4. Say what, if anything, was worth it, give a responsible general-education recommendation, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Your Response

0 words
Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and honest calibration, not diagnosing or prescribing. A responsible response separates real mechanisms from marketing, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Probiotics, test kits, and fecal transplants are not prescribed or endorsed through this course; a suspected inflammatory bowel or celiac condition, or alarm signs such as blood in the stool or unexplained weight loss, belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains the bloating as normal ramp-rate, substrate-specific fermentation, not detox or harm, and fixes the method (climb gradually, spread across meals, vary whole-food sources, add fluid).
  • Uses the rapid-shift-then-drift-back-to-baseline evidence to explain why a seven-day reset cannot buy a durable change, and that the community tracks a habitual pattern.
  • Grades the three product claims separately by evidence band, distinguishing a real mechanism (intestinal permeability) from an overreaching claim ("heals in 7 days"), and treats "reduces inflammation" and "rebalances" as unsupported as stated.
  • Grades the personalized test kit against the no-single-ideal point, and notes its useful advice was free.
  • Offers responsible, in-scope fundamentals (plant variety, gradual fiber, fermented foods, fluid, sleep, stress) and refers anything clinical, without diagnosing or prescribing.
