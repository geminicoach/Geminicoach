Peptides I · Chapter 2

Borrowing the Pulse

Sermorelin, CJC-1295, and tesamorelin do not manufacture a new hormone, they borrow the hypothalamus's own trigger, and whether that borrowed signal still arrives as a pulse or gets flattened into a plateau is most of what separates physiology from risk.

49 min read 8 cited sources

Mateo found the advertisement in a wellness clinic's email newsletter, wedged between a listicle on cold plunges and a limited-time offer on intravenous vitamin drips. It promised something specific: a "growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) peptide protocol" that would restore his "youthful growth hormone rhythm," rebuild lean mass while he slept, and do it, the copy insisted, "the way your body already knows how." He is 34, generally healthy, training three days a week, and not under any physician's care for a hormone problem. What stopped him from clicking through to the checkout page was one phrase he could not quite parse: "restores the pulse, not just the peak." He had assumed, reasonably enough, that more growth hormone (GH) was more growth hormone, however it arrived in the bloodstream. The idea that the pattern of a hormone's release might matter as much as its total amount was new to him, and it is the idea this chapter is built around.

That single word, pulse, is the real subject of this chapter, more than any specific brand name a peptide vendor might attach to it. GH does not circulate at a steady, constant concentration the way a hormone like thyroxine does. It arrives in sharp, intermittent bursts, several times a day, concentrated heavily during deep sleep, separated by long stretches where circulating GH is nearly undetectable. This chapter covers the first of two peptide classes that learners, athletes, and wellness-clinic marketing copy alike lump together under the label "growth hormone secretagogues," a broad term for anything that pushes the pituitary gland to release more GH. Specifically, this chapter is about the GHRH analogs, synthetic peptides engineered to mimic the body's own releasing signal: sermorelin, CJC-1295 and its closely related cousin modified GRF (1-29), and tesamorelin. Chapter 3 covers a mechanistically distinct second class, the ghrelin-receptor agonists, and the genuinely interesting pharmacology of what happens when the two classes are combined. Today's task is narrower, and in a sense more foundational: understanding the one receptor all three of today's compounds target, the natural signal they are built to imitate, and the honest evidence, compound by compound, behind what each one is actually shown to do in a living human being.

This is not a chapter that will tell Mateo whether to buy the peptide sitting in his inbox, and it should not try to. It is a chapter about the biology that lets him evaluate the phrase "restores the pulse" on his own terms by the time he finishes reading, rather than having to take the marketing copy's word for it. The same biology applies whether the learner working through it is sorting through a clinic's sales pitch, a friend's enthusiastic recommendation, or simply trying to understand what a physician means when a growth hormone axis test comes back abnormal.

The hypothalamus's trigger, and the receptor that reads it

Growth hormone is made and stored in specialized cells of the anterior pituitary gland called somatotrophs, but the pituitary does not decide on its own when to release it. That decision is made further upstream, in a small structure at the base of the brain called the hypothalamus, specifically in a cluster of neurons in a region called the arcuate nucleus. These neurons synthesize a 44-amino-acid peptide called growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH), sometimes written GHRH(1-44) to specify its full natural length, and release it into a private, local circulation called the hypophyseal portal system, a short loop of blood vessels that carries hypothalamic signals directly to the pituitary without first diluting them through the whole body. GHRH's job, once it arrives at the pituitary, is to instruct the somatotrophs to do two things: release GH they already have stored in secretory granules right now, and manufacture more GH for the next round.

The way GHRH delivers that instruction is through a specific docking site called the growth-hormone-releasing hormone receptor (GHRH-R), sitting on the surface of the somatotroph cell. GHRH-R belongs to a family of cell-surface receptors called G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), specifically the class B, or secretin-like, subfamily, a group of receptors built to recognize larger peptide hormones rather than small molecules. According to Gaylinn (2002), GHRH-R is essential for normal GH synthesis and release, and for the normal growth and proliferation of the somatotroph cells themselves, a fact demonstrated starkly by naturally occurring mutations of this receptor in both mice and humans, which produce GH deficiency, short stature, and an underdeveloped pituitary gland. When GHRH binds the extracellular face of GHRH-R, the receptor changes shape and activates an internal signaling partner called a stimulatory G protein (Gs), which in turn activates an enzyme called adenylate cyclase. That enzyme converts a common cellular fuel molecule into cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), a fast-acting internal messenger that activates a further enzyme, protein kinase A (PKA). PKA is what actually does the mechanical work: it triggers the somatotroph to dump its stored GH into the bloodstream within moments, and, on a slower timescale of hours, it increases transcription of the GH gene itself and supports the somatotroph's own proliferation, keeping the pituitary's GH-producing capacity replenished.

GHRH is not the only hypothalamic signal reaching the pituitary, and the second one matters just as much to this chapter, because it is what turns a steady drip into a pulse. A separate population of hypothalamic neurons releases somatostatin, sometimes called growth-hormone-inhibiting hormone, which binds its own distinct receptor on the somatotroph and actively suppresses GH release, largely by opposing the cAMP signal GHRH just worked to generate. GHRH and somatostatin are not typically high at the same moment. Instead, the hypothalamus alternates between the two: a period of low somatostatin and rising GHRH tone opens a window in which the pituitary can respond, producing a burst of GH release, followed by a period of rising somatostatin that closes the window and holds GH secretion down until the next cycle begins. According to Veldhuis and Bowers (2003), the cleanest way to understand this system is as a small, coordinated ensemble of three peptides working on complementary timescales, GHRH, somatostatin, and a third signal called ghrelin, released from the stomach rather than the brain, which Chapter 3 covers in full. Together, this three-signal system is what produces GH's signature pattern: not a tap left running, but a tap that opens and closes on a rhythm the body itself set.

Think About It

GHRH's receptor is a class B G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), a surface receptor, not a receptor sitting inside the cell waiting for a lipid to diffuse through the membrane. Before reading further, consider what that structural fact implies about how fast a GHRH analog can act once injected, and why these peptides have to be given by injection at all rather than swallowed as a pill.

GH's own effects are only part of the story, and it is worth naming the second messenger explicitly before moving on, because every compound in this chapter is ultimately judged by its effect on both. Once GH reaches the liver and other peripheral tissues, it stimulates the local production of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), a separate hormone that mediates a large share of GH's downstream effects on muscle, bone, and metabolism. IGF-1 has a much longer half-life in circulation than GH itself, which makes it a more stable, more clinically useful marker: a single blood draw for IGF-1 gives a reasonable window into a person's average GH exposure over the preceding day or so, where a single GH measurement mostly tells you whether you happened to catch a pulse or a trough. Both GH and IGF-1 also feed back on the hypothalamus, raising somatostatin tone and damping further GHRH release, a classic negative feedback loop that keeps the whole system self-limiting under normal physiology. Keep that feedback loop in mind. It is exactly the mechanism the next section shows getting bypassed when GH exposure stops looking like a pulse and starts looking like a plateau.

Pulsatile versus tonic: why the pattern is the whole point

Here is the idea Mateo's advertisement was gesturing toward, whether or not its copywriter actually understood the biology behind it. Pulsatile GH secretion refers to the natural pattern described in the previous section: sharp bursts of GH release, several times over 24 hours, concentrated heavily during the first few hours of deep, slow-wave sleep, with long, quiet troughs in between where circulating GH falls close to zero. Tonic secretion, by contrast, describes a flattened, continuously elevated pattern, GH or its downstream signal held at a roughly constant level around the clock rather than rising and falling in discrete bursts. The distinction is not academic. It is close to the single most important idea in evaluating any GH secretagogue, because the same total daily GH exposure can arrive as either pattern, and the body was built to expect only one of them.

Two separate mechanisms explain why the pattern matters, and both are worth holding onto. The first concerns the receptor itself. Prolonged, continuous stimulation of GHRH-R tends to blunt the somatotroph's own responsiveness over time, a general property shared by many GPCRs when a ligand is present constantly rather than intermittently, sometimes described as receptor desensitization. A pulsatile pattern, with real troughs between bursts, gives the receptor system a recovery window, allowing responsiveness to reset before the next pulse arrives, which is part of why a series of discrete pulses can sustain a larger cumulative signal over time than the same total exposure delivered as one flat, unbroken plateau. The second mechanism concerns the negative feedback loop described at the end of the previous section. That loop, GH and IGF-1 rising, then damping further GHRH release through increased somatostatin tone, depends on the system having discrete peaks to detect and discrete troughs to recover in. A tonic, non-physiologic elevation blunts that natural brake by keeping IGF-1 chronically present rather than chronically fluctuating, which is part of why continuously elevated GH and IGF-1 states, whether from disease or from a pharmacologic source, tend to produce a different, generally less favorable risk profile than a pattern of pulses of the same average size.

There is a third piece worth naming plainly, because it is the part a marketing phrase like "restores the pulse" is implicitly trying to claim credit for. GH's pulses are not evenly spread across the day at random. They cluster heavily around the onset of deep sleep and rise further with exercise and fasting, meaning the body concentrates its GH signaling during the specific windows when recovery and tissue repair are already the priority. A compound that mimics this rhythm, arriving briefly, prompting a burst, and then clearing, is doing something structurally different from a compound that holds GH or IGF-1 elevated continuously for days at a time, even if the two approaches could in principle deliver a similar total weekly dose of hormone. This is exactly the axis this chapter will keep returning to as it works through sermorelin, CJC-1295, modified GRF (1-29), and tesamorelin one at a time: not simply "does this compound raise GH," which all four unquestionably do, but "what pattern does it produce once it does."

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common assumption treats every GH secretagogue as interchangeable, on the theory that any compound raising average GH or IGF-1 by a similar amount should carry a similar risk and benefit profile. That assumption skips the variable this section just spent three paragraphs establishing. A compound that produces a brief, physiologic-looking pulse and a compound that holds GH or IGF-1 elevated in a flat plateau for days can reach a comparable average exposure while behaving very differently in terms of receptor responsiveness, feedback-loop integrity, and how closely the pattern resembles what the body evolved to expect.

One fragile signal, three ways to extend it

Native GHRH(1-44) is a poor drug candidate in its raw form, and understanding why explains most of what makes this compound class exist at all. Once released into circulation, GHRH is rapidly cut apart by an enzyme called dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4), giving it a functional half-life of only a few minutes. A signal that fragile cannot be manufactured, shipped, and injected on any practical schedule. Researchers solved this in a series of overlapping steps, and each of today's three GHRH-analog products represents a different point along that engineering path, which is exactly why they behave so differently in practice despite all targeting the same GHRH-R described in section one.

The first step was simple truncation. Researchers found that only the first 29 amino acids of the 44-amino-acid native hormone, a fragment usually written GHRH(1-29) or GRF(1-29), are required for full biological activity at GHRH-R. Sermorelin is essentially this fragment, unmodified, and it behaves close to how the natural hormone behaves: short-acting, cleared from circulation within roughly ten to twenty minutes, and, when injected, producing something that looks like a single physiologic-style pulse rather than a sustained elevation. The second step was chemical resistance. Specific amino acid substitutions within that same 29-residue backbone, most importantly at the position DPP-4 normally attacks, make the peptide harder for the enzyme to cut apart, extending its functional half-life from minutes to a somewhat longer but still fairly short window. This substituted backbone is what the field calls modified GRF (1-29), and on its own, it is still built to behave like a pulse generator rather than a plateau generator.

The third step is where the pharmacology genuinely diverges from the pulsatile ideal, and it is worth being precise about it, because a great deal of grey-market confusion sits exactly here. Modified GRF (1-29) can be further conjugated to a chemical handle called a Drug Affinity Complex (DAC), a maleimide group engineered to bind covalently to albumin, the most abundant protein circulating in blood plasma. Once attached to albumin, the peptide is shielded from enzymatic degradation and cleared far more slowly, on the order of days rather than minutes. This DAC-conjugated molecule is what the scientific literature actually means by CJC-1295. Products sold online under the CJC-1295 name are inconsistently one or the other, sometimes the full DAC-conjugated molecule, sometimes plain modified GRF (1-29) without the DAC attached, a distinction the buyer usually has no reliable way to verify and one this course will return to directly in a later chapter on grey-market sourcing and contamination. The distinction is not cosmetic. It is precisely the pulsatile-versus-tonic question from the previous section, expressed as a chemistry decision.

Tesamorelin took a different engineering route to the same underlying problem. Rather than truncating the molecule to 29 residues and attaching an albumin-binding handle, tesamorelin keeps closer to the full GHRH(1-44) backbone and adds a chemical modification at the amino end of the peptide, a trans-3-hexenoic acid group, that resists enzymatic degradation directly without needing to hitch a ride on albumin. The resulting molecule is intermediate in duration between the very short-acting sermorelin and the multi-day CJC-1295, engineered specifically to support a once-daily subcutaneous injection schedule, the schedule used throughout its clinical development program. Of the three engineering paths described in this section, tesamorelin's is also, not coincidentally, the one that was carried furthest through controlled human trials, which is the subject of two sections from now.

Sermorelin: the closest mimic, and a track record from a different population

Sermorelin has the longest documented history of the compounds in this chapter, and that history is worth knowing precisely, because it explains both why sermorelin has real earned credibility and why that credibility does not automatically transfer to how it is marketed today. Sermorelin was studied and used from the 1990s onward for two related purposes in a clearly defined clinical population: as a diagnostic tool to help identify growth hormone deficiency (GHD), by measuring how much GH the pituitary released in response to a controlled sermorelin dose, and as a growth-promoting treatment for children with confirmed idiopathic GHD. According to Prakash and Goa (1999), reviewing this evidence directly, sermorelin given intravenously produced a rapid, relatively specific test for GHD, with fewer false positives than some competing provocative tests, and once-daily subcutaneous sermorelin sustained meaningful increases in height velocity over twelve months of treatment in GHD-diagnosed children, with catch-up growth documented in the majority of those treated. The same review noted that sermorelin's growth-promoting effect, while real, was smaller than what direct recombinant human growth hormone treatment achieved in comparable patients, and that the most commonly reported adverse events were mild and transient, facial flushing and injection-site discomfort chief among them.

That is a genuinely solid evidence base, and it deserves to be stated as such rather than dismissed. What it is not, however, is evidence about the population sermorelin is now most commonly marketed to. The learners and adults now encountering sermorelin through wellness clinics and online research-chemical vendors are, in the great majority of cases, not children with a confirmed, biochemically diagnosed GHD. They are healthy or generally healthy adults being told the compound will improve sleep, body composition, or a vaguely defined "GH rhythm," uses the original trials described above simply did not test. This is an evidence-grade distinction, not a mechanism distinction: sermorelin's action at GHRH-R does not change depending on who is using it, but the direct clinical trial support for using it does change substantially once you move outside the diagnosed-GHD population it was originally studied in. Framed against the pulsatile-versus-tonic distinction from earlier in this chapter, sermorelin also has a genuine mechanistic point in its favor: because it behaves close to native GHRH(1-29), short-acting and quickly cleared, it is structurally the best-positioned of the three compound families in this chapter to actually produce something resembling a physiologic pulse rather than a sustained plateau, whatever the strength or weakness of the evidence for any specific claimed benefit in adults without diagnosed GHD.

Think About It

Sermorelin's clinical trial evidence comes almost entirely from children with a confirmed, biochemically diagnosed growth hormone deficiency, a narrow and specific population. Before the next section, consider what has to be true, and what remains genuinely untested, for that same evidence to be treated as support for a claim marketed to a healthy adult with no diagnosed hormone deficiency at all.

CJC-1295 and modified GRF (1-29): real pharmacology, thin human evidence

The one significant published human trial of the DAC-conjugated molecule that the scientific literature calls CJC-1295 comes from Teichman and colleagues (2006), and it is worth walking through in some detail, because it is doing a great deal of the evidentiary work for a compound with a much larger online reputation than its trial record would suggest. The study consisted of two randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, ascending-dose trials in healthy adults, ages 21 to 61, conducted over 28 and 49 days respectively. A single injection of CJC-1295 produced dose-dependent increases in mean plasma GH concentrations of two to tenfold, sustained for six days or more, and increases in mean plasma IGF-1 concentrations of 1.5 to threefold, sustained for nine to eleven days. The estimated half-life of the compound itself was 5.8 to 8.1 days. After multiple doses, mean IGF-1 levels remained above baseline for up to 28 days, and no serious adverse reactions were reported at the doses studied.

Read that result back against section two of this chapter and the tension becomes obvious. This is, by the trial's own numbers, a sustained, multi-day elevation in GH and IGF-1, not a series of discrete pulses with real troughs between them. It is exactly the tonic-leaning pattern flagged earlier as the physiologic tradeoff this whole chapter has been building toward, and it is a direct, mechanistic consequence of the DAC-albumin engineering choice described in the previous section. None of that makes the Teichman (2006) trial fraudulent or its results untrustworthy, it is a real, well-designed study, and its safety data over the studied duration were reassuring. What it does mean is that CJC-1295, structurally, is the compound in this chapter furthest from the pulsatile ideal, and that its own pivotal trial data document exactly the sustained-elevation pattern this chapter has spent two sections explaining why physiology does not naturally produce.

The evidence gap widens further once modified GRF (1-29) without the DAC conjugation enters the picture, because that specific molecule, the shorter-acting backbone alone, does not have a dedicated large controlled human trial of its own comparable to the Teichman (2006) study of the full DAC-conjugated compound. Much of what circulates about it online is inference: extrapolation from the closely related sermorelin literature, from the DAC-conjugated CJC-1295 trial, and from small, informal pharmacology discussions, rather than a dedicated efficacy and safety trial of that exact molecule in that exact form. According to Sinha and colleagues (2020), reviewing the broader class of growth hormone secretagogues used in the context of male hypogonadism and body composition, this thinness of dedicated clinical data is not unique to modified GRF (1-29), it characterizes much of the class once you move past the compounds that reached formal drug development. Notably, the company that originally developed CJC-1295 did not carry it through to a larger, pivotal trial program of its own. It moved forward instead with tesamorelin, the compound covered in the next section, which suggests something about where the sponsor's own confidence, and regulatory strategy, ultimately landed.

"A paucity of data examining the clinical effects of these compounds currently limits our understanding of GHS' role in the treatment of men with hypogonadism, but does open opportunities for future investigation."

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A widespread assumption treats CJC-1295 as an established, well-studied therapy roughly on par with tesamorelin, because both names circulate in similar online spaces. The actual evidence bases are not comparable. Tesamorelin carries multiple large, randomized, placebo-controlled phase 3 trials and a specific regulatory approval, covered next. CJC-1295 carries one published trial in healthy adults, and the molecule sold under that name is frequently not even reliably the same compound the trial tested. That gap, not brand familiarity, is what an evidence-graded assessment has to be built on.

Tesamorelin: the one compound with an approved, specific indication

Tesamorelin is the strongest evidence-grade compound in this chapter by a wide margin, and it earns that grade the honest way, through a multi-trial, regulator-reviewed development program rather than through reputation. Tesamorelin is approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), under the brand name Egrifta, specifically for the reduction of excess abdominal visceral fat in people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) associated lipodystrophy, a fat-redistribution syndrome that can develop in people living with HIV, particularly in the context of certain antiretroviral regimens, marked by a buildup of visceral fat around the internal organs alongside loss of subcutaneous fat elsewhere in the body. This is a narrow, specific, well-defined approved indication, not a general license for GH-axis stimulation in any population, and the distinction matters for exactly the same evidence-grading reasons raised in the sermorelin and CJC-1295 sections above.

The pivotal trial behind that approval, reported by Falutz and colleagues (2007), randomly assigned 412 people with HIV and abdominal fat accumulation to receive either daily subcutaneous tesamorelin or placebo for 26 weeks. Visceral adipose tissue, measured directly by computed tomography, decreased by 15.2 percent in the tesamorelin group and increased by 5.0 percent in the placebo group. Triglyceride levels improved, the ratio of total cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol improved, and IGF-1 rose by 81.0 percent in the tesamorelin group. Critically, no significant differences in glycemic measures, fasting glucose and insulin, were observed between the two groups, a genuinely reassuring finding given the class-wide theoretical concern about GH's effect on insulin sensitivity, covered directly in the next section. A follow-up study by Falutz and colleagues (2010), extending treatment to a full 12 months with a safety extension phase, found visceral fat reduced by approximately 18 percent in patients who continued tesamorelin, that the drug remained well tolerated across the longer duration, and, importantly for anyone reasoning about durability, that the improvements in visceral fat were rapidly lost in patients who were switched from tesamorelin back to placebo, indicating the effect depends on continued treatment rather than producing a lasting structural change once stopped.

A third study, by Stanley, Falutz, and colleagues (2012), dug into what the visceral fat reduction actually meant metabolically. Comparing patients who achieved at least an 8 percent reduction in visceral fat ("responders") against those who did not, the study found that responders experienced significantly greater improvements in triglyceride levels, fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1c, and adiponectin, a hormone secreted by fat tissue that generally reflects healthier metabolic function, over 52 weeks of treatment. This finding matters beyond the specific numbers: it shows tesamorelin's metabolic benefit is tied specifically to the degree of visceral fat reduction achieved, not a generic, compound-wide effect that shows up regardless of outcome. Put together, these three trials represent the deepest, most rigorously controlled human evidence base for any compound in this entire chapter, which is exactly why tesamorelin, alone among the four compounds discussed here, carries a specific, regulator-reviewed approval rather than a mechanism-based extrapolation.

It is worth being precise about the limits of that approval too, in the same evidence-graded spirit this course applies throughout. Tesamorelin's FDA approval is specific to HIV-associated lipodystrophy. Its use in other contexts, general visceral fat reduction outside HIV, broader metabolic or body-composition goals in people without HIV, aging-related interest in restoring a more youthful GH pattern, is off-label, and while some of that extrapolation is mechanistically reasonable given how tesamorelin behaves at GHRH-R, it has not been established through the same trial program that supports the approved indication. Strong evidence in one specific population is not the same claim as strong evidence for every population a compound might plausibly be marketed to, and that distinction is worth carrying forward into every chapter this course covers from here.

Think About It

Tesamorelin's approval is specific to one diagnosed condition in one patient population, HIV-associated lipodystrophy. A wellness clinic offering tesamorelin to a healthy adult with no HIV diagnosis and no lipodystrophy is extrapolating mechanistically plausible reasoning onto a population the pivotal trials never actually included. Hold that distinction in mind, it is precisely the evidence-grading discipline this entire course is built to teach.

What raising GH and IGF-1 actually costs

Every compound in this chapter works by the same basic route, more GHRH-R signaling, more GH released, more downstream IGF-1, and that route carries a reasonably consistent set of physiological costs regardless of which specific analog produced the signal. The most immediately noticeable is fluid retention. GH promotes sodium and water retention at the kidney, partly through effects on the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and partly through direct effects on kidney tubule cells, and the practical result can be peripheral edema, a puffy or swollen feeling, particularly in the hands, feet, and face. Closely related is joint discomfort. GH-driven fluid retention in and around joints and soft tissue can produce arthralgia, an aching or stiff sensation, and in some cases enough soft-tissue swelling around the wrist to compress the median nerve, producing carpal-tunnel-like numbness or tingling in the hand. These effects tend to track with the degree and duration of GH and IGF-1 elevation, and they are among the more commonly reported complaints across this compound class.

A second category of concern involves glucose metabolism. GH is, by its normal physiology, a counter-regulatory hormone, meaning one of its jobs is to oppose insulin's effect on blood sugar, part of how the body protects glucose supply to the brain during fasting and sleep. Chronically elevated GH and IGF-1, particularly in a tonic, non-physiologic pattern of the kind discussed earlier in this chapter, can reduce insulin sensitivity and nudge fasting glucose upward over time, a real and worth-monitoring concern for anyone using a compound in this class. It is worth stating plainly, though, that this theoretical concern did not show up as a significant finding in tesamorelin's own pivotal trials: Falutz and colleagues (2007) reported no significant difference in glycemic measures between the tesamorelin and placebo groups over 26 weeks. That is a genuinely reassuring, trial-documented result for tesamorelin specifically, in the HIV-lipodystrophy population it was tested in. It is not a blanket reassurance that every compound in this chapter, at every pattern of exposure a grey-market source might produce, carries the same low glycemic risk, precisely because the grey-market compounds have not been tested with anything approaching that same rigor.

A third concern sits further out on the horizon and deserves an honest, non-alarmist framing rather than either dismissal or exaggeration. IGF-1 is a genuinely potent growth-promoting, anti-apoptotic signal, meaning it encourages cell growth and division and discourages programmed cell death, properties that are entirely normal and desirable in the contexts GH and IGF-1 evolved to act in, childhood growth, tissue repair, recovery from exercise and illness. Chronic, pathological GH and IGF-1 excess, as seen in the disease acromegaly, is associated with a documented, elevated risk of certain cancers over decades of exposure, and IGF-1 levels are one of the markers oncologists and endocrinologists track carefully in some cancer survivorship contexts for exactly this reason. This is a real biological concern worth taking seriously, and it is also not the same claim as saying that tesamorelin, sermorelin, or CJC-1295, used at the exposures actually studied in the trials described in this chapter, have been shown to cause cancer in humans. They have not been studied over the multi-decade timeframe that question would require, and the honest position is exactly that: an unresolved, mechanistically grounded concern under active long-term monitoring, not a settled verdict in either direction. Anyone reasoning about this compound class should hold both halves of that sentence at once.

Signs That Warrant Medical Evaluation

Persistent hand or wrist numbness and tingling, joint swelling that does not resolve, unusual or rapid weight gain with visible fluid retention, new or worsening headaches, vision changes, or unexplained fatigue in the context of GH-axis stimulation of any kind are not something to self-interpret. These are signs that warrant a clinician's evaluation, including bloodwork such as IGF-1 and fasting glucose, not a diagnosis to reach on your own or through a forum thread.

Case study: Mateo and the pulse he almost bought blind

Case

Mateo is 34, generally healthy, training three days a week, with no diagnosed hormone condition and no clinician currently involved in any decision about GH-axis peptides. A wellness-clinic newsletter's advertisement for a "GHRH peptide protocol" used the phrase "restores the pulse, not just the peak" to describe its offering, without naming a specific compound or explaining what that phrase actually meant biologically. He came to this chapter wanting to understand that phrase well enough to evaluate the advertisement on his own, not to receive a recommendation about whether to use anything.

Running Mateo's situation back through this chapter resolves the confusion cleanly, and the resolution is the actual point of the exercise, not any single fact along the way. His starting assumption was that more GH is more GH, regardless of how it arrives in the bloodstream. Sections one and two replace that with the correct model: GH's physiologic signature is a pulse, produced by an alternating rhythm of GHRH and somatostatin acting on one receptor, GHRH-R, and the pattern of exposure, pulsatile versus tonic, carries real mechanistic weight of its own, independent of the total average amount of hormone delivered.

His advertisement's specific phrase, "restores the pulse," turns out to be doing real, if unstated, work once you know the chemistry. Of the three GHRH-analog families covered in this chapter, sermorelin and plain modified GRF (1-29) are structurally the closest to producing an actual physiologic pulse, short-acting, quickly cleared, mimicking native GHRH(1-29). The DAC-conjugated CJC-1295 is structurally the furthest from that ideal, its own pivotal trial documenting a sustained, multi-day elevation in GH and IGF-1, the tonic-leaning pattern this chapter spent two sections explaining why physiology does not naturally produce. If Mateo's advertisement was in fact selling the DAC-conjugated version under a "restores the pulse" banner, the marketing language and the underlying pharmacology would be in direct tension, precisely the kind of gap this course exists to teach a learner to notice.

The evidence-grading exercise resolves the rest. Sermorelin carries genuine, credible trial evidence, but from a diagnosed pediatric GHD population, not from healthy adults using it for sleep or body composition. CJC-1295 and modified GRF (1-29) carry pharmacologically real but thin human evidence, one meaningful trial for the DAC-conjugated form and essentially none dedicated to the non-DAC form specifically. Tesamorelin carries the strongest evidence of the four, multiple large randomized trials and an FDA approval, but for a narrow, specific population, HIV-associated lipodystrophy, that does not include a healthy 34-year-old with no HIV diagnosis. None of the compounds Mateo might be looking at, in other words, carry direct trial support for the specific, general "youthful rhythm" claim the advertisement made.

What Mateo actually walks away with is not a verdict on whether to buy the peptide. It is a working framework: identify which compound is actually being described, ask whether its documented pharmacology produces a pulse or a plateau, and ask whether the evidence behind it comes from a population and use case anything like his own. That framework travels with him past this specific advertisement, into whatever the next confident claim turns out to be.

Where this course stops

Before this chapter closes, the frame governing this entire course deserves to be restated plainly, because it shapes what today's material is for and what it deliberately is not for. This is education and harm reduction. We explain the biochemistry of the GHRH receptor, the honest reasons pulsatile and tonic patterns behave differently, the evidence, compound by compound, behind sermorelin, CJC-1295, modified GRF (1-29), and tesamorelin, and the real risks that come with raising GH and IGF-1, descriptively. We do not provide dosing guidance, cycle or stack designs, or sourcing recommendations, and nothing in this chapter is meant to function as a green light or a how-to guide for using any of these compounds.

That boundary follows directly from the biology, not from a legal formality bolted onto it. Everything covered today, GHRH's route to the pituitary through the hypophyseal portal system, the receptor-level logic behind why a pulse and a plateau are not interchangeable, the specific, documented trial results behind tesamorelin's approved indication, is true independent of any individual's decision about whether to ever use a GHRH analog. Understanding this biology has value on its own, whether the person doing the learning is a learner like Mateo evaluating a clinic's marketing claim, a person already working with a physician on a diagnosed GH-axis issue, or simply someone who wants to be able to read a confident claim with actual knowledge behind the evaluation. Decisions about whether to use any GH-axis peptide, and the ongoing medical monitoring of anyone already doing so, including bloodwork such as IGF-1 and fasting glucose, belong with a licensed physician who can order and interpret those results in the context of an individual's actual health history and, where relevant, an actual diagnosis.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

That handoff point is not a weakness in what this chapter covered. It is the same professional discipline this course applies to every compound it examines, and it is worth carrying forward explicitly into the next chapter, because Chapter 3 introduces a second receptor system, the ghrelin-receptor agonists, whose real pharmacological interest lies partly in how it combines with everything covered here today. A learner who understands why the pulse matters in this chapter is exactly positioned to understand why that combination raises the stakes, for benefit and for risk alike, rather than simply doubling one number.

Key Takeaways

  • Growth hormone (GH) is released from pituitary somatotroph cells in response to growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH), a hypothalamic peptide that binds the GHRH receptor (GHRH-R), a class B G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that signals through cyclic AMP (cAMP) and protein kinase A (PKA). Somatostatin opposes this signal, and the alternation between the two produces GH's natural pulsatile pattern.
  • Pulsatile (physiologic) GH release, concentrated around sleep, exercise, and fasting, is mechanistically distinct from tonic (flattened, non-physiologic) elevation. Continuous stimulation can blunt receptor responsiveness and bypass the natural negative feedback loop that GH and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) normally exert on the hypothalamus.
  • Sermorelin, modified GRF (1-29), CJC-1295, and tesamorelin all act at the same GHRH-R but differ enormously in duration of action, from sermorelin's near-native, short-lived pulse to CJC-1295's multi-day, albumin-bound elevation, a direct consequence of their different chemical engineering.
  • Evidence grade varies sharply across this class. Tesamorelin is FDA-approved for HIV-associated lipodystrophy on the strength of multiple large randomized trials (Falutz et al., 2007, 2010; Stanley et al., 2012). Sermorelin has solid but narrow historical evidence in diagnosed pediatric growth hormone deficiency. CJC-1295 and modified GRF (1-29) carry pharmacologically real but thin human trial support, largely from a single study (Teichman et al., 2006).
  • Raising GH and IGF-1 carries real, class-wide risks, water retention, joint discomfort, theoretical effects on insulin sensitivity, and an unresolved, mechanistically grounded long-term concern around chronic growth-signal exposure, alongside the specific reassurance that tesamorelin's own pivotal trials did not show significant glycemic worsening in the population studied.
  • This course is education and harm reduction, not a protocol source. Understanding mechanism and evidence grade is valuable on its own and does not replace the judgment of a licensed clinician for any individual decision or health concern.
Looking Ahead

GHRH-R is only one of two receptor systems the pituitary uses to control GH release. Chapter 3 turns to the second: the ghrelin, or growth-hormone secretagogue, receptor, and the compounds built to activate it directly, ipamorelin, GHRP-2, GHRP-6, hexarelin, and the oral compound MK-677. It also covers the pharmacology this chapter has been setting up without naming outright, the real synergy between a GHRH analog and a ghrelin-receptor agonist, why combining the two produces a larger GH pulse than either alone, and why that same combination raises the stakes on everything this chapter covered about pattern, evidence grade, and risk.
