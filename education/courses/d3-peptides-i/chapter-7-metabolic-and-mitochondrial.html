The Powerhouse Compounds

Four compounds, one shared target: the mitochondrion. Only one of them has made it into a genuinely difficult human trial, and only one of them is not actually a peptide at all.

52 min read 8 cited sources

Elio found the four names on the same list, in the same online thread, priced on the same vendor page, described in nearly identical marketing language: "targets mitochondrial dysfunction," "exercise in a vial," "clinically studied compound." MOTS-c, SS-31, SLU-PP-332, 5-amino-1MQ. To Elio, 34, a data analyst who had spent the last year reading everything he could find about metabolic health after a relative was diagnosed with a rare mitochondrial disease, the four names looked like a matched set, interchangeable variations on one theme. He assumed that if one of them was real enough to be worth discussing, all four probably were, roughly equally studied, roughly equally peptides, roughly equally close to being an actual medicine. Nothing about the marketing copy he had been reading gave him any reason to think otherwise.

That assumption is the reason this chapter exists, and unwinding it is the actual work of the next several thousand words. These four compounds do share something real: they were each designed, discovered, or repurposed specifically because the mitochondrion, the organelle inside nearly every cell that manufactures the chemical energy a cell runs on, is where a huge share of metabolic disease, muscle wasting, and age-related decline in physical capacity actually originates. That shared target is a legitimate and increasingly well-studied idea in biomedicine. But the four compounds built around that one idea sit at wildly different points on the evidence ladder, they work through entirely different molecular mechanisms, and, most surprising to a reader who has only seen them listed side by side, they are not even all built from the same kind of molecule. One of the four, SLU-PP-332, is not a peptide at all. It is what chemists call a small molecule, structurally unrelated to the other three, and its regular inclusion on "peptide" vendor lists is itself a piece of marketing sleight of hand this chapter is going to correct directly.

Elio is our thread through this chapter, not because his situation is unusual, but because it is close to universal among people encountering this corner of the field for the first time. He is not being coached toward a decision about using anything, and this course will not push him, or you, toward one. What he needs, and what this chapter is built to give him, is a clear, honest map: what the mitochondrion actually does, why these four specific compounds were built around it, what each one's real mechanism is, how far the human evidence for each one actually goes, and where the genuine, still-open risks sit. By the end, the matched set on that vendor page should look like four very different entries with four very different evidence grades, because that is what they are.

The organelle every metabolic compound is chasing

Start with the target itself, because nothing in this chapter makes sense without it. The mitochondrion (plural: mitochondria) is a small, double-membraned structure present in the vast majority of human cells, often described in introductory biology as the cell's "powerhouse," a nickname that is genuinely earned rather than merely catchy. Mitochondria take the breakdown products of the food a person eats, glucose and fatty acids chief among them, and run them through a set of chemical reactions that end in the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the molecule nearly every energy-consuming process in the body spends as its immediate fuel. A muscle fiber contracting, a neuron firing, a kidney cell pumping ions against a gradient, all of it is ultimately paid for in ATP, and the overwhelming majority of that ATP is minted inside mitochondria.

The specific machinery responsible for that final, most efficient stage of energy production is called the electron transport chain (ETC), a series of large protein complexes embedded in the mitochondrion's inner membrane. The ETC passes electrons, harvested from the food-derived fuel, from one protein complex to the next, using the energy released at each step to pump protons across the inner membrane. That proton gradient is then allowed to flow back down through one final enzyme complex, a molecular turbine of sorts, which uses the flow to physically spin a rotor and stitch phosphate groups onto ADP, producing ATP. This entire process, cell biologists call oxidative phosphorylation, and it is dramatically more efficient at extracting usable energy from a given amount of fuel than the simpler, oxygen-independent backup pathway (glycolysis) that a cell can run in the mitochondrion's absence. A cell with healthy, plentiful mitochondria can do more work per calorie. A cell with damaged, sparse, or dysfunctional mitochondria cannot, no matter how much fuel is available to it.

The inner membrane that houses the ETC is not built from an ordinary phospholipid mixture. It is unusually rich in a distinctive, four-tailed lipid called cardiolipin, found almost nowhere else in the human body, which gives the inner membrane its characteristic shape, folded into deep pleats called cristae that dramatically increase its surface area, and which physically anchors and organizes the ETC protein complexes so they can work efficiently together. Cardiolipin is not a passive structural filler. It is now understood to be an active participant in oxidative phosphorylation, stabilizing the ETC complexes into larger functional groupings called supercomplexes and regulating a protein, cytochrome c, that under normal conditions ferries electrons but that, when cardiolipin is damaged or peroxidized, can flip into a destructive role that helps trigger programmed cell death. According to Szeto (2014), cardiolipin damage and depletion show up across a remarkably wide range of conditions associated with energy deficiency, aging, ischemic injury, heart failure, and several inherited mitochondrial diseases among them, which is exactly why a compound capable of protecting this one lipid became an attractive drug target in its own right, a thread this chapter picks back up in the very next section.

This is the piece of biology that makes the whole category of "metabolic and mitochondrial" compounds hang together as a category at all. When researchers say a compound is aimed at mitochondrial dysfunction, they generally mean one of a few things: making existing mitochondria run more efficiently, encouraging the cell to build more of them (a process called mitochondrial biogenesis), protecting the inner membrane's specialized lipid architecture from damage, or altering the availability of small metabolic cofactors that mitochondrial and cellular energy pathways depend on. Every compound in this chapter fits into one of those buckets, and none of them fits into more than one or two. Keeping that map in view is what will let Elio, and you, tell the difference between four compounds that share a target and four compounds that share a mechanism, which, as the rest of this chapter shows, they very much do not.

Think About It

Nearly every cell in the body contains mitochondria, but muscle, heart, brain, and kidney tissue contain unusually dense concentrations of them. Before reading further, consider what that uneven distribution might predict about which organs suffer first, and worst, when mitochondrial function declines, whether from disease, aging, or simple disuse.

MOTS-c: a peptide encoded by the mitochondrion itself

MOTS-c, short for mitochondrial open reading frame of the twelve S ribosomal RNA type-c, is a genuinely unusual molecule, and its name describes exactly where it comes from. Mitochondria carry their own small, separate loop of DNA, distinct from the DNA packaged in the cell's nucleus, and for decades that mitochondrial DNA was assumed to encode nothing but the handful of large proteins needed to build parts of the electron transport chain. According to Lee et al. (2015), a short stretch of that mitochondrial DNA, tucked inside the gene for the small ribosomal RNA subunit and previously assumed to be functionally silent, actually encodes a 16 amino acid peptide, MOTS-c, that gets translated and released into the cytoplasm and bloodstream as a genuine signaling molecule. This finding helped establish an entire small class of similarly hidden signals called mitochondrial-derived peptides (MDPs), of which MOTS-c and a related peptide called humanin are the best studied.

What MOTS-c actually does, according to Lee et al. (2015), is act as a metabolic messenger that reports on the mitochondrion's status to the rest of the cell and, once released into circulation, to distant tissues as well. Mechanistically, MOTS-c travels to skeletal muscle, where it interferes with a metabolic pathway called the folate cycle and its downstream process of building new purines (the molecular building blocks of DNA and RNA, and also, notably, of ATP itself). Blocking that pathway locally raises the ratio of AMP to ATP inside the cell, which activates an enzyme called AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), widely regarded as the cell's master fuel gauge. Once active, AMPK shifts the cell's priorities toward burning stored fuel, improving glucose uptake, and, over a longer timescale, promoting the growth of new, healthier mitochondria. In the same study, mice given MOTS-c were substantially protected against age-related and high-fat-diet-induced insulin resistance and against diet-induced weight gain, without any direct action on food intake.

This mechanism is what earns MOTS-c its "exercise-mimetic" reputation, a phrase that is doing quite a lot of work and deserves to be examined honestly rather than repeated uncritically. AMPK activation is one of the genuine, well-established downstream effects of vigorous physical exercise itself, part of why a hard training session improves insulin sensitivity for hours or days afterward. MOTS-c administration in animal studies reproduces a piece of that same signaling cascade pharmacologically, which is a legitimately interesting finding and the reason the "exercise in a vial" language keeps circulating. But reproducing one downstream signal that exercise also happens to produce is not the same thing as reproducing everything exercise does. Real exercise involves mechanical loading of bone and connective tissue, cardiovascular conditioning, neuromuscular coordination, and dozens of other signaling pathways running in parallel, none of which a single peptide targeting one node of one pathway can be assumed to replicate.

It is also worth being precise about where this evidence currently stands, because the marketing language around MOTS-c tends to blur it. According to Merry et al. (2020), reviewing the broader mitochondrial-derived peptide field, the insulin-sensitizing and metabolically protective effects of MOTS-c described by Lee et al. (2015) and in subsequent work are consistently observed in rodent and cell-culture models, and circulating MOTS-c levels in humans correlate with metabolic health, exercise status, and age in observational studies. What does not yet exist is a completed, controlled human clinical trial testing injected or otherwise administered MOTS-c as a therapeutic for insulin resistance, obesity, or any other condition. The evidence grade here is honestly stated as preclinical, strong and mechanistically coherent within animal and cell models, genuinely promising as a research direction, but not yet validated in a human trial of the kind this chapter will describe for elamipretide in the next section.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common claim holds that MOTS-c is "clinically studied" because it appears in published, peer-reviewed research. Published research and a completed human clinical trial are not the same thing. The MOTS-c studies underlying its insulin-sensitizing reputation, including the discovery study by Lee et al. (2015), were conducted in mice and in isolated human cells, not in a controlled trial of people taking the peptide. Peer-reviewed and preclinical are both accurate descriptions at once.

SS-31 and elamipretide: propping up the inner membrane

The compound most people encounter under two different names, SS-31 and elamipretide, is in fact one molecule at two different stages of its own history. SS-31 was the laboratory research code, short for Szeto-Schiller peptide 31, named for the two scientists who developed it, and elamipretide is the compound's later, formal international nonproprietary drug name, adopted once it advanced into pharmaceutical development. Both names refer to the same short, four-amino-acid peptide, and seeing both used interchangeably in the literature and in patient-facing trial materials is normal rather than a sign of two different products. Structurally, elamipretide belongs to a small family the field calls aromatic-cationic peptides, engineered specifically to be small enough and charged in exactly the right way to concentrate at the inner mitochondrial membrane rather than being diluted evenly throughout the cell.

Its mechanism connects directly back to the biology laid out earlier in this chapter. According to Szeto (2014), elamipretide binds selectively to cardiolipin through a combination of electrostatic attraction (cardiolipin carries a negative charge that the peptide's positively charged residues are drawn to) and hydrophobic interaction with cardiolipin's fatty tails. Once bound, it appears to stabilize cardiolipin's structural role at the cristae, protecting it against the kind of oxidative damage that, left unchecked, converts cytochrome c from an electron carrier into a destructive peroxidase and helps trigger cell death. In animal models of everything from ischemia-reperfusion injury to heart failure to age-related decline, this stabilization has been associated with better-preserved ATP production and reduced tissue injury. The elegance of the mechanism, one small peptide binding one specific lipid that the entire ETC structurally depends on, is a large part of why elamipretide became the furthest-advanced compound in this entire chapter.

And it genuinely is the furthest advanced, by a wide margin. Elamipretide has been tested in real, randomized, placebo-controlled human clinical trials, not just cell and animal models, in several serious mitochondrial and cardiac conditions. The most encouraging results so far come from Barth syndrome, a rare, inherited mitochondrial disease driven by a defect in cardiolipin remodeling itself, which makes it about as direct a match between mechanism and disease as this field currently offers. According to Thompson et al. (2024), reporting the 168 week open-label extension of the TAZPOWER trial, patients with Barth syndrome who continued daily elamipretide showed sustained improvement on a standard six-minute walk test, reduced fatigue scores, and favorable trends in cardiac imaging measures and in the blood ratio of damaged to healthy cardiolipin, an outcome directly traceable to the peptide's proposed mechanism.

The picture is honestly more mixed once the trials broaden out to a wider mitochondrial disease population. According to Karaa et al. (2024), reporting a post hoc analysis of the MMPOWER-3 trial in adults with primary mitochondrial myopathy, the trial's overall, genotypically diverse study population did not show a statistically significant benefit of elamipretide over placebo on its primary walk-test endpoint. When the same researchers broke the population down by the specific genetic cause of each patient's mitochondrial disease, one subgroup, patients whose disease stemmed from defects in the machinery that maintains and repairs mitochondrial DNA itself, showed a walk-test improvement that trended toward significance, and a further subgroup within that group reached statistical significance. This is precisely the kind of result that separates honest, rigorous clinical development from marketing copy: a real trial can produce a genuinely mixed, genotype-dependent result, and a follow-up trial designed around exactly that finding, rather than either declaring victory or quietly disappearing, is the appropriate scientific response, and is in fact what is happening next in this compound's development.

"This peptide technology platform represents a paradigm shift in targeting the fundamental cause of cellular energy failure for age-related degenerative diseases."

Hazel H. Szeto, Protein and Peptide Letters (2018)
Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A frequent oversimplification treats elamipretide as "clinically proven." The honest picture is more specific than that phrase allows: elamipretide has produced durable, mechanistically coherent benefit in Barth syndrome over a multi-year open-label extension, and a mixed, genotype-dependent result in a broader trial of primary mitochondrial myopathy that did not meet its overall primary endpoint. Both of those facts are true at once, and citing only the positive result, or only the negative one, misrepresents where this compound actually stands.

SLU-PP-332: an exercise mimetic that is not a peptide

SLU-PP-332 deserves to be named plainly for what it is before anything else is said about it: a small molecule, a compound built through conventional medicinal chemistry rather than assembled as a chain of amino acids, and therefore not a peptide by any structural definition of the word, no matter how often it gets bundled onto peptide vendor lists alongside MOTS-c and elamipretide. This is not a minor technicality. It shapes how the compound is made, how it is dosed (small molecules are frequently viable as oral compounds, since they are not automatically destroyed by digestive enzymes the way an unprotected peptide chain generally is), and how its safety profile has to be evaluated, since small molecules and peptides carry different classes of off-target risk. Grouping SLU-PP-332 with genuine peptides obscures a real chemical distinction that matters for exactly the kind of evidence-grade reasoning this course is built around.

What SLU-PP-332 actually is, mechanistically, is a synthetic agonist of a group of proteins called estrogen-related receptors (ERRs), a small family of nuclear receptors that, despite the name, do not bind estrogen itself and instead sit constitutively active inside cells that rely heavily on mitochondrial energy production, skeletal muscle, heart, and brown fat prominent among them. ERRs work as transcription factors, switching on genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and the broader transcriptional program that a cell normally activates during and after sustained aerobic exercise. According to Billon et al. (2024), administering SLU-PP-332 to diet-induced obese mice reproduced several of exercise's core metabolic benefits directly, increased energy expenditure, increased fatty acid oxidation, reduced fat mass accumulation, and improved insulin sensitivity, all without the animals actually exercising more.

That result is genuinely striking, and it is also, once again, entirely a mouse result. The evidence for SLU-PP-332 at the time of this chapter's writing consists of cell-based and rodent studies examining its effects on ERR activation, mitochondrial gene expression, and metabolic parameters in diet-induced obesity and metabolic syndrome models. No completed human clinical trial data exists for SLU-PP-332. That places it, honestly, at an earlier stage than elamipretide and roughly alongside MOTS-c on the evidence ladder, preclinical, mechanistically well characterized within animal models, and not yet tested in the controlled human trials that would be required before any claim about its effects in a person could be responsibly made.

The "exercise mimetic" framing that both MOTS-c and SLU-PP-332 attract is worth pausing on directly, because it carries an implicit promise that neither compound's evidence base currently supports: that a molecule can substitute for the cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and neurological adaptations that structured physical activity produces across an entire body, not merely inside the specific metabolic pathway a given compound happens to target. AMPK activation (MOTS-c's route) and ERR activation (SLU-PP-332's route) are two different molecular doors into an overlapping but not identical piece of exercise's downstream signaling. Reproducing a downstream signal pharmacologically is a legitimate and interesting research strategy. It is not evidence that either compound reproduces exercise as a whole, and no publication cited in this chapter makes that broader claim on the animal data alone.

Think About It

Two different compounds, one a 16 amino acid peptide, the other a synthetic small molecule with no amino acid chain at all, both get marketed as "exercise mimetics" and both act on mitochondrial energy pathways. Before reading further, consider what it means that two structurally unrelated molecules can converge on overlapping downstream biology, and what that convergence does and does not tell you about how well either one has actually been tested.

5-amino-1MQ: starving an enzyme to spare a fuel source

The fourth compound in this chapter's lineup works through a different logic entirely, not by directly stabilizing mitochondrial structure or switching on mitochondrial biogenesis, but by inhibiting a single metabolic enzyme and letting the downstream consequences of that inhibition do the work. 5-amino-1MQ is a small molecule, structurally related to nicotinamide, engineered to selectively and potently inhibit an enzyme called nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT), an enzyme found at elevated levels in the liver and white adipose tissue of people and animals with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and broader metabolic syndrome.

NNMT's normal job is to attach a methyl group onto nicotinamide, the same nicotinamide that also serves as a direct precursor for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), a molecule central to virtually every redox reaction in cellular metabolism, including the reactions mitochondria run to generate ATP. To perform that methylation, NNMT consumes a separate molecule called S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the cell's primary methyl-group donor, used across a huge range of biological processes beyond this one reaction. According to Sun et al. (2024), reviewing NNMT's role in metabolic disease, elevated NNMT activity in fat and liver tissue depletes both nicotinamide, starving the NAD+ salvage pathway, and SAM, and this depletion is mechanistically linked to increased fat storage (lipogenesis) and reduced cellular energy expenditure. Inhibiting NNMT, in other words, is a strategy aimed at preserving two metabolically valuable cofactors that an overactive enzyme would otherwise consume.

The direct evidence for 5-amino-1MQ specifically, as opposed to the broader NNMT-as-a-target hypothesis, comes from a smaller but mechanistically clean body of animal work. According to Neelakantan et al. (2017), describing the development and characterization of a related series of selective, membrane-permeable NNMT inhibitors including this compound class, treatment of diet-induced obese mice reduced body weight and white adipose tissue mass, decreased individual fat cell size, and lowered plasma cholesterol, without any measurable change in the animals' food intake, consistent with an effect on energy expenditure and fat storage rather than appetite. Selectivity testing in the same work showed the compounds did not meaningfully inhibit other structurally related methyltransferases or enzymes in the NAD+ salvage pathway, an important finding for a drug strategy that depends on hitting one enzyme precisely rather than disrupting methylation broadly.

As with MOTS-c and SLU-PP-332, the honest evidence grade for 5-amino-1MQ stops at the mouse cage. There is no published, completed human clinical trial testing 5-amino-1MQ for weight loss, metabolic syndrome, or any other indication as of this writing. The mechanistic story, an enzyme that is overactive specifically in metabolic disease, consuming two cofactors metabolically valuable enough that sparing them measurably shifts fat storage and energy expenditure in animal models, is coherent and worth taking seriously as a research direction. It is not, on its own, evidence of what selectively inhibiting NNMT does in a human body over months or years, a gap this chapter's next section returns to directly.

Think About It

NNMT inhibition is proposed to work by sparing NAD+ and SAM from being consumed by an overactive enzyme. Both of those molecules are used in dozens of other reactions throughout the body, not only the fat-storage pathway 5-amino-1MQ is marketed against. Before the next section, consider what questions a careful researcher would need to answer about a compound that shifts the availability of two cofactors used almost everywhere in the cell.

The risks and unknowns of rewiring the power plant

Every compound in this chapter is attractive precisely because the mitochondrion sits so close to the center of nearly all cellular activity. That centrality is also exactly why manipulating mitochondrial pathways from the outside carries a distinct category of risk, different in character from a drug that acts on a single, narrowly expressed receptor. A compound that shifts AMPK activity, ERR signaling, cardiolipin stability, or NAD+ and SAM availability is not tuning one dial in one tissue. It is reaching into a control system that every energy-consuming cell in the body depends on, and the honest unknowns here deserve the same directness this chapter has applied to the mechanisms themselves.

The first unknown is simply duration. Every piece of animal evidence described in this chapter comes from studies measured in weeks, occasionally a few months. None of it establishes what continuous activation of AMPK, continuous ERR agonism, or sustained NNMT inhibition does to a body over years, which is the timescale over which most people interested in metabolic or performance benefits would actually want to use a compound like this. Chronic activation of pathways that evolved to respond to intermittent stress, a bout of exercise, a period of fasting, is not guaranteed to produce a proportionally larger benefit when sustained indefinitely, and in several other areas of metabolic pharmacology, chronic pathway activation has instead produced diminishing returns, compensatory downregulation, or entirely new problems that a short study was never positioned to catch.

The second unknown concerns specificity and off-target signaling. ERR receptors, the direct target of SLU-PP-332, are expressed across a wider range of tissue than muscle and fat, including some contexts where nuclear receptor activation has a documented relationship to cell growth signaling, a relationship this course does not have controlled human safety data to resolve one way or the other for this specific compound. NNMT inhibition redirects the availability of SAM, a methyl donor used far beyond fat metabolism, including in DNA methylation and neurotransmitter synthesis, and the downstream consequences of shifting that one cofactor's availability across an entire body have not been mapped in any published human study. None of this means either mechanism is dangerous. It means neither mechanism has been followed far enough, in the right kind of study, to say with confidence that it is not.

The third and most practical unknown for anyone actually encountering these compounds outside a hospital or a research lab is sourcing and purity. Because MOTS-c, SLU-PP-332, and 5-amino-1MQ are not approved medications, they are not manufactured, tested, or labeled under any pharmaceutical quality standard, and material sold under these names has no guaranteed relationship to what was actually used in the published animal studies this chapter cites. This is a real and separate category of risk from the pharmacological unknowns above, and a later chapter in this course addresses grey-market sourcing, contamination, and legality directly. The two problems compound each other rather than canceling out: even a compound with a genuinely promising mechanism carries added risk when what is actually being taken is unverified.

Elamipretide is a partial exception to some, though not all, of this. As an actual investigational and, in specific rare-disease programs, clinically administered drug, its manufacturing, dosing, and safety monitoring in trials have been subject to real regulatory oversight, and its adverse-event profile in those trials, according to Thompson et al. (2024), has been dominated by manageable injection-site reactions rather than the kind of systemic red flags that would halt a program. But even here, the mixed MMPOWER-3 result described earlier is itself a genuine unknown resolving in real time, not a settled matter, and the compound remains, outside its specific trial and rare-disease access programs, unavailable as an approved general-use medication.

Think About It

A compound with a coherent mechanism, a positive animal result, and no completed human safety data occupies a genuinely different risk category from one with a completed, controlled human trial, even a trial with a mixed result. Before the next section, consider why "it has a good mechanism" and "it has been shown safe in humans" are two entirely different claims, and why marketing language so often lets the first one stand in for the second.

Case study: Elio and the matched set that was not one

Case

Elio is 34, works as a data analyst, and has no diagnosed metabolic or mitochondrial condition of his own. His interest in this topic began when a relative was diagnosed with a rare mitochondrial disease and started, under specialist care, an elamipretide trial program. Reading about his relative's treatment led Elio down a much broader online rabbit hole, where MOTS-c, SS-31, SLU-PP-332, and 5-amino-1MQ appeared together, repeatedly, as a single "mitochondrial optimization" category. He arrived at this course wanting to understand what his relative was actually receiving, and, separately, what the other three compounds he kept seeing listed alongside it actually were.

Running Elio's four names back through this chapter resolves his central confusion directly: these are not four versions of one thing. His relative's compound, elamipretide, is a peptide with a specific, well-characterized mechanism, binding cardiolipin at the inner mitochondrial membrane, and it is the only one of the four with completed, controlled human trial data behind it, data that is genuinely encouraging in Barth syndrome and genuinely mixed in the broader primary mitochondrial myopathy population described by Karaa et al. (2024). Understanding that mixed result, rather than either an unqualified success or a hidden failure, is exactly the kind of literacy this course exists to build, and it gave Elio a far more accurate picture of his relative's situation than the forum threads he had been reading, which tended to flatten the finding into one direction or the other depending on the poster's agenda.

MOTS-c, the second name on his list, turned out to be a real and mechanistically fascinating discovery, a peptide the mitochondrion's own DNA encodes and releases as a metabolic signal, described first by Lee et al. (2015). But its insulin-sensitizing and exercise-mimetic effects remain, honestly, mouse-cage findings, not the "clinically studied" claim the vendor page Elio had been reading implied. SLU-PP-332 turned out to be the biggest surprise of the four: not a peptide at all, a small molecule ERR agonist with its own separate, animal-only evidence base described by Billon et al. (2024), grouped alongside genuine peptides purely by marketing convenience rather than any structural or regulatory logic. And 5-amino-1MQ, the fourth name, worked through yet another distinct mechanism again, inhibiting one specific enzyme to spare two metabolically valuable cofactors, with its own separate mouse evidence from Neelakantan et al. (2017), and its own separate absence of human trial data.

What Elio walked away with was not a verdict on any of the four compounds, and it was not a recommendation about his relative's treatment, which remains, appropriately, a decision between his relative and the specialists running that trial program. What he walked away with was the ability to read that original vendor page correctly: four names sharing a target, the mitochondrion, but sitting at three entirely different points on the evidence ladder, built from at least two entirely different kinds of molecule, and carrying at least four entirely different, and separately honest, evidence-grade descriptions. That is the actual content of this chapter, and it is a far more useful thing to carry forward than any single fact about any one compound on its own.

Where this chapter stops: mechanism is not permission

This course is education and harm reduction, and this chapter has stayed inside that frame throughout. We have explained what the mitochondrion does, why each of these four compounds was built or repurposed to act on it, how each one's mechanism actually works, and, as precisely as the published literature allows, how far the human evidence for each one genuinely goes. That last part matters more here than in almost any other corner of this course, because the honest evidence grade for three of these four compounds, MOTS-c, SLU-PP-332, and 5-amino-1MQ, stops at the mouse cage, and the honest evidence grade for the fourth, elamipretide, is a genuinely mixed clinical picture still being resolved through ongoing trials rather than a settled success story.

None of that mechanistic detail, however coherent it sounds, and none of the genuinely promising animal data described throughout this chapter, amounts to a safety or efficacy verdict for any individual person. A compound's molecular logic can be entirely sound, cardiolipin protection, AMPK activation, ERR agonism, NNMT inhibition are all real, well-described biological phenomena, without that logic telling a specific person with a specific body, a specific health history, and specific goals whether a given compound is appropriate, safe, or even legal for them to obtain. That gap between mechanism and permission is not a formality bolted onto the science. It follows directly from everything this chapter has actually shown: even elamipretide, the single most rigorously tested compound in this entire lineup, produced a mixed result in its broadest human trial, which is exactly the kind of outcome that only a real, controlled study, not a mechanism diagram, can reveal.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

That boundary is exactly what served Elio well. His relative's care sits, appropriately, with the specialists running that trial program, people with access to genotype data, ongoing monitoring, and the specific clinical context this chapter's biology can inform but never substitute for. His own curiosity about the other three compounds is well served by exactly what this chapter gave him: an accurate, honestly graded map of what each one is, how each one is proposed to work, and how far each one has actually been tested, without that map ever pretending to be a recommendation. Carrying that same discipline forward, distinguishing a compelling mechanism from a demonstrated human outcome, distinguishing a peptide from a small molecule marketed as one, distinguishing a positive trial from a mixed one, is the actual skill this chapter was built to teach, and it will keep mattering as this course moves into the HPG-axis peptides in the next chapter and the grey-market and legal realities that close this module after that.

Key Takeaways

  • The mitochondrion generates the ATP nearly every energy-consuming process in the body runs on, largely through the electron transport chain (ETC) housed in its cardiolipin-rich inner membrane. Compounds described as "metabolic" or "mitochondrial" generally act by improving existing mitochondrial efficiency, promoting new mitochondrial growth, protecting the inner membrane's lipid architecture, or altering key metabolic cofactors, and rarely more than one of those at once.
  • MOTS-c is a genuine mitochondrial-derived peptide (MDP), encoded within mitochondrial DNA itself, that activates AMPK in skeletal muscle and improved insulin sensitivity and reduced obesity in animal models, according to Lee et al. (2015). Its human evidence remains preclinical.
  • SS-31, formally elamipretide, binds cardiolipin at the inner mitochondrial membrane and is the furthest-advanced compound in this chapter, with a durable positive result in Barth syndrome (Thompson et al., 2024) and a mixed, genotype-dependent result in a broader primary mitochondrial myopathy trial (Karaa et al., 2024).
  • SLU-PP-332 is a small molecule ERR (estrogen-related receptor) agonist, not a peptide, that reproduces several exercise-associated metabolic effects in obese mice, according to Billon et al. (2024). It has no completed human trial data and does not belong on a peptide list by chemical structure.
  • 5-amino-1MQ inhibits NNMT (nicotinamide N-methyltransferase), sparing NAD+ and SAM from an enzyme overactive in metabolic disease, and reduced weight and fat mass in obese mice, according to Neelakantan et al. (2017). It remains preclinical, with no human trial data.
  • Manipulating mitochondrial and metabolic pathways from the outside carries unknowns distinct from single-receptor drugs: unstudied chronic-use effects, off-target signaling in tissues beyond the intended one, and, for the non-approved compounds, unregulated sourcing and purity. A sound mechanism is not evidence of human safety, and this course does not provide dosing, sourcing, or a recommendation for any individual.
Looking Ahead

The compounds in this chapter act on the machinery that powers nearly every cell in the body. Chapter 8 shifts to a much more specific axis, the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) system, and the peptides, kisspeptin and gonadorelin among them, that act directly on it. The evidence-grading discipline built in this chapter, mechanism first, human trial status stated honestly, structure named precisely, carries forward directly into that next system.
