Chapter 5 : Motion Is the Medicine (Mostly)

Active recovery genuinely helps how you feel and helps between efforts, but easy has to stay easy, and it never outranks sleep or nutrition.

What the Evidence Actually Supports

Perceived soreness / fatigueSmall-to-large decrease with active recovery (Dupuy et al., 2018). Real, but massage often beats it on the same outcomes.
Lactate clearanceFastest near 80 to 100% of lactate threshold, not at a gentle drift (Menzies et al., 2010). Not a reliable recovery marker (Ortiz et al., 2019; Halson, 2014).
Same-day cool-downLargely ineffective for next-day performance or injury prevention; does not blunt long-term adaptation either (Van Hooren & Peake, 2018).
Between-effort recoveryShort sessions (about 6 to 10 minutes) between bouts in a competition or session show real performance benefit (Ortiz et al., 2019; Nalbandian et al., 2017).
Deep tissue adaptationNot meaningfully accelerated by active recovery. It supports the process; it does not drive it.

The Intensity Trap

  • Active recovery is only recovery if it stays genuinely easy.
  • Push it past a low ceiling and it becomes another training stimulus, not protection for the recovery window.
  • Test: can you comfortably hold a conversation, and do you finish more refreshed than worked?
  • No answer to that test means it was not recovery, whatever the training plan called it.

The Finite Budget

  • Recovery draws on one shared account: time, energy, attention, and physiological capacity.
  • Sleep and nutrition are the foundational levers with the largest effects.
  • An ambitious "recovery" session can crowd out sleep or fuel and produce a net negative, even though it felt virtuous.
  • The budget shrinks on heavy weeks; the same session that was harmless last week may not be this week.

When Rest Beats Movement

  • Accumulated sleep debt: the marginal minutes are worth more spent horizontal.
  • Genuine energy depletion (after a long event or under-fueling): another withdrawal the system cannot afford.
  • Early window after an injury or a joint or tendon flare: belongs to a rehabilitation plan, not a generic active-recovery habit.

The Overtraining Continuum

  • Functional overreaching : planned, temporary overload that supercompensates into fitness.
  • Non-functional overreaching : overload without adequate recovery, heading the wrong way.
  • Overtraining syndrome : prolonged underperformance that can take months to resolve (Meeusen et al., 2012).
Where education ends   This builds understanding of what active recovery actually does, not a license to diagnose or treat. Sharp or worsening pain, pain at rest or at night, joint instability, or a cluster of symptoms like unexplained weight loss are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
