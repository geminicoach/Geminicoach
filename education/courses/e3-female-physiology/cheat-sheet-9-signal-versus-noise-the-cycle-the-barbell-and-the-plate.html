Chapter 9 : Signal Versus Noise

Cycle-syncing marketing outruns the evidence. Grading the claims honestly reveals where the ground is thin and where it is genuinely firm.

The Cycle, Quick Anchor

Follicular phaseFirst day of bleeding to ovulation. Estrogen rises from its low point toward its mid-cycle peak.
OvulationRelease of an egg, typically around the cycle midpoint.
Luteal phaseOvulation to the next period. Progesterone rises then falls; estrogen makes a smaller second rise.

What The Evidence Says

  • Phase-based lifting : NOISE. Umbrella review (Colenso-Semple et al., 2023) finds scant, low-quality, inconsistent evidence. Largest performance meta-analysis finds at most trivial effects.
  • Iron and the bleed : SIGNAL. Basic accounting: bleeding removes blood, blood contains iron, this repeats for decades.
  • Appetite shift by phase : DEPENDS. Direction has support (lower follicular, higher luteal); magnitude is not yet quantifiable.
  • Calcium and vitamin D : SIGNAL, but steady. Needs do not meaningfully shift week to week; consistent intake over months matters most.

The Measurement Problem

  • Only 44 percent of exercise studies verify cycle phase with actual hormone testing (Janse de Jonge et al., 2019).
  • Calendar-only counting can misclassify anovulatory or luteal-deficient cycles as normal ones.
  • Small samples plus a bias toward positive, publishable findings produce a literature that looks busy but is fragile.

The Decision Frame

  • How was phase determined: blood, or just calendar?
  • How many participants, and were ovulatory cycles confirmed?
  • Consistent with the higher-level reviews, or a contradicting outlier?
  • Is the effect large enough to change what a real person does, or a trivial blip?
  • Have you actually tracked yourself, or are you assuming a pattern a program told you to expect?

Food-First Iron

  • Heme iron (red meat, animal flesh): absorbed at roughly 15 to 35 percent.
  • Non-heme iron (lentils, spinach, fortified grains): absorbed at under 10 percent; vitamin C helps, tea/coffee/calcium can blunt it.
  • Low-iron signs worth a blood test, not a guess: persistent fatigue, effort feeling harder than usual, breathlessness, restless legs, pale skin or brittle nails.
Recognize and refer   Prolonged absence of menstruation can signal amenorrhea or Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport and warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician, not a shrug. Iron screening, interpretation, and any supplementation decision belong to a clinician too; too much iron carries real risks. Contraception, amenorrhea, and postpartum questions are clinical territory throughout this course.
