The Fifth Vital Sign

Reading the menstrual cycle as a signal of whole-system health, without overreading a single month.

27 min read 8 cited sources

When a nurse takes your pulse, your blood pressure, your temperature, and counts your breaths, nobody thinks those numbers are the whole story of your health. They are quick, cheap windows into whether the machinery underneath is running. A resting heart rate of forty can mean an endurance athlete or a failing conduction system, and the number alone does not tell you which. It tells you where to look. That is exactly the spirit in which a growing body of clinical opinion asks us to read the menstrual cycle: as a recurring, low-cost, remarkably informative window into whether the whole reproductive axis is well fed, well rested, and doing its job.

This chapter teaches you to read that window with both curiosity and restraint, and to understand what the tools people use to observe it, a thermometer, a strip of paper, an honest look at a day's cervical mucus, can and cannot add to that reading. A regularly ovulatory cycle says something real. A single strange month usually does not. Learning the difference is most of the skill.

Throughout this chapter you will follow one learner, Odalys, a 33-year-old primary school teacher and recreational runner who came off a hormonal intrauterine device eight months ago. She and her partner are not trying to conceive yet, but they are thinking about it within the next year or two, and Odalys wanted to understand her own rhythm before she needed it for anything. For four months she has been charting basal body temperature, checking her cervical mucus most days, and using over-the-counter test strips that detect a surge in luteinizing hormone. Her tracking app is thorough, encouraging, and, on one particular month, alarmed: it flagged her cycle as irregular and suggested she "look into possible causes." Odalys is not a patient in this course and she is not a case to be solved. She is a stand-in for the ordinary confusion that self-tracking produces once the data starts arriving faster than the framework needed to read it, and by the end of this chapter you will be better equipped to read her charts than the app that scared her.

Why "vital sign" is more than a slogan

In 2015 the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, working through its Committee on Adolescent Health Care and endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, formally asked clinicians to treat the menstrual cycle as an additional vital sign in girls and adolescents (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2015). Their argument was pragmatic: just as an abnormal blood pressure or heart rate can surface a serious condition early, an abnormal menstrual pattern can flag a health concern that would otherwise go unnoticed for years.

A 2025 systematic review pushed the idea from slogan to criteria. Rosen Vollmar and colleagues (2025) checked the cycle against the standard tests a measurement must pass to earn the title vital sign: does it predict health outcomes, indicate when treatment is needed, let you monitor a clinical course, help identify pathology, affirm wellness when it is normal, and respond to environmental exposures? The cycle does all six, from the first period through the years after the last one. That is a high bar, and it is worth pausing on why the cycle clears it.

Each criterion has a concrete face. A shortening cycle in the mid-forties predicts the approach of menopause. A cycle that stops for several months signals when a workup for pregnancy, thyroid disease, or a pituitary problem is warranted. A cycle that steadies again over the course of treatment for an eating disorder lets a clinician monitor recovery without another invasive test. A cycle that will not regularize despite months of a reasonable intervention flags that something else is still wrong. A cycle that arrives like clockwork affirms that the reproductive axis, and a fair amount of the rest of the body's regulatory machinery, is currently well. And a cycle that changes after starting a new medication, a large change in body weight, or a move to a demanding new routine shows the axis responding to its environment in real time. Six criteria, six everyday moments, and not one of them requires more than a calendar and attention.

The reason the cycle clears all six is architectural. A predictable, ovulatory cycle is not produced by the ovaries alone. It is the visible output of a conversation that runs from the hypothalamus in the brain, to the pituitary gland just below it, to the ovaries, and back again. This loop is called the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, or the reproductive axis for short. The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone, abbreviated GnRH, in carefully timed pulses. Those pulses tell the pituitary when to release its two gonadotropins. The ovaries respond, and their hormones feed back to fine-tune the brain. When you see a regular cycle with genuine ovulation, you are seeing evidence that this entire chain has enough energy, enough rest, and enough signaling integrity to complete a month-long negotiation on schedule. That is why the cycle reports on the whole system and not just the uterus.

What the gonadotropins and ovulation actually tell you

Two pituitary hormones do most of the timing work, and they are worth refreshing because their names get used loosely. Follicle-stimulating hormone rises at the transition from one cycle's luteal phase into the next cycle's follicular phase. As the name says, it stimulates a cohort of follicles to begin growing and prompts them to secrete inhibin B. In the mid-follicular phase, one follicle is selected to become dominant while the others regress (Mihm et al., 2011). Luteinizing hormone, commonly abbreviated LH, is the trigger. A sharp surge in luteinizing hormone is what actually causes the dominant follicle to release its egg. Ovulation follows the surge by roughly a day.

Here is what these hormones honestly indicate, and where folk wisdom oversells them. A luteinizing hormone surge is the best widely available real-time sign that ovulation is about to happen, which is why urine-based ovulation predictor kits detect it. But a surge is a prediction, not a confirmation. It tells you the trigger has been pulled; it does not prove the egg was released or that a functional corpus luteum formed afterward. Genuine confirmation of ovulation comes from downstream evidence, most reliably a sustained rise in progesterone in the luteal phase, and it is that confirmed ovulation, not the surge alone, that certifies the axis completed its work.

The most important myth to retire is that a regular, normal-length cycle is automatically an ovulatory one. It is not. In a population-based study of 3,709 spontaneously menstruating women in Norway, none using hormonal contraception, Prior and colleagues (2015) assessed 1,545 women in their presumed luteal phase and found that only 63.3 percent were ovulatory. Thirty-seven percent were anovulatory in the month studied. Strikingly, the ovulatory and anovulatory groups did not differ meaningfully in age, body mass index, cycle length, age at first period, smoking, or self-reported health. In other words, a woman with a textbook-looking cycle can have a month without ovulation and show no outward tell. Regularity is reassuring, but it is not proof.

This matters for how you talk about fertility. Ovulation is necessary for natural conception, so confirmed ovulation is genuinely good news about that month. But a cycle is a snapshot of function, not a guarantee of outcome. It does not measure egg quality, tubal patency, a partner's contribution, or the many other factors conception depends on. Reading a cycle can tell you the axis is working. It cannot tell any specific person that they will conceive, or when. That last leap is clinical territory, and it belongs to a clinician who can order the right tests and see the whole person.

The widget makes a point that calendar math tends to hide. The fertile phase spans roughly the five days before ovulation through the day of ovulation itself, because sperm can survive several days in the reproductive tract while the egg is viable for less than a day (Mihm et al., 2011). If you knew the exact day of ovulation, that window would be tight and confident. But you rarely know it in advance. The moment cycle length varies from month to month, the plausible ovulation day smears across a range, and the honest fertile window widens with it. This is not a flaw in your estimate. It is the true state of the knowledge, and pretending otherwise is how well-meaning advice goes wrong.

Reading the body's own signals: what fertility awareness methods actually track

The gonadotropins described above are invisible without a blood draw, but their downstream effects are not. For decades, a cluster of techniques collectively called fertility awareness based methods, abbreviated FAM, have let people observe those effects directly, using nothing more than a thermometer, their own attention, and, more recently, a small paper test strip. Vigil and colleagues (2012), reviewing the physiological basis of these methods, frame fertility awareness as fundamental self-knowledge, useful whether someone is trying to conceive, trying to avoid conceiving, or simply trying to understand a body that has started sending confusing signals. Three signs do most of the work: basal body temperature, cervical mucus, and a home test for the luteinizing hormone surge. Each one tracks a different point in the hormonal sequence you just learned, and each one has a different strength and a different blind spot. Learning what each sign can and cannot tell you is the difference between using these tools with genuine skill and using them to manufacture false certainty, which is precisely the trap Odalys's app nearly set for her.

Basal body temperature: the after-the-fact confirmation

Basal body temperature, abbreviated BBT, is your resting body temperature measured first thing in the morning, before you rise, eat, or even sit up. The signal it captures is thermogenic: progesterone, released in quantity by the corpus luteum only after ovulation has occurred, acts on the hypothalamic temperature-regulating center and nudges resting temperature up by roughly three to five tenths of a degree Fahrenheit. Charted daily, a normal ovulatory cycle produces a recognizable two-phase, or biphasic, pattern: a lower plateau through the follicular phase, a shift upward around the time of ovulation, and a higher plateau through the luteal phase that holds until the next period, when it drops again.

That shift is useful precisely because of what it confirms and precisely because of when it arrives. A sustained temperature rise is one of the more accessible ways to get retrospective evidence that progesterone rose and, by extension, that ovulation likely occurred that cycle, echoing the surge-versus-confirmation distinction from earlier in this chapter. What it cannot do is predict ovulation before it happens. By the time the temperature climbs, the egg has already been released, which makes basal body temperature charting excellent for confirming a pattern over months and nearly useless for telling someone the fertile window is open today. It is also a noisy signal in practice: a late night, a fever, alcohol the evening before, an inconsistent wake time, or a poor night of sleep can all shift a single reading enough to obscure the pattern. This is why it is read as a trend across a chart, never as a single morning's number, and why one odd reading, on its own, means almost nothing.

Cervical mucus: the real-time forecast

Where basal body temperature looks backward, cervical mucus looks forward, because it responds to rising estrogen in the days before ovulation rather than to progesterone afterward. As a dominant follicle matures and estrogen climbs, the mucus produced by the cervix changes in a predictable sequence: from dry or minimal just after menstruation, to a cloudy, sticky, tacky texture through the early follicular phase, and finally, in the day or two before ovulation, to a clear, slippery, stretchable quality often compared to raw egg white. This so-called peak mucus corresponds to the highest, most fertile-friendly estrogen output of the cycle, when the cervix is most permissive to sperm survival and transport. After ovulation, under the influence of progesterone, the mucus reverts abruptly to a thicker, less hospitable state.

Vigil and colleagues (2012), synthesizing decades of research on the physiological basis of fertility awareness, describe cervical mucus observation as one of the more informative self-observed signs precisely because, unlike a temperature shift, it changes ahead of ovulation rather than after it, giving a genuine forward-looking signal rather than a retrospective one. That is also its main practical difficulty: reading mucus well takes practice, the texture can be genuinely ambiguous day to day, and factors unrelated to fertility, including certain medications, vaginal infections, and even hydration, can alter what someone observes. Accuracy tends to sharpen considerably with a few cycles of practice, which is part of why formal fertility awareness instruction historically involved a teacher reviewing charts with a learner rather than a one-time explanation handed over and left alone.

LH testing: catching the trigger

Luteinizing hormone testing works differently again, because it does not infer a hormonal shift from a downstream body change, it measures the hormone directly, in urine, using the same antibody-based strip technology as a home pregnancy test. As described earlier in this chapter, a surge in luteinizing hormone is what triggers ovulation, roughly a day later, so a positive strip is the most immediate, real-time signal of the three: it announces that ovulation is imminent rather than confirming, after the fact, that it already happened. This is why LH strips are popular with people actively trying to time intercourse around ovulation, and why LH testing complements basal body temperature so well when both are charted together: the strip flags the imminent event, and the temperature shift a few days later confirms it occurred.

The evidence on how much this actually helps is more measured than the marketing around ovulation kits suggests, and it is worth reading honestly. Gibbons and colleagues (2023), in a Cochrane systematic review of seven randomized trials involving 2,464 women or couples trying to conceive, found that using a urinary luteinizing hormone test probably increases the chance of a live birth compared with timing intercourse without any ovulation prediction, though the certainty of that evidence was rated moderate. For fertility awareness based methods that rely primarily on basal body temperature and cervical mucus rather than a hormone strip, the review found the evidence too limited, too small, and too inconsistent to draw a confident conclusion about live birth rates either way. None of this means these methods are without value. It means the honest scientific summary is closer to "probably somewhat helpful, evidence still developing" than to the confident promises printed on the box, and that is exactly the kind of calibrated, unglamorous conclusion this course asks you to sit with rather than round up.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A positive result on a home ovulation test is often read as "I am definitely fertile right now" or, worse, mistaken after the fact for "ovulation definitely happened." Neither reading is quite right. The strip detects the luteinizing hormone surge, which predicts ovulation about a day later; it does not detect the egg's release itself, and it says nothing about whether a corpus luteum formed properly afterward. Pair it with basal body temperature charting if the goal is genuine confirmation, and treat a single strip, on its own, as a prediction worth noting, not a fact worth banking on.

Case

Odalys, 33, charts basal body temperature every morning before her alarm, checks and logs her cervical mucus most days, and uses a luteinizing hormone test strip starting around day nine of most cycles. Four months in, her charts show a clear, if slightly variable, pattern: a mucus shift toward a slippery, stretchy quality somewhere between day twelve and day sixteen, a positive strip a day or so later, and a temperature rise that confirms it within another day or two. In her fourth charted cycle, though, the pattern did not resolve the way the previous three had. Her temperature never produced a clean, sustained shift, her mucus stayed ambiguous for over two weeks, and her app, reading only the raw numbers, flagged the cycle as irregular and suggested she investigate possible causes.

Notice what all three methods share. None of them, alone or combined, diagnoses anything. Basal body temperature, cervical mucus, and luteinizing hormone testing are observation tools, not laboratory tests, and their honest purpose is to let a person see, in their own body, the same hormonal sequence charted in the last section: rising estrogen, a luteinizing hormone surge, ovulation, and a progesterone-driven temperature shift. Reading them well means reading them as a pattern across cycles, the same discipline that applies to cycle length itself, which is where we turn next. One unusual month on a chart, exactly like one unusual month of cycle length, is far more often noise than signal. The question is not whether Odalys's fourth cycle looked different. It obviously did. The question is what a single different month is actually allowed to tell you, and what it is not.

How wide is normal, really

If there is one number to unlearn, it is twenty-eight. The idea of a fixed twenty-eight-day cycle is a historical convenience, not a biological rule. Reviews of menstrual physiology are consistent about where the real variability concentrates: it is greatest in the first few years after a first period, when the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis is still maturing and ovulation is inconsistent, and it widens again in the years just before menopause, as the ovary's dwindling follicle supply makes each cycle less predictable (Mihm et al., 2011; Rosen Vollmar et al., 2025). In the middle decades of the reproductive years, by contrast, cycles tend to settle into their most consistent rhythm. This is not a minor footnote. A teenager whose cycles run anywhere from three to eight weeks apart in her first two years of menstruating is not showing a warning sign. She is showing exactly the pattern physiology predicts, and treating it as alarming does real harm by teaching her to distrust a body that is functioning as expected.

Modern datasets, drawn from hundreds of thousands of tracked cycles, sharpen the picture further. Bull and colleagues (2019) analyzed 612,613 confirmed ovulatory cycles from 124,648 users of a fertility-tracking application. The mean cycle length was 29.3 days, not 28. And even within these confirmed ovulatory cycles the internal variation was large: the follicular phase averaged 16.9 days with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning 10 to 30 days, while the luteal phase averaged 12.4 days with a confidence interval of 7 to 17 days. Cycle length shortened by about 0.18 days per year of age between 25 and 45, and its variability increased sharply above age 40.

Individual variation compounds this age effect. Two people of the same age, both cycling normally, can differ from each other by several days in average length and still both be well within range, because normal is a band, not a single number, and where a given person sits inside that band is simply part of her own baseline. The practical implication is that a large one-time deviation from the population average is far less informative than a deviation from a person's own established pattern, which is a theme this chapter returns to shortly.

Bleeding itself follows a similarly wide, and often underestimated, band. Typical menstrual bleeding lasts around three to seven days, and normal daily blood loss varies severalfold between individuals who are equally healthy, which is why some people soak through in two days what others take a week to shed without either pattern signaling disease. Where bleeding earns closer attention is in its extremes and in its trend: soaking through protection every hour for several consecutive hours, bleeding that regularly lasts beyond seven or eight days, or a flow that has clearly become heavier or lighter than a person's own baseline over several cycles. Those are patterns worth mentioning to a clinician, not because heavy or light bleeding is inherently alarming, but because trend and extremity, exactly as with cycle length, are what separates ordinary variation from a change worth a second look.

Put these together and a practical picture emerges. A great deal of what looks like an alarming change is simply the wide, normal texture of a healthy cycle, especially in the years just after the first period and in the approach to menopause. The physiological reviews frame the everyday normal band as roughly 26 to 35 days with about five days of bleeding (Mihm et al., 2011), but the tails are genuinely wide and shift with life stage. Literacy here means holding two facts at once: normal is wider than most people believe, and it is not infinitely wide.

Signal versus a change worth a clinician's attention

The whole practical value of the fifth vital sign lives in one distinction: telling ordinary variation apart from a change that warrants professional evaluation. Get this wrong in the alarmist direction and you frighten someone over a single late period that a stressful week fully explains. Get it wrong in the dismissive direction and you wave off a pattern that deserved attention months ago. Neither serves the person in front of you, and neither is your call to make on their behalf. Your job is to notice well and to know where the boundary of your role sits.

A workable frame is to weigh three things: the size of the deviation, its persistence, and its context. A one-month oddity, even a fairly dramatic one, is usually noise, because healthy cycles are genuinely noisy. The signals that deserve a clinician's eye are the ones that persist and cluster. Consistently very short or very long cycles, bleeding that is consistently very heavy or very prolonged, cycles that stop for several months, or a clear and sustained departure from a person's own established pattern: these are patterns, not blips, and patterns are what vital signs are for.

Apply that frame to Odalys's flagged cycle. The size of the deviation was real: a fourth cycle that did not resolve the way the first three had, no clean temperature shift, mucus that stayed ambiguous well past when it usually clarified. But persistence and context tell a different story. It was one cycle out of four charted, following months of a consistent pattern, arriving during a stretch when Odalys had also picked up her mileage for a half marathon and slept poorly for two weeks straight while her school's term ended. A single month of heavier training and thinner sleep is exactly the kind of context that can blunt or delay ovulation without indicating an underlying problem, and one month, even a dramatic one, is not yet a pattern. Her app could not know any of that context. It could only flag a number that fell outside its template. Reading the chart the way this chapter teaches, with size, persistence, and context all in view, turns an alarming red flag into a reasonable, low-urgency thing to keep watching rather than an emergency to chase down immediately.

The one recognize-and-refer thread

Two related conditions deserve special naming because they hide inside "my cycle just got irregular" and because they connect the axis directly to energy and stress. The first is amenorrhea, the absence of menstruation, whether periods never started or stopped for several months in someone who previously menstruated. The second is Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, abbreviated RED-S, a syndrome in which chronically low energy availability disrupts many body systems, the reproductive axis among them.

The mechanism is not mysterious, and it is one of the better-established findings in the whole field. Loucks and Thuma (2003) manipulated energy availability, defined as dietary energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, in 29 regularly menstruating young women, and found that the pulsatile release of luteinizing hormone is disrupted once energy availability falls below a threshold of roughly 30 kilocalories per kilogram of lean body mass per day. The relationship is a threshold, not a gentle slope: above the line the axis is fine, and below it the brain quiets the pulses that drive the whole cycle. This is why a cycle that grows irregular or disappears can be the first visible sign that the system is underfed or overtrained. The cycle is doing exactly its job as a vital sign, reporting that the whole organism is short on fuel.

Here is the firm line. Learning to notice the possibility of amenorrhea or RED-S is squarely within your reach and is the point of this chapter. Diagnosing either, or interpreting them for a specific person, or deciding what to do about them, is clinical work that belongs to a qualified professional. The same firmness applies to three areas where the temptation to interpret is especially strong and the stakes are especially high: contraception, which changes the cycle by design and rewrites every rule above; amenorrhea itself; and the postpartum period, where the axis is doing something genuinely different and normal ranges do not transfer. In all three, the correct move when something looks off is to recognize it and to point toward professional evaluation, not to explain it away and not to explain it yourself.

The line this chapter will not cross

A charting app that says "investigate possible causes" is, whether it intends to or not, nudging a person toward self-diagnosis. It is worth naming exactly why that nudge is a problem, because self-tracking tools are only going to get more common, more detailed, and more confident-sounding in the years ahead. An app, or a course like this one, can teach you what a hormone does, what a chart pattern usually means, and where the edges of normal typically sit. None of that adds up to knowing what is actually happening inside a specific person's body in a specific month. A single ambiguous cycle has dozens of plausible explanations, from ordinary variation to travel to illness to a training block to something that genuinely warrants medical evaluation, and no chart, however detailed, sorts those possibilities out on its own. Only a clinician, working with a full history, an examination where appropriate, and laboratory testing chosen for the specific question at hand, can do that sorting.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Odalys's app suggested, in the friendly, slightly ominous language such apps favor, that she "look into possible causes," and a quick search turned up a list of possibilities ranging from mildly reassuring to frightening, polycystic ovary syndrome among them. She read enough to worry and not enough to know anything reliably. What she did next is the right move for anyone in her position: she kept charting for two more cycles, watched the pattern return to its previous consistency, and brought all four months of charts to an appointment anyway, because persistent uncertainty about your own body is a perfectly good reason to see a clinician even when the story resolves on its own. She did not diagnose herself with anything, and neither will you, for her or for anyone else. She used her data to have a better conversation with a professional, which is precisely what data like hers is for.

A note on the evidence, and on humility

It would be dishonest to leave you with more certainty than the science supports. The claim that a regularly ovulatory cycle reflects whole-system health is well grounded, and the energy-availability threshold is a genuinely strong result. But a large and popular industry of cycle-based training schedules, phase-specific diets, and symptom-timed supplements rests on weak, inconsistent, or contradictory data. Much of it extrapolates far beyond what the studies actually show, often from small samples with loose control of confounders. When you encounter a confident claim that you should eat, train, or supplement a particular way on a particular cycle day, the appropriate default is skepticism, not adoption. Grade the evidence honestly and say so plainly.

The same caution applies to the growing ecosystem of cycle-tracking apps and wearables built around fertility awareness data. Many are genuinely useful for exactly the purpose described in this chapter: recording observations, surfacing patterns, and making basal body temperature or cervical mucus easier to chart consistently than a paper calendar ever was. But a growing number layer on predictive claims, cycle-phase forecasts, symptom explanations, and, as Odalys discovered, diagnostic-sounding language that reach well past what the underlying data can support. An app cannot see a person's stress, sleep, training load, or medical history the way a clinician can, and a pattern-matching algorithm trained on population averages will occasionally flag a perfectly normal individual variation as abnormal, simply because it does not match the template. Treat these tools as excellent recording instruments and mediocre diagnosticians, because that is what the evidence currently supports them being.

Finally, a word of humility that the course itself takes seriously. What you are reading is a distillation of research, and a distillation is not the territory. Clinical expertise and the lived experience of women reading their own bodies carry knowledge that no review captures. The fifth vital sign is a powerful lens precisely because it is simple and cheap, but a lens is for looking, not for concluding. Notice well, hold the boundary, and hand the interpreting to those whose role it is.

It is worth naming what Odalys actually walked away with, because it is the real deliverable of a chapter like this one. She did not leave with a diagnosis, a verdict on whether her fourth cycle meant something was wrong, or a number to chase every morning. She left with a working model: basal body temperature confirms after the fact, cervical mucus forecasts ahead of time, a luteinizing hormone strip predicts imminently, and none of the three, alone or together, replaces a clinician's judgment when a real question arises. She kept her charts, because they are useful and because they gave her clinician something concrete to review. What she stopped doing was treating a single unusual month, read by an app with no context for her training load or her sleep, as a verdict on her health. That is not a smaller outcome than a diagnosis would have been. It is a sturdier one.

Key Takeaways

  • A regularly ovulatory cycle is a vital sign because it reports on the whole reproductive axis, the loop running from the hypothalamus to the pituitary to the ovaries, and it meets all six standard criteria for a vital sign.
  • Follicle-stimulating hormone recruits and grows follicles; a luteinizing hormone surge triggers ovulation. A surge predicts ovulation but does not confirm it; confirmation comes from downstream progesterone.
  • A regular, normal-length cycle is not automatically ovulatory. In one population study, 37 percent of assessed cycles were anovulatory with no outward tell.
  • Basal body temperature, cervical mucus, and luteinizing hormone testing are self-observation tools, not diagnoses: temperature confirms retrospectively, mucus forecasts prospectively, and a strip predicts imminently, and each is read as a pattern across cycles rather than as a single data point.
  • Normal is far wider than twenty-eight days. Average length runs closer to 29 to 30 days, variability is greatest just after the first period and near menopause, and the everyday band spans roughly 26 to 35 days.
  • Distinguish a change worth attention from ordinary noise by weighing size, persistence, and context. Single odd months, in cycle length or in a fertility awareness chart, are usually noise; persistent, clustered patterns are signal.
  • Recognize, do not diagnose: amenorrhea and Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport can hide inside "my cycle got irregular," and low energy availability disrupts luteinizing hormone pulsatility below about 30 kilocalories per kilogram of lean body mass per day. Refer.
  • This course builds understanding, not permission to diagnose. When self-tracking data raises a real question, in yourself or anyone else, the next step is a qualified clinician, not a self-arrived-at label.
Looking Ahead

Reading the cycle as a signal only matters if you also understand what happens when that signal is deliberately overwritten. Hormonal contraception works precisely by interrupting the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian conversation this chapter described, which is why none of the fertility awareness methods above apply while someone is using it, and why "my cycle looks different since starting the pill" is not the same question as the irregularity this chapter covered. Chapter 5 takes apart contraception by mechanism, method by method, so you can see exactly what each one changes, what it does not change, and why the fifth vital sign temporarily stops being readable while it is in use.
