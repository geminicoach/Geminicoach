Chapter 8 : Estrogen and the Living Skeleton

Bone is living tissue in constant remodeling. Estrogen is its primary brake on breakdown, and the years that matter most for building it never come back.

The Remodeling Team

OsteoclastsThe demolition crew: attach to bone surface, dissolve mineral, clear old matrix, carving out a small pit.
OsteoblastsThe construction crew: move into the cleared pit, lay down new osteoid, which hardens as mineral deposits.
OsteocytesFormer osteoblasts embedded in bone; a connected sensor network that detects strain and directs remodeling to where it is needed.
The balanceIn a healthy young adult, resorption and formation roughly match. Tip that balance, even slightly, month after month, and bone is lost.

Estrogen's Brake

  • RANKL / OPG : estrogen raises osteoprotegerin (a decoy) and lowers RANKL, so fewer osteoclast precursors mature.
  • Cell survival : estrogen shortens osteoclast lifespan and lengthens osteoblast and osteocyte lifespan.
  • Oxidative stress : low estrogen raises reactive oxygen species, activating FoxO, which pushes bone-building cells toward apoptosis.
  • Net effect of low estrogen: a resorption-formation gap opens from multiple directions at once.

The Bone Bank: Build, Hold, Lose

  • Build : roughly four years around the mid-teens capture about 39 percent of total adult bone mineral. This window does not reopen.
  • Hold : regular ovulatory cycles keep cyclical estradiol exposure that defends the peak that was built.
  • Lose : estrogen falls sharply at menopause; the same withdrawal can start decades early if a young cycle goes quiet for long enough.

T-score vs Z-score

  • T-score : compares against young, healthy adults. Used for people well past menopause.
  • Z-score : compares against others of the same age and sex. More appropriate for a younger, premenopausal person.
  • Reading and interpreting either score is a clinical task, not a lookup.

Where the Threads Converge

  • Low energy availability suppresses the HPG axis (functional hypothalamic amenorrhea), the cycle goes quiet, estrogen falls, and bone suffers: one cascade, not three coincidences.
  • A full remodeling cycle takes months; bone loss produces no symptoms along the way, so "feeling fine" tells you little.
  • Randomized trial evidence (Ackerman et al., 2019) shows restoring estrogen, especially transdermal, can improve bone microarchitecture, but that decision belongs entirely to a clinician.
Recognize and refer   A persistently absent, infrequent, or irregular cycle, especially alongside signs of low energy availability, and a stress fracture in an unusual location or one that does not clearly match training load, are patterns worth naming. Interpreting a DEXA scan, ordering labs, and deciding on hormone therapy or activity changes belong to a qualified clinician. Notice the pattern, name it without alarm, and point toward care.
