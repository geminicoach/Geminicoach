Chapter 7 : The Cost of Running on Empty

Energy availability is what is left over for the body after exercise is paid for. When it runs short for long enough, the body triages a whole portfolio of systems at once.

The Energy Availability Equation

EA formulaEnergy availability = (dietary intake minus exercise energy expended) divided by fat-free mass (kcal/kg FFM/day). Not the same as intake, not the same as energy balance.
The landmarkLoucks and Thuma (2003): LH pulsatility disrupted below roughly 30 kcal/kg FFM/day in a lab study. A useful concept, not a personal cutoff; individual variation is large.
Adaptable vs problematic2023 IOC consensus: the body can tolerate adaptable LEA for a while without lasting harm; problematic LEA produces measurable harm. Where that line falls is individual.
Intentional vs unintentionalLEA can come from deliberate weight cutting or leanness pressure, or entirely unintentionally when training volume rises faster than intake. It is the shortfall itself, with or without an eating disorder, that drives harm.

RED-S: One Cause, Many Systems

  • Menstrual : GnRH pulse generator slows, LH pulsatility falls, cycle quiets or stops.
  • Bone : suppressed estrogen plus energy shortfall tip remodeling toward loss, raising stress injury risk.
  • Thyroid/endocrine : low T3 pattern, blunted growth hormone, appetite hormones shift.
  • Cardiovascular : unfavorable lipid shift, impaired vessel lining function.
  • Immune : more illness, more training days lost to being sick.
  • Performance : decreased endurance, blunted adaptation, worse coordination and judgment, despite leanness feeling like an advantage.

Who Carries More Risk

  • Weight-class sports : acute cuts layered on a chronic shortfall (wrestling, rowing lightweight, combat sports).
  • Physique/aesthetic sports : sustained leanness pressure, judged or scored (bodybuilding, gymnastics, diving, figure skating).
  • The gradual drift : training volume creeps up across a season without intake rising to match. No single week looks alarming.
  • Adolescents and postpartum athletes : energy needs shift for reasons unrelated to sport; a once-adequate intake can quietly stop matching output.

Why It Gets Normalized

  • A skipped period gets reframed as convenient; constant hunger gets praised as discipline.
  • Frequent minor illness gets shrugged off as bad luck; a stalled pace reads as a plateau to push through.
  • No single dramatic moment announces LEA; it accumulates quietly over months.
  • A lean physique is routinely read as fitness, even when it is quietly working against the athlete underneath.

The Skill: See the Set, Not the Symptom

  • A quiet cycle, plus a nagging bone injury, plus frequent illness, plus stalled performance, may be one story, not four.
  • Screening tools (like the LEAF-Q) exist for trained professionals; recognizing the constellation is within reach for an educated observer, interpreting it is not.
  • Weight stability does not rule out LEA; core systems can be downshifted while the scale holds steady.
Recognize and refer   Persistent low energy availability, menstrual dysfunction beyond a cycle or two, or any sign of disordered eating are not situations to coach around, track privately, or wait out. Each warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician, ideally a physician, registered dietitian, and mental-health professional working together. Notice the pattern, take it seriously, then refer. This is the firmest line in the course, especially around amenorrhea and RED-S.
