Signal Versus Noise: The Cycle, the Barbell, and the Plate

Grading the cycle-syncing industry honestly, and finding the parts that actually hold.

31 min read 8 cited sources

Open any fitness app built in the last few years and there is a good chance it will offer to tell you when to lift heavy, when to back off, when to eat more carbohydrate, and when to protect your recovery, all keyed to the day of your menstrual cycle. The promise is seductive because it sounds like precision. It sounds like science finally taking female physiology seriously instead of treating a woman as a smaller man. And it arrives with the confidence of a settled field.

That appeal is not manufactured out of nothing. For decades, exercise and sport science built its evidence base almost entirely on male participants, treating the menstrual cycle as a nuisance variable to be controlled away rather than a system worth studying on its own terms. The backlash against that neglect is legitimate, and the demand for research that actually includes women is overdue and important. The problem is that a legitimate demand for better research has been answered, in the commercial space, with a product that looks like the finished research rather than a call for more of it. An app cannot sell you "we genuinely do not know yet, but here is how to pay attention to yourself," not the way it can sell you a calendar with a checkmark on it. That commercial pressure toward false certainty is worth naming before we even open a single study, because it explains why the confidence you encounter in the marketplace so consistently outruns the confidence you find in the literature.

Here is the uncomfortable thing this chapter asks you to sit with: much of that confidence is manufactured. When you actually open the studies underneath the marketing, you find small samples, cycle phases guessed at rather than measured, confounders left uncontrolled, and results that contradict each other from one paper to the next. This is the chapter where our honest grading commitment has to do its hardest work, because grading honestly here means telling you that a large and profitable industry is selling more certainty than the evidence can buy.

Throughout this chapter you will follow one learner, Petra, a 29-year-old competitive powerlifter who found a popular cycle-syncing app the winter before a big meet. The app told her which lifts to prioritize by day, when to expect her numbers to dip, and when to eat more or less, all keyed to a calendar it generated the moment she entered her last period date. For a few months she trusted it completely, restructuring a training block around a calendar rather than around how a session actually felt. Then a bar that should have moved easily on a green light day did not move, and a bar the app had flagged as a red light day flew up faster than she expected. She started asking a harder question: was the calendar actually reading her body, or was it reading a story about her body that sounded convincing but had never been tested very well? Petra is not a patient in this course and she is not a case to be solved. She is a stand-in for a common experience, trusting a confident interface over an uncertain literature, and by the end of this chapter you will be equipped to ask better questions than the app ever did.

What "cycle syncing" actually claims

Let us name the claim precisely, because vague claims are impossible to grade. The strong version of phase-based prescription holds that you should change what you do based on cycle phase: lift heavier and chase adaptation in the follicular phase when estrogen is rising, then deload, switch to lighter work, or prioritize recovery in the luteal phase when progesterone dominates. Some versions extend this to nutrition, prescribing different macronutrient targets, calorie levels, or even specific foods by phase, a different plate for every week of the month. The underlying logic is that the sex hormones estrogen and progesterone swing predictably across roughly a month, and that these swings must therefore move the needle on strength, power, hypertrophy, and recovery enough to justify rewriting your program and your grocery list.

A quick anchor before we go further, since the phases anchor everything that follows. The follicular phase begins on the first day of bleeding and runs until ovulation, roughly the first half of a textbook cycle, and it is here that estrogen rises from its lowest point toward its mid-cycle peak. Ovulation is the release of an egg, typically around the midpoint of the cycle. The luteal phase follows, running from ovulation to the next period, and it is here that progesterone rises and then falls if pregnancy does not occur, with estrogen making a smaller second rise before both hormones drop together just before bleeding starts again. Cycle-syncing programs map training and nutrition recommendations onto this two-act structure, treating the follicular phase as a "build" window and the luteal phase as a "protect" window. That framing sounds tidy. Real cycles, as the sections ahead will show, are rarely as tidy as the diagram.

The logic is not absurd. Estrogen and progesterone do have plausible mechanistic effects on muscle, connective tissue, substrate use, and the central nervous system. Estrogen has documented effects on collagen synthesis and on how muscle repairs itself in cell and animal models, and progesterone shifts core body temperature and can nudge respiratory drive during exercise. Nobody serious argues these hormones do nothing. The question is never whether an effect could exist in theory. The question is whether the effect is large enough, consistent enough, and well enough measured in real, trained humans to change what a real person should do on a Tuesday. That is a much higher bar than plausibility, and it is the bar the evidence has to clear before it earns a place in your training plan.

There is also a subtler reason to expect the popular narrative to run ahead of the data. A finding that says "phase matters, train accordingly" is more publishable, more shareable, and more monetizable than a finding that says "we looked hard and found nothing worth changing." That asymmetry does not mean researchers are dishonest. It means that in a field with small studies and a lot of statistical noise, the results that survive to become headlines and app features are disproportionately the ones that happened to come out positive, a pattern researchers call publication and reporting bias. Keeping that asymmetry in mind is part of reading this literature honestly, and it is a habit worth carrying into every other chapter of this course as well.

The measurement problem underneath everything

Before we look at outcomes, we have to look at how researchers even know which phase a participant was in. This sounds like a technicality. It is actually the crack that runs through the whole foundation. According to Janse de Jonge and colleagues (2019), a review of cycle verification methods used in exercise studies published between 2008 and 2018 found that only 44 percent of studies actually measured estrogen and progesterone in the blood. The rest relied on counting calendar days, which assumes a textbook cycle that many people simply do not have.

Why does this matter so much? Because a cycle can look regular on a calendar and still be anovulatory, meaning no egg was released and progesterone never rose the way the model assumes. Or it can have a short, inadequate luteal phase. If you sort participants into a "luteal" group by calendar date and some of them never actually entered a proper luteal hormonal state, you have blended two different physiologies into one bucket and called it data. Janse de Jonge and colleagues recommend a three-method verification approach: calendar tracking, plus testing for the luteinizing hormone (LH) surge that precedes ovulation, plus a blood measurement of the hormones themselves. Very few studies do all three. Most do the weakest one, and then a headline gets built on top of it.

Small samples compound the measurement problem rather than sitting beside it as a separate issue. Many of the studies in this literature enroll somewhere between ten and twenty participants split across two or three phases, which leaves little statistical power to detect anything but a large effect, and true effects here, if they exist at all, are more likely to be small. A small, underpowered study that happens to turn up a positive result is also more likely to be overestimating the true size of that effect, a pattern sometimes called the winner's curse: the studies most likely to get published and cited are the ones whose noise happened to point in a dramatic direction. Put a shaky verification method next to a small sample and a bias toward positive results, and you have a recipe for a literature that looks busy and productive while actually being fragile and inconsistent from one attempt to the next.

Think About It

If a study sorted people into cycle phases using only a calendar, and a meaningful fraction of those people had anovulatory cycles that month, what direction would that push the measured "difference between phases"? Toward finding a real effect, or toward blurring one into noise? Sit with why either answer complicates the headline you are about to read.

Reading the evidence plainly

Now the outcomes. Start with performance broadly. According to McNulty and colleagues (2020), a large systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis of cycle phase and exercise performance in regularly menstruating women concluded that performance might be trivially reduced in the early follicular phase compared with other phases. Read that carefully. The word is "might," the effect is "trivial," and the authors state directly that the evidence base is insufficient to generate exercise guidelines for women. That is the responsible reading of one of the most cited meta-analyses in this space: a possible tiny effect, and an explicit refusal to prescribe from it.

Narrow to strength and resistance training, where phase-based lifting advice concentrates most heavily, since it is the corner of the industry that Petra's app was built around. Colenso-Semple and colleagues (2023) conducted an umbrella review, which is a review of other reviews and meta-analyses, examining the whole body of work on cycle phase and resistance exercise. Its verdict was blunt: the evidence is scant, low in quality, and largely inconsistent. The authors found no persuasive case that short-term hormonal fluctuations appreciably influence either acute strength performance or the longer-term adaptations lifters actually train for, and they documented a pervasive pattern of poor and inconsistent methods across the literature. In other words, the highest-level synthesis available says the strong claim, the one printed on the app's calendar, is not supported.

So why does anyone still believe it?

Because the literature genuinely contradicts itself, and you can find a paper for almost any position, which is exactly the environment in which a confident app thrives. Niering and colleagues (2024) published a systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies covering 433 participants and reported small to medium effects favoring the late follicular and ovulation phases for isometric and isokinetic strength, with only small effects for dynamic strength. Taken alone, that sounds like support for phasing. But the same authors acknowledge their pool is small and heterogeneous, and their finding sits in direct tension with the Colenso-Semple umbrella review. This is not a case where one side is lying. It is a case where the underlying studies are individually weak enough that pooling them different ways produces different answers. That instability is itself the finding, and it is the opposite of what a calendar app implies when it hands you a single number with confidence.

The deepest diagnosis comes from a methodological working guide for research on women in sport and exercise science. According to Elliott-Sale and colleagues (2021), the great majority of high-quality sport science data still come from studies with men as the only participants, and the field has lacked agreed standards for female-specific variables such as cycle phase verification, hormonal contraceptive use, and reproductive changes across the lifespan. The result is an evidence base that is not merely thin but structurally unreliable, one that cannot yet support confident guidelines for female exercisers. When the tool that measures the world is miscalibrated, you do not fix it by collecting more measurements with the same tool.

It is premature to state that short-term hormonal fluctuations appreciably influence acute performance or longer-term adaptations to resistance exercise training.

Paraphrasing the conclusion of Colenso-Semple and colleagues (2023)

Notice what the honest verdict is and is not. It is not "the cycle does nothing." It is "the strong, confident, calendar-driven prescription outruns the data, badly." Those are very different statements, and keeping them distinct is the whole discipline of this chapter.

There is a related idea worth separating out clearly, because it is easy to blur the two. A population-level review can correctly conclude that there is no reliable average effect of cycle phase on strength, while a specific individual can still have a real, personal, repeatable pattern of her own. Averages hide variation; they do not erase it. The mistake is not in noticing your own pattern. The mistake is assuming your pattern generalizes to everyone else, or assuming that because researchers have not found a reliable population average, your own tracked experience must be imaginary. Both of those are overreaches, just pointed in opposite directions.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common defense of cycle syncing goes like this: my training partner swears by it, so it must be doing something. Notice what that argument actually proves. A person can feel more in control, sleep better because they are paying closer attention, or simply enjoy having a plan, and all of that is real, valuable experience. None of it demonstrates that the underlying mechanism, a hormone level driving performance on a predictable schedule, is what produced the result. Feeling helped and being explained by the claimed mechanism are two different questions, and the evidence above answers only the second one.

The honest posture: load-and-recovery mindfulness

If calendar prescription is not supported, what is the defensible stance? The course calls it load-and-recovery mindfulness. The idea is simple and it respects both the evidence and your own experience. You do not rewrite your movements by phase. You stay attentive to the signals that actually predict how a session will go: energy, sleep quality, mood, motivation, joint comfort, and perceived readiness. When those signals say you are ready, you push. When they say you are flat, you adjust the session in front of you. This is autoregulation, and it is what good training has always done. It simply does not need a calendar to work.

The distinction is worth making sharp because it changes what you notice. A phase prescription tells you in advance that day 22 will be a bad day, which can become a small self-fulfilling prophecy or an excuse waiting to be used. Load-and-recovery mindfulness makes no prediction. It asks you to read today's actual state and respond to it. If your luteal phase reliably brings poorer sleep and lower drive, you will feel that and adjust, and you will have learned something true about your own body rather than borrowing a claim from a shaky literature. Some people do notice real, repeatable patterns across their cycle. The honest move is to treat those as personal data worth respecting, not as a universal law worth prescribing to anyone else.

In practice, load-and-recovery mindfulness does not require expensive technology. It can be as simple as a short check-in before a session: how did you sleep, how does your body feel moving through a light warmup, what is your motivation like today on a simple low, medium, high scale. Some people prefer a numeric rating of perceived readiness on a ten-point scale, logged in a notebook or a basic app, reviewed every few weeks to see if any real pattern emerges over time. The tool matters far less than the habit of asking the question honestly, in the moment, rather than letting a pre-written calendar answer it for you in advance. If a genuine pattern does emerge after real tracking, that is valuable information about you specifically, and it is worth respecting. It is just not evidence about anyone else, and it never needed a hormone assay to be useful.

Think About It

What is the practical difference between "I train lighter in my luteal phase because an app told me to" and "I read low readiness today and adjusted"? Both might produce a lighter session. Only one of them keeps you honest about cause and effect. Why does that distinction matter over months of training, and what happens to your confidence in your own judgment if you outsource it to a calendar instead?

Case

Petra's app had marked the week before her meet as a low output week and recommended she skip her heaviest deadlift session. Her actual readiness that morning, read from her own sleep, appetite, and how the bar felt on her warmup sets, said something different: she felt strong. She ran the session as planned rather than as scheduled, and it went well. The point of this story is not that the app is always wrong or that ignoring fatigue is smart. It is that a number generated by her own body that morning carried more information than a number generated by a calendar months in advance, and learning to tell the two apart is a skill worth building.

The one thread that is not autoregulation: recognize and refer

Everything above is about ordinary variation in a healthy, regularly menstruating body. There is one situation where "read your readiness and adjust" is not enough, and it is important to name the boundary clearly. If menstruation stops for months in someone who is not pregnant and not postpartum and not in menopause, that is amenorrhea, and it is not a training tweak spot to push through. It can be a sign of Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S), a state in which the body is not getting enough energy to cover both training and basic physiological function, with downstream effects on hormones, bone, and much else.

Your job as an educated observer is to recognize the pattern, not to diagnose it and certainly not to treat it. A missing period across several months, alongside persistent fatigue, frequent injury, or a very restricted intake, is a signal that warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician. That is the entire scope: notice, take it seriously, and understand that it may need professional care. The same firm boundary applies to anything involving hormonal contraception, the interpretation of amenorrhea, and the postpartum period. Those are clinical territory, full stop. Education can help you ask better questions; it cannot replace the person qualified to answer them.

Where the ground is firmer: iron and the bleed

Having spent the first half of this chapter deflating overclaims, honesty now cuts the other way. There is a part of the cycle-and-lifestyle story that rests on much firmer ground, and it deserves to be taken seriously precisely because we were willing to reject the weak parts. That part is iron.

The logic here is not statistical hairsplitting; it is basic accounting. Menstrual bleeding removes blood, blood contains iron, and this happens month after month for decades. According to Badenhorst and colleagues (2021), that recurring loss is why iron deficiency is substantially more common in female athletes than in male athletes, and the difference is largely attributable to menstruation during the premenopausal years. Iron is not a boutique concern. It sits at the center of oxygen transport, and low iron status can quietly drag down energy and performance well before it becomes a diagnosable anemia.

A recent exploratory study followed regularly menstruating female football players and measured iron and blood production markers across the cycle. Nolte and colleagues (2025) found that menstrual blood loss, tracked with a validated pictorial assessment chart, was significantly associated with lower ferritin, the storage form of iron, and with a higher reticulocyte production index, a marker of the body manufacturing new red blood cells faster than usual. That is evidence that menstruation is a direct physiological trigger for the body to adapt its iron handling, not merely a coincidence that happens to occur in the same population that trains hard. The broader iron literature makes a telling observation about the field itself: studies of iron status in athletes frequently fail to report participants' cycle phase or status at all, which means the same measurement culture that weakened the training research shows up here too. Even so, the underlying signal, that the bleed costs iron and the body responds, holds up far better than anything in the training-and-lifting literature.

Food-first iron and the case for red meat around menstruation

If iron is a genuine, recurring cost, the sensible next question is where iron actually comes from in food, and here the honest answer favors a food-first approach over a supplement-first one for most people. Dietary iron comes in two forms. Heme iron is found in animal flesh, especially red meat, and the body absorbs it comparatively efficiently, generally in the range of 15 to 35 percent of what is eaten. Non-heme iron, found in plant foods like lentils, spinach, and fortified grains, is absorbed far less efficiently, often under 10 percent, and its absorption swings widely depending on what else is on the plate. Vitamin C enhances non-heme absorption; compounds in tea, coffee, and some whole grains can blunt it; and calcium taken at the same meal can compete with iron for absorption.

This is why red meat, eaten thoughtfully around the days of heaviest flow, is a reasonable, food-first way to help offset a real and recurring loss, rather than a fad recommendation dressed up in cycle language. It is not the only path. A person who does not eat red meat can still meet iron needs by leaning on non-heme sources and pairing them deliberately with vitamin C, and plenty of people do this well. The point is not that everyone must eat a steak on a specific day of their cycle. The point is that once you understand the accounting, food choices around menstruation stop being arbitrary and start being a reasonable response to a measurable, physical cost, which is a very different thing from a phase-based macro chart with no accounting behind it at all.

How to hold this without overstating it

Here is the careful framing. The goal is understanding and awareness, not a self-directed supplementation protocol. Knowing that recurring menstrual loss is a genuine iron cost lets you make sense of unexplained fatigue, understand why iron status is worth monitoring, and ask a clinician the right question at the right time. Badenhorst and colleagues note that for people with a history of iron deficiency, periodic screening makes sense, on the order of a few times a year during heavy training. But screening, interpretation, and any decision to supplement are clinical acts. Iron is not a harmless nutrient to megadose on a hunch; too much carries its own real risks, including gastrointestinal harm and, over time, effects from excess storage. So the mature stance is: take the cost seriously, stay aware, favor food-first choices day to day, get proper testing when it is warranted, and leave the treatment decision, including whether a supplement is appropriate at all, to a professional who can see your actual numbers.

It also helps to know, in a general way, what low iron status can look like, again so that you can recognize a pattern worth mentioning to a clinician rather than diagnose yourself from a symptom list. Persistent fatigue that does not track with training load, effort that feels harder than usual at a familiar pace, unusual shortness of breath, restless legs at night, and pale skin or brittle nails are all patterns associated with low iron status. None of them are specific to iron alone, which is exactly why they warrant a blood test rather than a guess. A person who eats little or no red meat, trains hard, and notices several of these signs together has a reasonable food-first move available, leaning further into non-heme sources paired with vitamin C, and a reasonable clinical move available, asking for ferritin and related markers to be checked. The two are not in conflict, and neither one is a substitute for the other.

Energy and appetite: real experience, unsettled numbers

The next piece sits between the firm iron ground and the shaky training ground. Many people report that appetite and energy intake shift across the cycle, often feeling hungrier and eating more in the luteal phase and somewhat less around the follicular phase. Is this real? According to Rogan and Black (2022), the first narrative review to synthesize the literature on dietary energy intake across cycle phases found that intake does appear lower in the follicular phase and higher in the luteal phase, with the sharpest dip around ovulation. So the direction of the pattern has support.

But the same review is candid that the magnitude is not yet clearly quantifiable and that the evidence base has large gaps and methodological limits. This is the right place to practice the chapter's core skill. The lived experience of appetite change is reported often enough, and consistently enough in direction, to take seriously. The established mechanism and precise magnitude are not settled. Both statements are true at once, and mature reasoning holds them together rather than collapsing into either "it is all in your head" or "your body demands exactly a fixed number of extra calories on a specific day."

What does that mean in practice? It means that if you notice a reliable uptick in hunger in the back half of your cycle, you can trust your own observation and respond to it flexibly, without needing a rigid phase-based meal plan to authorize it. Eating a bit more when genuinely hungrier is not a diet failure; it may be a sensible response to a real fluctuation. The honest posture toward appetite is the same as toward training: notice your own signal, respect it, track it over a few cycles if you want real data instead of a guess, and do not dress it up as a precise scientific law it has not earned.

It is also worth distinguishing hunger from craving, since cycle-syncing marketing often blurs the two into a single "give in because biology demands it" message. Hunger is a general drive to eat more; a craving is a pull toward a specific food, and the two do not always move together or mean the same thing. Some of what gets labeled as luteal phase hunger is genuine increased energy need. Some of it is a craving shaped by mood, stress, sleep, or simple habit, none of which are wrong to notice or respond to, but none of which need a hormonal justification either. There is also a real risk on the opposite side worth naming: a person who tries to fight a genuine increase in appetite with rigid restriction in the luteal phase, because a program says that phase is for "staying lean," is fighting her own physiology for no evidence-based reason, which tends to backfire into exactly the kind of overeating the restriction was trying to prevent.

Energy intake appears lower in the follicular phase and higher in the luteal phase, but the magnitude of these fluctuations is not yet clearly quantifiable.

Paraphrasing Rogan and Black (2022)

Calcium, vitamin D, and the skeleton underneath all of this

Zoom out for a moment to a nutrient story that is not about the monthly cycle at all, but sits directly underneath it and connects back to earlier ground this course has already covered. Estrogen has a protective role in bone remodeling, restraining the cells that break bone down so that building and breaking stay in balance. When estrogen is chronically low, whether from RED-S, certain contraceptive choices, or the menopausal transition, that brake weakens and bone loss can accelerate. Calcium and vitamin D do not change that hormonal picture, but they supply the raw material and the regulatory signal bone needs to do its job well regardless of what the hormonal backdrop looks like.

This is a case where the honest advice is almost boringly stable across the cycle rather than phase-dependent: calcium needs and vitamin D needs do not meaningfully shift week to week the way the training-and-lifting claims imply. What matters is a consistent, adequate intake sustained over months and years, since bone remodels slowly and responds to steady patterns, not to a single well-timed week. Food-first calcium sources include dairy, fortified plant milks, canned fish eaten with the bones, and leafy greens, though the calcium in some greens is bound up with compounds that limit how much of it actually gets absorbed. Vitamin D comes from sun exposure on skin, from a small number of foods like fatty fish and fortified products, and, when levels run low, from a supplement a clinician can recommend and monitor with an actual blood value rather than a guess.

The point of raising this here is not to repeat everything this course has already said about the skeleton. It is to model the same discipline one more time: some things genuinely do vary with the cycle and deserve cycle-aware attention, like iron and, to an unsettled degree, appetite. Other things, like the daily calcium and vitamin D your bone-building cells need, are steady requirements that a phase-based framework has no special insight into. Confusing the two, treating everything as cycle-dependent because some things are, is exactly the overreach this chapter exists to correct.

This also illustrates a useful distinction between what you can track yourself and what belongs to a clinician. Training readiness is something you genuinely can observe day to day, in your own body, without special equipment. Bone density is not. It is measured with a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan, an imaging test ordered and interpreted by a qualified clinician, not something you can estimate from how your joints feel or how your training is going. That is not a small technicality. It means the individual-tracking spirit of this chapter has a real limit: some things, like readiness and appetite, genuinely are yours to observe and learn from, while others, like bone density and iron stores, require instruments and expertise you do not have access to on your own. Knowing which category a question falls into is itself part of thinking clearly about your health.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A tempting shortcut says bone health is a concern for later in life, so it does not belong in a chapter about cycles and training. But bone mass built or lost during the active training years sets the baseline everyone carries into the menopausal transition, where estrogen's protective effect withdraws and losses can accelerate quickly. Treating calcium and vitamin D as optional now, while chasing a phase-based macro chart instead, gets the priorities backward. The unglamorous, steady nutrient is doing more for the skeleton than the trendy one.

A decision frame you can carry

When the next cycle-based training or nutrition claim reaches you, and it will, run it through four questions. First, how was cycle phase determined, by blood measurement or merely by calendar? Second, how many participants, and were regularly ovulating cycles actually confirmed? Third, are the results consistent with the broader body of work, or do they contradict the higher-level reviews? Fourth, and most important, is the effect large enough to change what a real person should do, or is it a statistically detectable but trivial blip? A claim that survives all four is rare. Most collapse at the first.

A fifth question is worth adding for anyone building their own practice from this chapter rather than just evaluating someone else's claim: have you actually tracked yourself long enough to know your own pattern, or are you assuming one because a program told you it should be there? Three cycles of honest, simple tracking, energy, sleep, hunger, mood, performance, will tell you more about your own body than any population-level study ever could, precisely because a population-level study is not about you. That is the entire spirit of the shift from population rules to individual tracking: not abandoning evidence, but recognizing that the most relevant evidence about your own cycle is the evidence you collect yourself, read honestly, and hold with the same appropriate humility this chapter has tried to model throughout.

That skepticism is not cynicism. It is respect. Female physiology deserves rigorous study, not a rushed marketing story that borrows the authority of science while skipping its discipline. And a note of humility belongs here too: this course distills research, but the research itself is incomplete, and the lived and clinical expertise of individuals and their care providers fills gaps the papers cannot. The most honest thing an educator can do is tell you where the map is firm, where it is blank, and where someone has drawn confident roads that were never actually surveyed.

Petra kept training through her meet, but she deleted the calendar recommendations and kept the readiness log. She still notices real patterns some months, heavier legs in certain weeks, hungrier in others, and she treats those as her own data rather than borrowed certainty. When her numbers dipped for two cycles in a row for no clear training reason, that pattern, not a phase label, was what sent her to get bloodwork rather than to a new supplement. That is the whole shift this chapter asks for: from population rules applied to everyone, to a person paying close, honest attention to herself.

Key Takeaways

  • The strong version of phase-based training prescription is not well supported; the highest-level review found scant, low-quality, and contradictory evidence, and performance meta-analyses find at most trivial effects (Colenso-Semple et al., 2023; McNulty et al., 2020).
  • The literature's core flaw is measurement: fewer than half of studies verify cycle phase with actual hormone testing, which blurs any real effect and destabilizes conclusions (Janse de Jonge et al., 2019; Elliott-Sale et al., 2021).
  • Contradictory findings, such as Niering and colleagues (2024) reporting small to medium strength effects against the umbrella review's null verdict, reflect a heterogeneous, underpowered literature rather than a hidden truth waiting to be uncovered.
  • Load-and-recovery mindfulness, reading today's actual energy, sleep, and readiness, is a more honest stance than rewriting your movements by calendar.
  • Iron is a genuine, recurring cost of menstruation and a stronger claim than phase-based lifting; food-first choices, including red meat for those who eat it, are a sensible response, while screening and any supplementation decision belong to a clinician (Badenhorst et al., 2021; Nolte et al., 2025).
  • Appetite and energy intake shifts across phases are credible as lived experience with an established direction but unsettled magnitude (Rogan and Black, 2022).
  • Calcium and vitamin D needs are steady across the cycle rather than phase-dependent, and consistent intake over months and years matters more than any single week's timing.
  • Recognize and refer: prolonged absence of menstruation may signal amenorrhea or Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport and warrants professional evaluation. Contraception, amenorrhea, and postpartum questions are clinical territory.
