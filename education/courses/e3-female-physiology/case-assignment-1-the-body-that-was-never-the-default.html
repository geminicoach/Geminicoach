Chapter 1: Reason Through Conflicting Advice

Take the evidence-grading habit from the chapter and use it to sort a real, common dilemma: two confident articles that cannot both be right.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 20 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Amara, 27
Amara is an amateur triathlete preparing for her first Olympic-distance race. Searching for training guidance, she finds two articles. The first claims women should schedule their hardest interval sessions in the days right after their period ends, citing a study of eight women. The second claims cycle phase has no meaningful effect on fatigue or performance at all, citing a large study that, when Amara reads the methods section, turns out to have enrolled zero women. Both articles are written with total confidence. Amara asks: "Which one do I actually believe?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, using specific figures from the chapter's audits, why sport and exercise science literature is generally thin on female-specific findings.
  2. Explain why a study that enrolled no women, or very few, cannot be used to claim that no sex difference exists.
  3. Apply the three-tier evidence-grading framework to each of Amara's two articles, and assign each a tier with justification.
  4. Decide what a responsible response to Amara would say, and state clearly where general education ends and personalized coaching or clinical guidance begins.

Your Response

0 words
Where education ends
You are demonstrating evidence literacy and plain-language communication, not prescribing a training plan. A responsible response explains the state of the evidence honestly, including its limits, and does not tell Amara what to do next week. Building an individualized program belongs with a coach or clinician who knows her full history.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Cites specific audit figures (Costello et al. 2014 about 39 percent, Cowley et al. 2021 about 34 percent and 31 percent male-only, Kuikman et al. 2022 about 3 percent in performance-enhancement research) to explain the thin evidence base.
  • Explains why an all-male or near-all-male sample cannot establish "no sex difference," distinguishing "not adequately tested" from "confirmed identical."
  • Correctly grades each of Amara's two articles with the three-tier framework, justified by sample size and design.
  • Gives a responsible, education-only response that does not prescribe a specific training plan, and states where general education ends.
