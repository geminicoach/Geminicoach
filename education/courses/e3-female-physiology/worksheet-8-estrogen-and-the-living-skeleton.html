Part C · Two Kinds of Reasoning

  1. Explain the difference between a T-score and a Z-score, and why that distinction matters when a younger, premenopausal person is being compared to a reference population.
  2. Explain why the youth building window (roughly the four years around peak bone mineral accretion) does not reopen, and what that implies for someone who suppresses estrogen during those years.
  3. Distinguish a normal, expected withdrawal from the bone bank (such as lactation) from a withdrawal tied to an unresolved energy deficit. What is the key difference?

Part D · Trace the Cascade

A young athlete increases training and reduces intake across a season. Trace, step by step, how this can end in a stress fracture. Name the mechanism at each step.

  1. What happens to the reproductive axis first, and why does it go quiet before the skeleton visibly fails?
  2. What happens to estradiol as a result?
  3. What happens to the RANKL to osteoprotegerin ratio, and to osteoblast and osteoclast survival?
  4. Why does a resorption-formation gap need months to become a stress fracture, and why might the person feel completely fine along the way?

Part E · Reason About a Bottleneck

  1. Trabecular bone remodels faster than cortical bone. Why does this mean some of the earliest evidence of bone loss under low estrogen shows up in the spine, hip, or wrist before overall density has moved very far?
  2. Why is calcium and vitamin D supplementation, on its own, an incomplete response to a quiet cycle in a young athlete?

Where education ends
This worksheet builds literacy about bone remodeling and estrogen, not a license to assess, diagnose, or treat, in yourself or anyone else. A persistently absent or irregular cycle, especially alongside a stress fracture, is a plausible signal worth naming, not a pattern to interpret, quantify, or reassure someone about. Recognize the pattern, then refer it.
