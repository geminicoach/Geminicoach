When the Cycle Goes Quiet

A missing period is not a diagnosis. It is a question, and the answer matters enormously.

34 min read 8 cited sources

Three people tell you their cycle has gone silent. The first started a progestin-only contraceptive method four months ago and has had no bleed since. The second is a physique competitor twelve weeks out from a show, eating in a deep deficit and training twice a day; her last period was in the spring. The third is thirty-four, eats plenty, trains moderately, is not on any hormonal method, and has simply stopped bleeding for the past four months with no explanation she can find.

Three quiet cycles. On the surface they look almost identical. Underneath, they could not mean more different things. One is expected and benign. One is the body powering down its reproductive machinery to conserve resources. One is a genuine unknown that needs a clinician. Confusing them is not a small error. It is the kind of error that delays care for someone who needs it, or manufactures alarm for someone who does not. This chapter is about learning to tell them apart at the level of recognition, and then knowing exactly where your job ends.

Throughout this chapter you will follow one thread, a learner named Naledi, a twenty-seven-year-old amateur triathlete who spent the spring ramping her training block for a half-Ironman while working long shifts and, without ever quite deciding to, eating less than the block demanded. Her period, which had been reliable for a decade, went quiet five months ago. She feels tired but functional, which is exactly the trap. Naledi is not a patient in this course and not a case for you to solve. She is a stand-in for the honest confusion that a quiet cycle produces, in the person living it and in anyone trying to make sense of it from the outside, and by the end of this chapter you will understand her situation more clearly than a supplement label or a training forum ever could. Her story is a thread, not a diagnosis, and we will hold that distinction carefully the whole way through.

Amenorrhea is a symptom, not a disease

Start with vocabulary, because precision here prevents most of the trouble. Amenorrhea simply means the absence of menstruation. Clinicians split it into two forms. Primary amenorrhea is the failure to begin menstruating by the expected age in adolescence. Secondary amenorrhea is the stopping of periods in someone who previously had them, typically defined as no bleeding for three or more consecutive months in a person who had a prior regular pattern, or six months in a person whose cycles were already irregular. The 2025 update to the Female Athlete Triad Coalition consensus statement notes that in adolescents, sustained energy deficiency can itself delay the first period or cause primary amenorrhea, so the two categories are not as cleanly separated as the labels suggest (De Souza et al., 2025). A young athlete whose periods never started on schedule and an adult whose periods stopped after years of regularity can be living through versions of the same underlying process, just met at different points along a life stage.

Here is the single most important idea in the chapter, and everything else hangs from it: amenorrhea is a symptom, not a diagnosis. It is the smoke, not the fire. A review from Cedars-Sinai's Barbra Streisand Women's Heart Center opens with a line worth memorizing outright: the menstrual cycle functions as a reproductive vital sign, one that provides insight into hormonal balance well beyond fertility, because the same signal that governs ovulation also touches bone, the cardiovascular system, and mental health (Shufelt et al., 2017). Read that claim carefully. It means a quiet cycle is not primarily a fertility problem you can set aside if pregnancy is not the goal. It is a reading on a gauge that reflects the state of the whole system, the way a fever reflects an immune response rather than being the illness itself. When the cycle goes quiet, the correct first response is not "what is the treatment" but "what is this a sign of." That reframing, from answer to question, is the entire posture this chapter asks you to adopt.

Hold that vital sign idea against a habit that is worth naming and retiring. It is tempting, and common, to treat an absent period as background noise, something to note and move past, especially in a fitness or training context where a missing cycle can even get framed as a mark of discipline. The Cedars-Sinai review has a phrase for the population this framing puts at risk: the "walking well," young women who feel fine day to day and have no obvious symptoms of low estrogen, and who are therefore unaware of the consequences quietly accumulating underneath (Shufelt et al., 2017). Feeling fine is not the same as being fine. That gap, between subjective feeling and physiological status, is exactly why the vital sign framing matters. Nobody would shrug off a resting heart rate that would not come down. A cycle that has gone silent for months deserves the same category of attention, calm rather than alarmed, but attentive rather than dismissive.

Three categories that look alike and mean different things

Sort the causes of a quiet cycle into three broad buckets. You are not diagnosing which bucket a real person belongs in; you are learning that the buckets exist, that they are not interchangeable, and that the same visible fact, no bleeding, sits at the top of all three.

The first is a medically induced change. This is the absent bleed from the progestin-based contraceptive methods covered in the previous chapter. The hormonal input is deliberately altering the endometrial lining so that there is little or nothing to shed, or in some methods also suppressing ovulation as part of how the method works. The reproductive axis may be doing exactly what the medication intends, on a timeline a clinician anticipated when the method was prescribed. This is expected, it is documented, and on its own it is benign. It is the one category where a quiet cycle is not, by itself, a warning.

The second is a functional or hypothalamic pattern. Here the axis has not been switched off by a drug; it has quietly powered itself down in response to a signal that resources are scarce, or that the body has read the environment as unsafe or overloaded. This is the pattern that sits squarely in the middle of everything this course cares about, because it lives at the intersection of training load, nutrition, psychological stress, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis you met in the chapter on the conversation between brain and ovary. We will spend most of this chapter here, because it is the category most likely to be misread, in both directions, by people without a clinical background.

The third is a pathological cause. This is the quiet cycle that reflects underlying disease: a pituitary tumor, a thyroid disorder, primary ovarian insufficiency, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), an outflow-tract problem such as scarring inside the uterus, or another organic condition. This category is precisely why you do not diagnose. Some of its members are serious, some are treatable only if caught early, and none of them can be ruled in or out by looking at training logs and food diaries, however carefully kept.

Notice what the three buckets share and what separates them. All three can present as the identical, single visible fact: no bleeding for months. What separates them is not anything you can see from the outside. It is the mechanism underneath, whether the axis is intentionally altered by medication, whether it has powered itself down in response to a resource signal, or whether something structural or disease-related is interrupting it. The visible symptom is one thing. The three very different stories that can produce it are another, and the entire discipline of this chapter is refusing to collapse that difference.

The functional or hypothalamic pattern, up close

Recall the machinery from earlier in the course. The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses, generated by a small network of neurons that behaves like a metronome, firing in bursts and then falling briefly silent before firing again. Those pulses drive the pituitary to release the two gonadotropins, luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). Those in turn drive the ovary to grow a follicle, produce estradiol, ovulate, and produce progesterone. The whole cadence, from GnRH pulse to LH and FSH release to ovarian response, depends on the pulse generator in the hypothalamus keeping time. Slow the metronome and everything downstream slows with it.

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA) is what happens when that pulse generator slows down. The pulses become sparse and irregular, luteinizing hormone secretion loses its rhythm, the gonadotropins fall, estradiol drops, and the cycle fades to silence. Crucially, nothing is broken. There is no tumor, no failed ovary, no structural fault. The system is intact and is choosing, in effect, to spend less on a metabolically expensive process, reproduction, because it has read the environment as one of scarcity or threat. A peer-reviewed review describes functional hypothalamic amenorrhea as a reversible disorder driven by suppression of GnRH pulsatility, arising from low energy availability, psychological stress, disordered eating, excessive exercise, or some combination of these (Morrison et al., 2021). Read that list again: it is rarely one clean cause. Real presentations tend to braid two or three of these threads together, which is one more reason a non-clinician cannot responsibly sort out which thread matters most in a given person.

How common is this pattern? More common than most people assume. That same review identifies functional hypothalamic amenorrhea as the most common cause of secondary amenorrhea in women of reproductive age (Morrison et al., 2021). A Mayo Clinic Proceedings review puts it at roughly one-third of all secondary amenorrhea cases and, importantly, notes that it is frequently under-recognized and under-treated, with real gaps remaining in how well it is studied given how common it is (Saadedine et al., 2023). That combination, common and overlooked, is exactly why a course like this asks you to learn to notice it, and exactly why noticing it is a genuinely useful skill even though acting on it clinically is not your role.

Energy availability, not body fat, is the lever

For decades the folk model held that women stop menstruating when they get "too lean," as if body fat percentage were the switch. The evidence dismantled that story. The pivotal work here belongs to Anne Loucks, whose research established that reproductive function is regulated by energy availability, defined as dietary energy intake minus the energy expended in exercise, expressed relative to lean body mass. When energy availability falls below a threshold of roughly twenty to thirty kilocalories per kilogram of lean body mass per day, the disruption of luteinizing hormone pulsatility appears. Body fatness, per se, is not the regulator (Loucks, 2003).

Two consequences of that finding are worth holding onto. First, exercise itself has no suppressive effect on the menstrual cycle beyond its energy cost. The problem is not that training is inherently harmful to the cycle; the problem is the arithmetic of intake minus expenditure. A person can create a deficit large enough to matter through eating too little, training too much, or both, and the axis responds to the net figure, not to the training in isolation (Loucks, 2003). Second, because the driver is a signal about resources rather than a structural injury, the pattern is generally reversible when energy availability is restored. That is a hopeful fact, but it is a clinical fact, not a license for a non-clinician to prescribe a fix, a point this chapter will return to more than once.

It is worth being precise about why a signal about scarcity would shut down reproduction specifically, rather than some other system. From an evolutionary standpoint, pregnancy and lactation are among the most energetically expensive processes a body can undertake. A system that continued to invest heavily in reproduction during a genuine famine or a sustained overload would be making a poor trade. The GnRH pulse generator sits downstream of signals related to available energy, including metabolic hormones that track fuel status, and when those signals read scarcity for long enough, the pulse generator slows as a conservation measure. This is not malfunction. It is the same axis, working correctly, applying a very old rule under conditions the rule was built for.

Weight-cutting and physique sport

Certain contexts concentrate this risk. Sports and pursuits that reward leanness, restrict intake, or demand large training volumes push energy availability down almost by design. Physique sport is a clear example. A cross-sectional study of one hundred and four female physique athletes found that 58.65 percent reported menstrual cycle disorders, defined as amenorrhea of three or more consecutive months, and 46.15 percent screened as at risk of low energy availability on the Low Energy Availability in Females Questionnaire (LEAF-Q). The women reporting menstrual disturbance were younger and trained more per week, and menstrual changes tracked with increased exercise intensity, frequency, and duration (Witkoś et al., 2024). When more than half of a population reports a silent or disrupted cycle, the pattern is not an anomaly. It is close to an occupational feature of that sport.

Endurance sport carries a related, if differently shaped, version of the same risk. Long training blocks, especially ones layered on top of work, travel, or other life demands, can quietly erode energy availability over weeks even when nothing about the training looks extreme on any single day. This is Naledi's situation. Nothing about her spring looked reckless. She did not cut calories on purpose, and she did not increase her mileage dramatically overnight. She simply added a demanding training block to an already full life, and her appetite did not keep pace with the new cost. Five months later her cycle is silent, and she has no idea why, because nothing about what she did felt like a decision to stop menstruating. That is precisely how this pattern usually arrives. It rarely announces itself. It accumulates.

This is the domain where recognition skills matter most, because it is where a person is most likely to encounter someone in the functional pattern, and where the surrounding culture is most likely to treat a missing period as a badge of dedication rather than a signal worth acting on. Learning to see it plainly, without either panic or dismissal, is the skill this chapter is built to teach.

Widening the lens: Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport

Menstrual disruption is rarely a solitary event. The older framework called this cluster the Female Athlete Triad, linking energy status, reproductive function, and bone health. The 2025 update to that consensus statement retains the three interrelated components, adds the idea of metabolic compensation to the energy-status continuum, and broadens the reproductive-function continuum to include the full range of ovarian steroid hormone exposure and functional hypothalamic oligo-amenorrhea, meaning infrequent as well as fully absent cycles (De Souza et al., 2025). Oligomenorrhea, cycles that arrive but are unusually spaced out, deserves a mention here because it is easy to miss. A cycle that stretches from twenty-eight days to fifty or sixty days is not "absent," but it can reflect the exact same underlying process as a fully silent one, just earlier along the same slope.

The International Olympic Committee took the concept further under the name Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S). The 2023 consensus statement expands the model beyond women to both sexes and across all levels of participation, and frames low energy availability as a spectrum running from adaptable, where the body adjusts without lasting harm, to problematic, where health and function suffer. Menstrual dysfunction sits as a central consequence within that model, alongside effects on bone density, immune function, cardiovascular health, growth and development in younger athletes, and psychological health, and the statement provides structured clinical assessment tools for risk stratification (Mountjoy et al., 2023). The reason to know this framework is not so a non-clinician can score anyone on it. It is to understand that a quiet cycle in this context is often the visible tip of a larger disturbance touching bone, cardiovascular health, and more, which is one more reason it belongs with a clinician rather than with a coach, trainer, or well-meaning friend.

Naledi's story fits this widened lens well. A missing period is the symptom she can name, but the underlying process, if it is functional, is not really about her reproductive system in isolation. It is about her whole body running a sustained energy deficit that her cycle happens to be the most visible signal of. If that deficit has been running for five months, it is reasonable to wonder what else it may be touching that she cannot see or feel yet, which is a question a clinician, not a training log, is equipped to answer.

How medically induced quiet differs, mechanically

It is worth slowing down on the first bucket, because "the method is doing this on purpose" is true but easy to state too simply. Not every progestin-based method quiets the cycle the same way, and understanding the differences sharpens the contrast with the functional pattern rather than blurring it.

Continuous progestin exposure, whether from a pill taken every day, an implant, or an injection, thins the endometrial lining directly. Progestin opposes the buildup that estrogen normally drives, so the tissue that would otherwise be shed each month simply does not accumulate to a bleed-worthy thickness. That is the primary mechanism behind the light, irregular, or absent bleeding many people notice on these methods, and it is a local, tissue-level effect more than a whole-axis shutdown.

Some methods add a second layer on top of that local effect. Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, the injectable method, reliably suppresses ovulation for most users by acting at the level of the hypothalamus and pituitary, damping the gonadotropin signal enough that the ovary does not release an egg for the duration of the injection's effect. The etonogestrel implant does something similar, though less universally, suppressing ovulation in a large share of cycles while allowing some ovarian activity in others. A hormonal intrauterine device delivering levonorgestrel sits at the opposite end of that spectrum: its progestin acts predominantly at the local, endometrial level, with comparatively little effect on the broader hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, so many users continue to ovulate more or less normally even while their bleeding is minimal or absent.

Here is the useful comparison to functional hypothalamic amenorrhea. In a medically induced quiet cycle, the intervention is deliberate, dosed, monitored by a clinician, and reversible on a known timeline once the method is stopped. The person did not lose access to their reproductive hormones by accident; a method was chosen, with informed consent, specifically because that effect was wanted or was an acceptable tradeoff for effective contraception. In functional hypothalamic amenorrhea, nothing was chosen. The axis powered down as an unplanned, unmonitored response to an energy or stress signal that nobody was tracking as a variable, and the person experiencing it usually has no idea it is happening until the bleeding stops and they start asking questions. Same visible outcome, opposite story about intention, monitoring, and cause.

This distinction has a practical consequence for recognition. If someone reports a quiet cycle and there is a clear, recent, matching medically induced explanation, an appropriately timed method start with no other red flags, that pattern deserves to be read as expected and benign, exactly as the first bucket describes. But the moment the timeline does not fit cleanly, the method changed months ago and the bleeding pattern shifted only recently, or there is no method at all, the induced explanation stops covering the facts, and the situation moves toward the second or third bucket by default.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common assumption holds that any quiet cycle in someone using birth control is automatically explained by the method, full stop, no further thought needed. That shortcut works often enough to feel reliable, which is exactly what makes it risky. A hormonal method can coexist with an unrelated cause of amenorrhea, including a functional pattern or a pathological one. The presence of a plausible explanation is not the same as the confirmed explanation, and a non-clinician cannot tell the difference between "explained by the method" and "coincidentally also happening for another reason" from the outside.

Pathological causes: when the axis is not choosing to quiet

The third bucket is the one this course asks the most humility about, because it is the one where recognizing a pattern and understanding a pattern are furthest apart. A brief tour of what belongs here is not meant to turn anyone into a diagnostician. It is meant to show why the pathological bucket cannot be told apart from the functional one by looking, which is the entire justification for the boundary the next section is built around.

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common causes of irregular or absent cycles, and it offers a genuinely instructive contrast with functional hypothalamic amenorrhea at the level of the axis itself. Where functional hypothalamic amenorrhea involves a pulse generator running slow and sparse, PCOS more often involves a GnRH pulse generator running fast, favoring luteinizing hormone production over follicle-stimulating hormone and contributing to the elevated luteinizing hormone to follicle-stimulating hormone ratio frequently seen in the condition. That faster rhythm, combined with elevated androgens and disrupted follicle development, produces irregular ovulation or none at all, alongside a cluster of other features that can include acne, excess hair growth, and metabolic changes such as insulin resistance. The mechanism runs in nearly the opposite direction from functional hypothalamic amenorrhea, even though both can present as a missing period.

Thyroid disorders, both an underactive and an overactive thyroid, can disrupt the menstrual cycle. An underactive thyroid raises thyrotropin-releasing hormone, which can in turn raise prolactin, and elevated prolactin suppresses GnRH pulsatility much the way an energy deficit does, producing a quiet cycle through a completely different starting cause that happens to converge on a similar downstream pathway.

A prolactin-secreting pituitary tumor, called a prolactinoma, is a further example of that same convergence. Excess prolactin directly suppresses the GnRH pulse generator, and depending on the size and location of the tumor, it can also produce headaches, visual changes, and milky nipple discharge unrelated to breastfeeding, a symptom called galactorrhea. A quiet cycle paired with that combination of features is a recognizable red flag pattern, not because a non-clinician should attempt to name the tumor, but because the combination is specific enough that it should move a situation immediately and without hesitation toward professional evaluation.

Primary ovarian insufficiency is different again. Here the problem sits at the ovary itself, which stops responding normally well before the age typically expected for that decline. Because the ovary is not producing estradiol at the level the brain expects, the pituitary compensates by raising follicle-stimulating hormone, often substantially. This produces a hormonal signature nearly the opposite of functional hypothalamic amenorrhea: where functional hypothalamic amenorrhea shows low gonadotropins and low estradiol because the brain has quieted its signal, primary ovarian insufficiency shows high follicle-stimulating hormone and low estradiol because the ovary itself is not answering a normal signal.

Notice the pattern across all four examples. Each one can present, to a person or to someone observing from the outside, as exactly the same thing Naledi is living through: a missing period, otherwise feeling more or less normal. Distinguishing between them requires blood work, sometimes imaging, and clinical interpretation of results in context, none of which is available to pattern recognition alone. This is not a gap in your education that more reading would close. It is a gap that exists by design, because these conditions genuinely require laboratory and clinical tools to separate.

Think About It

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea and primary ovarian insufficiency can look identical from the outside, a missing period and nothing else obviously wrong, yet one involves gonadotropins that are too low and the other involves follicle-stimulating hormone that is too high. What does that tell you about the value of guessing a cause from symptoms alone, without laboratory testing?

Recognize and refer: where your job ends

This is the heart of the chapter, and it is worth stating flatly. The job of a learner in this course is to recognize when a quiet cycle is not the expected result of contraception and may signal something worth professional evaluation. The limit of that job is exactly there. Evaluation belongs to a clinician. Nobody outside that role diagnoses, treats, or positions themselves to manage the situation, no matter how confident the pattern recognition feels.

The reason this boundary is non-negotiable is written into the definition of the functional pattern itself. Every serious clinical source treats functional hypothalamic amenorrhea as a diagnosis of exclusion. The Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline is explicit: the diagnosis can only be made after anatomic and organic pathology have been ruled out, and it sets specific thresholds, a cycle interval persistently longer than forty-five days or amenorrhea of three months or more, as the point at which evaluation is warranted (Gordon et al., 2017). "Diagnosis of exclusion" means the pattern cannot be arrived at simply by recognizing it. It is arrived at only by first excluding the pathological causes, and excluding those requires examination, laboratory work, and sometimes imaging, all entirely outside the scope of anyone without clinical training. Looking at a physique competitor in a deep deficit and thinking "this looks functional" may well be an accurate read, and it is still not a conclusion anyone without a license is entitled to draw, because the tumor, the thyroid disorder, and the ovarian insufficiency described in the previous section can wear the exact same face.

So the correct action, whenever a quiet cycle is not a clearly expected induced change, is the same regardless of how confident the pattern recognition feels: encourage evaluation by a qualified clinician. Not because the pattern was misread, but because reading it was never the deliverable, and because the guideline authors, the people who actually wrote the diagnostic criteria, built exclusion into the definition on purpose, fully aware that confident-looking cases are sometimes wrong.

Recognize And Refer

Recognition and referral are two separate skills, and this course teaches only the first. Recognizing means noticing that a cycle has gone quiet, understanding which broad category it might plausibly fit, and knowing that the categories carry very different weight. Referring means saying, plainly and without hedging, that this is worth bringing to a clinician, and then stepping back. It does not mean naming a condition, suggesting a supplement or a training change intended to fix it, or reassuring anyone that it is "probably nothing." The moment recognition turns into a recommendation about what to do medically, the line has been crossed.

Why the boundary protects the person, not just you

It is tempting to read "recognize and refer" as professional self-protection, a way to stay out of trouble. It is more than that. Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea carries real downstream consequences: hypoestrogenism, bone loss, and elevated cardiovascular risk, with additional effects on cognition, sexual function, fertility, and mental health described across the literature (Morrison et al., 2021; Saadedine et al., 2023; Shufelt et al., 2017). The Endocrine Society guideline recommends a multidisciplinary approach to treatment, typically involving medical, nutritional, and mental health support together, precisely because the condition reaches into several body systems at once (Gordon et al., 2017). Someone in this pattern needs coordinated professional care, not a well-meaning adjustment to their diet or training suggested by a person without the training to manage the whole picture. Referring is not passing the buck. It is routing the person to the level of care the condition actually requires, delivered by people equipped to deliver it.

Think About It

If functional hypothalamic amenorrhea is often reversible by restoring energy availability, why is it still wrong for a non-clinician to simply advise someone to eat more and train less and call it handled? What could that miss?

A word on the evidence

Honesty about the science is part of the job. The energy-availability threshold from Loucks is robust and foundational, and the recognition of functional hypothalamic amenorrhea as common and under-treated is well supported. But a great deal of the popular advice built around the menstrual cycle, the training periodization schemes keyed to cycle phases, the phase-specific nutrition protocols, rests on weak, inconsistent, or contradictory data. The frameworks in this chapter, the Triad and Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, are consensus statements, which means they represent expert synthesis and are revised as evidence accumulates, not settled physical law. Hold the strong findings firmly and the speculative ones loosely, and remember that clinical judgment and the lived expertise of the person in front of the science both matter alongside the distilled research.

Case study: Naledi and the season that went quiet

Case

Naledi is twenty-seven, an amateur triathlete training for her first half-Ironman. She works full time, trains most mornings before work and long hours on weekends, and describes her eating as "whatever fits." Nothing about her plan looked extreme to her. She did not set out to under-fuel, skip meals on purpose, or chase a particular number on a scale. Her period, regular since her teens, went quiet five months ago. She feels tired but otherwise fine, sleeps reasonably well, and has kept up with her training block without missing a planned session. She is not sure whether the missing periods are a big deal or a normal side effect of "training hard," and she has not mentioned it to anyone until now.

Run Naledi's situation through this chapter and the confusion does not resolve into a diagnosis, which is not available here and never will be from a course. It resolves into a clear set of questions and a clear next step. Start with the three buckets. Naledi is not using a hormonal method, so the first bucket, the expected induced change, does not apply. That single fact already narrows the field considerably and is worth noticing: ruling something out is itself useful information, even without ruling anything in.

Move to the functional pattern. Her situation lines up with the mechanism described earlier in this chapter with uncomfortable precision. A demanding training block, added on top of an already full life, without a matching increase in intake, is close to a textbook description of falling energy availability. Nothing about her week looks like the popular image of an eating disorder or an obviously punishing training log, and that is exactly the point Loucks's research makes: the axis responds to the net number, intake minus expenditure relative to lean mass, not to how dramatic the situation looks from the outside (Loucks, 2003). Five months of silence also crosses the threshold the Endocrine Society guideline flags as warranting evaluation, three months or more of amenorrhea (Gordon et al., 2017). On the surface, this looks like a strong candidate for functional hypothalamic amenorrhea.

Here is where the discipline of this chapter has to hold. "Looks like a strong candidate" is not the same as "is." Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea is a diagnosis of exclusion, which means the pathological bucket from earlier in the chapter, the thyroid disorder, the prolactin-secreting tumor, the possibility of primary ovarian insufficiency even at twenty-seven, has to be ruled out first, and ruling it out requires blood work that nobody outside a clinical setting can order or interpret. Naledi feeling generally well is reassuring in an everyday sense, and it is not evidence one way or the other, because several of the pathological causes covered earlier can also present with someone feeling generally well, at least at first. The "walking well" phrase from earlier in this chapter describes her precisely, and it describes why appearances cannot resolve this (Shufelt et al., 2017).

So what does the chapter actually offer Naledi? Not a diagnosis, and not a training or nutrition fix dressed up as one. It offers her an accurate map of what her missing period might mean, three genuinely different possibilities, a clear reason why guessing between them from the outside is unreliable even when the guess feels obvious, and a specific, actionable next step: bring this to a clinician who can order the appropriate testing, a step that is warranted by her own timeline under the guideline's own threshold. That is not a smaller outcome than a confident answer would have been. It is a more honest one, and it is the one that actually protects her, whether the eventual finding is a functional pattern that responds to restored energy availability under proper care, or something else entirely that would have gone unaddressed if everyone involved had simply assumed "it's probably just the training."

Key Takeaways

  • A quiet cycle is a symptom and a question, never a diagnosis in itself. The menstrual cycle functions as a reproductive vital sign, reflecting the state of bone, cardiovascular, and mental health, not just fertility.
  • Sort quiet cycles into three buckets that look alike but mean different things: an expected medically induced change, a functional or hypothalamic pattern, and a possible pathological cause.
  • Medically induced changes differ by mechanism, from local endometrial thinning to whole-axis ovulation suppression, but they share deliberate dosing, clinical monitoring, and a known, reversible timeline, which is what separates them from an unplanned functional shutdown.
  • Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA) is the most common cause of secondary amenorrhea, is often under-recognized, and reflects suppressed gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulsatility, not a broken system.
  • Energy availability, roughly intake minus exercise energy expenditure per kilogram of lean mass, is the regulator. Below about twenty to thirty kilocalories per kilogram of lean mass per day, the cycle is disrupted. Body fatness is not the switch, and exercise does not suppress the cycle beyond its energy cost.
  • Physique sport and demanding endurance training concentrate this risk; in one physique-athlete sample, over half reported menstrual disorders, and the pattern often accumulates quietly rather than announcing itself.
  • Pathological causes such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), thyroid disorders, prolactin-secreting pituitary tumors, and primary ovarian insufficiency can produce an identical visible symptom through very different, sometimes nearly opposite, hormonal mechanisms, which is exactly why they cannot be told apart without laboratory testing.
  • Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea is a diagnosis of exclusion. A pattern can be recognized, but it cannot be concluded, because the pathological causes wear the same face and can only be ruled out clinically.
  • Recognize and refer. For any quiet cycle that is not a clearly expected induced change, encourage clinician evaluation. Recognition is not a license to diagnose, treat, or manage.
  • Grade the evidence honestly: the energy-availability threshold is strong, but much cycle-based training and nutrition advice rests on weak or contradictory data.
Looking Ahead

We have named energy availability as the lever behind the functional pattern and left it mostly as a single number. Next, we open that number up. The following chapter is a deep dive into energy availability itself, how it is conceived, why the threshold behaves the way it does, and what the spectrum from adaptable to problematic really looks like in practice under the Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) model, so that the pattern learned here gains the depth to make recognition sharper and referral more grounded.
