Part C · Two Kinds of Reasoning

  1. Explain why iron is a stronger, better-supported claim than phase-based lifting, even though both involve the menstrual cycle.
  2. Distinguish hunger from craving. Why does cycle-syncing marketing benefit from blurring the two together?
  3. A population-level review finds no reliable average effect of cycle phase on strength. Explain why an individual can still have a real, personal, repeatable pattern of her own without that fact contradicting the review.

Part D · Trace the Reasoning

A friend shares a claim from a popular cycle-syncing app. Apply the chapter's four-question decision frame to it, then answer the fifth question about your own tracked data.

  1. How was cycle phase determined in the underlying research, by blood measurement or by calendar?
  2. How many participants, and were regularly ovulating cycles actually confirmed?
  3. Are the results consistent with the broader body of work, or do they contradict the higher-level reviews?
  4. Is the effect large enough to change what a real person should do, or is it a trivial, statistically detectable blip?

Part E · Reason About a Bottleneck

  1. Explain, in terms of absorption efficiency, why red meat eaten thoughtfully around heavy flow is a reasonable food-first response to menstrual iron loss.
  2. Why does the chapter treat low iron symptoms (persistent fatigue, unusual breathlessness, restless legs) as a reason to seek a blood test rather than to self-diagnose or self-supplement?

Where education ends
This worksheet builds literacy for grading cycle-based claims honestly, not a license to diagnose, prescribe supplements, or manage amenorrhea, contraception, or the postpartum period. Prolonged absence of menstruation, and any decision to test or supplement iron, belong to a qualified clinician. Recognize the pattern, then refer it.
