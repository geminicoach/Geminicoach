The Cost of Running on Empty

When the fuel left over for the body's own work runs short, physiology starts making hard choices.

18 min read 8 cited sources

Imagine a person training hard, eating what feels like a full plate, and doing everything a committed athlete is supposed to do. Their splits are stagnant. They catch every passing cold. A stress reaction shows up in a foot. And their period, which used to arrive quietly and reliably, has gone silent for months. Nobody is starving themselves. Nobody skipped meals on purpose. And yet the body is behaving as though a famine has arrived.

This is not a rare edge case. It is one of the most common and most under-recognized problems in sport, and it is the single most important health topic in this course. It also happens to be widely misread as vanity, as an eating disorder, or as simple overtraining. It is often none of those. To understand it, we have to look at what is left in the tank after the workout is done.

What energy availability actually means

In the previous chapter we traced how the menstrual cycle works as a functional signal, quiet or loud, depending on what the body is coping with. Now we follow that signal to its most powerful upstream cause: energy availability.

Energy availability is not the same as how much you eat, and it is not the same as energy balance. It is defined as dietary energy intake minus the energy burned during exercise, and then normalized to fat-free mass, usually expressed in kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day (Areta et al., 2020). Put plainly, it is the energy left over for everything the body does that is not exercise: repairing tissue, thinking, fighting infection, regulating hormones, keeping bone alive, and running reproduction.

The mental model that matters is a household budget. Income is what you eat. One large, non-negotiable expense is the training you have already committed to. Energy availability is what is left in the account after that expense clears, and it is that remainder, not the gross income, that has to cover rent, utilities, and groceries for the rest of the body. You can eat what looks like plenty and still leave the core functions underfunded, simply because exercise took the larger share first.

This distinction is the whole game. A person can be in apparent energy balance, weight stable on the scale, and still be running their internal systems on a deficit, because the body has quietly downshifted those systems to make the math work (Areta et al., 2020). Weight stability reassures people that everything is fine. It is not a reliable reassurance.

Think About It

Two people eat the same 2,400 kilocalories in a day. One does an easy 30-minute walk; the other completes a two-hour training session. Same intake, same body size. Why might only one of them be at risk of low energy availability, and why would the bathroom scale fail to reveal it?

Low, intentional, and unintentional

Low energy availability is what happens when that remainder falls too far, for too long. Crucially, it arrives by several different roads, and the road matters for how we think about it, though not for how firmly we hand assessment to professionals.

Sometimes low energy availability is intentional: deliberate weight cutting for a weight-class sport, a physique-sport push toward extreme leanness, or restrictive eating tied to a psychological condition. Sometimes it is entirely unintentional: a dedicated person increases training volume across a season and simply never scales intake to match, or has an appetite that does not keep pace with output, or follows well-meant advice to "stay lean" without understanding the cost (Nattiv et al., 2007). The unintentional path is important precisely because it can happen to a thoughtful, disciplined person who believes they are doing everything right.

The landmark position stand from the American College of Sports Medicine made this point explicitly: low energy availability can be inadvertent, intentional, or driven by disordered eating, and it is the low energy availability itself, with or without an eating disorder, that drives the downstream harm (Nattiv et al., 2007). This is why it is a mistake to collapse this topic entirely into "disordered eating." The two can overlap, and often do, but they are not the same thing, and treating them as identical causes us to miss the person who is undereating for their output without any eating pathology at all.

Where the threshold sits, and how sure we are

The most-cited number in this field comes from a controlled experiment by Loucks and Thuma (2003), who studied 29 regularly menstruating women and found that luteinizing hormone pulsatility, the pulsing hormonal signal from the brain that drives the ovarian cycle, was disrupted when energy availability dropped below roughly 30 kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day. Above that threshold, the pulses held steady; below it, pulse frequency fell. This experiment is the mechanistic backbone of the whole framework, showing a direct line from the energy budget to the brain's control of reproduction.

Here honesty is required. That 30 kilocalorie threshold is a useful landmark, but it is not a bright line that applies identically to every person. It emerged from short-term laboratory manipulations in a specific group, and individual variation is large. The most current International Olympic Committee consensus deliberately moved away from a single magic number, distinguishing instead between adaptable low energy availability, which the body can tolerate for a while, and problematic low energy availability, which produces harm, and noting that where that line falls depends on the individual (Mountjoy et al., 2023). Much of the evidence beyond short-term studies is associative rather than causal (Areta et al., 2020). So use the threshold as a concept, not as a diagnostic cutoff you can apply to yourself or anyone else.

From a quiet cycle to a whole-body triage

When the leftover falls too low, the body does not fail everywhere at once. It triages. It protects the functions needed for immediate survival and dials down the ones it can defer. Reproduction is expensive and, from a strict survival standpoint, deferrable, so it is often first to be sacrificed (Nattiv et al., 2007; Ihalainen et al., 2024). This is exactly why the menstrual cycle is such an honest early signal: it is the canary, going quiet before louder alarms sound.

But framing this purely as a menstrual issue badly understates its reach. That was the central insight behind the shift from the older female athlete triad to a broader framework. The triad, as formalized by the American College of Sports Medicine, described three interrelated points on a spectrum: energy availability, menstrual function, and bone mineral density (Nattiv et al., 2007). It was a major advance, but it left out much of what low energy availability actually touches, and it was framed around female athletes only.

In 2014 the International Olympic Committee introduced Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, abbreviated RED-S, to capture the full picture: not just menstrual and bone effects but impaired metabolic rate, immunity, protein synthesis, cardiovascular health, and more, in both female and male athletes (Mountjoy et al., 2014). The 2023 update integrated more than 170 new studies and refined the models further, but the core idea held: one upstream cause, many downstream systems, all connected (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

The systems, one by one

It helps to name what gets affected, because the whole point of the framework is that these are not separate problems.

Menstrual and reproductive. The disruption of luteinizing hormone pulsatility described by Loucks and Thuma (2003) suppresses the downstream cycle. This can range from subtle shortening of the luteal phase, to skipped ovulation, to full amenorrhea, the absence of menstrual periods. A promising line of thinking suggests that subtle markers, such as a missing luteinizing hormone surge or low luteal-phase progesterone, may reveal dysfunction before periods stop entirely (Ihalainen et al., 2024). This is a topic for qualified evaluation, not self-tracking to a conclusion.

Bone. This is the thread we carry directly into the next chapter. Reproductive hormones, estrogen in particular, are protective of bone. When low energy availability suppresses those hormones and simultaneously deprives bone of the raw energy and nutrients it needs to remodel, bone mineral density can fall and the risk of bone stress injuries rises (Nattiv et al., 2007; Gallant et al., 2024). A quiet cycle and weakening bone are not two coincidences. They share one cause.

Endocrine and metabolic. Beyond the reproductive axis, low energy availability lowers resting metabolic rate and disrupts thyroid signaling, appetite hormones, and other endocrine axes, part of the body's coordinated downshift (Mountjoy et al., 2014; Ihalainen et al., 2024). Iron status can also suffer, with knock-on effects on energy and performance.

Immune. The person who catches every cold is not imagining it. Impaired immunity and higher illness burden are documented consequences, and the meta-analysis by Gallant et al. (2024) found increased illness-related absence among athletes with low energy availability.

Performance. This is the cruel irony at the center of the topic, which we take up next.

The leanness trap: when less fuel dims performance

Perhaps the most damaging myth in leanness-focused sport is the belief that getting lighter or leaner always makes you faster or better. Within a healthy range, favorable body composition can help. But past a point, low energy availability actively degrades the very performance it is supposed to serve, and the person experiencing it often cannot feel the trade happening.

The evidence here has grown sharper. The first systematic review and meta-analysis on the topic pooled 59 studies covering 6,118 athletes and found that 44.7 percent had low energy availability, roughly comparable in female athletes (44.2 percent) and male athletes (49.4 percent) (Gallant et al., 2024). Athletes with low energy availability showed decreased running performance, blunted training response, reduced endurance, and declines in coordination, concentration, explosive power, and agility, along with more time lost to illness (Gallant et al., 2024). An earlier narrative review documented prevalence in the range of 22 to 58 percent across sports and reached similar conclusions about the performance cost (Logue et al., 2020).

So the leanness push can produce a person who is lighter, who believes leanness is helping, and who is measurably slower, weaker, and more injury-prone than they would otherwise be. The body is not being stubborn. It is doing exactly what a household in a budget crisis does: it stops investing in the future to pay the bills today, and adaptation, the thing training is supposed to buy, is a future investment.

One more honest note about the evidence. That same review by Logue et al. (2020) found that knowledge of RED-S and its performance implications remains low among athletes and coaches. So the pattern is common, costly, and poorly understood by the very people surrounded by it. That combination is why normalization is such a danger.

A committed distance runner has lost weight over a season, believes they are in the best shape of their life, and yet their times have plateaued and a stress reaction has appeared. Why the early signs get normalized

Sport cultures that prize leanness create the perfect conditions to miss this. A skipped period gets reframed as convenient. Constant hunger gets praised as discipline. Frequent minor illness gets shrugged off as bad luck. Cold hands, low mood, poor sleep, and stalled progress each get an isolated, benign explanation. Because low energy availability so often arrives unintentionally and without any dramatic eating pathology, there is no obvious villain to point to, and the individual pieces look small.

The framework's gift is that it teaches you to see the pieces as a set. A quiet cycle plus a nagging bone injury plus frequent illness plus stalled performance is not four small stories. It may be one story with one cause. Screening tools exist, such as the Low Energy Availability in Females Questionnaire, but these are instruments for trained professionals, and even they have limitations (Logue et al., 2020). Recognizing the constellation is within reach for an educated observer. Interpreting it, confirming it, and doing anything about it are not.

The recognize-and-refer boundary

This is the firmest line in the course, and it belongs especially around amenorrhea and RED-S. What this chapter equips you to do is recognize the pattern and understand its reach. What it does not do, and what you must not do, is diagnose, assess energy availability in a real person, prescribe intake or training changes, or manage the condition. The absence of periods in particular has many possible causes, some benign and some serious, and sorting them out is medical work.

The reasons for the boundary are not bureaucratic. Assessment requires clinical measurement and history. Care is genuinely multidisciplinary, often involving a physician, a registered dietitian, and a mental-health professional working together (Nattiv et al., 2007; Mountjoy et al., 2023). And getting it wrong carries real harm, both from missing a serious cause and from mishandling a fragile situation. Your role is to notice, to take it seriously, and to understand that what you have noticed may warrant professional evaluation. Then you hand it on.

A note of humility fits here too. This course distills a research literature that is still evolving, that leans heavily on short-term studies, and that has genuine gaps. Clinical expertise and lived female experience carry knowledge the studies have not captured. Holding the pattern lightly, and the boundary firmly, is the mature response to both.

Key Takeaways

  • Energy availability is the energy left for core body functions after exercise is paid for, normalized to fat-free mass. It is not intake and not energy balance, and weight can stay stable while it runs low.
  • Low energy availability can be intentional, from weight cutting or physique demands, or entirely unintentional in a committed person who simply undereats for their output. It is not the same thing as an eating disorder, though the two can overlap.
  • The suppression of luteinizing hormone pulsatility below roughly 30 kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day is the mechanistic landmark, but it is a concept, not a personal cutoff, and individual variation is large.
  • Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, or RED-S, ties menstrual, bone, endocrine, metabolic, immune, and performance effects to one upstream cause. A quiet cycle and weakening bone share that cause.
  • Low energy availability is common, affecting roughly 45 percent of athletes in pooled data, and it degrades performance even as leanness feels like an advantage.
  • Your role is to recognize the pattern and its reach and to understand it may warrant professional evaluation. Assessment, diagnosis, and care belong to qualified professionals, never to you, especially with amenorrhea and RED-S.
