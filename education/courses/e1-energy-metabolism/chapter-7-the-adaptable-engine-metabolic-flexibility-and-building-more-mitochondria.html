The Adaptable Engine: Metabolic Flexibility and Building More Mitochondria

Why the machinery you have been studying is not fixed, and how the way you live rebuilds it.

32 min read 8 cited sources

Picture two people stepping off a bus and breaking into a jog to catch a departing train. Both are the same age, the same weight, the same height. One accelerates smoothly, breathing hard but controlled, and arrives at the platform ready to sprint the last few steps. The other feels her legs flood with heaviness within twenty seconds, her breathing ragged, a burning tightness climbing her thighs. She slows. She misses the train. From the outside their bodies look identical. On the inside, one engine can change what it burns and how fast it burns it, and the other cannot.

That difference has a name, and it is largely trainable. Everything you have learned so far in this course described the engine as a fixed object: fuels, pathways, thresholds, a crossover point. In this chapter we do something more interesting. We watch the engine remodel itself in response to how it is used.

What Metabolic Flexibility Actually Means

Let us begin from the ground up, because this term gets used loosely. Your body runs on two main fuels during exercise and daily life: carbohydrate, stored in muscle and liver as a chain-molecule called glycogen and circulating in the blood as glucose, and fat, stored in fat tissue and inside muscle fibers as fatty acids. In earlier chapters you learned that these two fuels are burned in different proportions depending on how hard you are working. At rest and during low-intensity effort, fat supplies most of the energy. As intensity rises, the mix tips toward carbohydrate, and the intensity at which carbohydrate overtakes fat is the crossover point you met in Chapter 5.

Metabolic flexibility is the capacity of your body to switch cleanly between these fuels as the situation demands, and to match the fuel to the moment. Goodpaster and Sparks (2017), in a landmark review in the journal Cell Metabolism, define it precisely as the ability to respond or adapt to conditional changes in metabolic demand. A flexible body reads the situation: sleeping, it leans heavily on fat; digesting a carbohydrate-rich meal, it shifts toward burning and storing that incoming glucose; sprinting for a train, it pivots hard toward carbohydrate because carbohydrate is the only fuel that can be burned fast enough to meet the demand.

The opposite condition, metabolic inflexibility, is being stuck. An inflexible body oxidizes roughly the same fuel mixture regardless of what the situation calls for. Galgani and colleagues (2008), writing in the American Journal of Physiology: Endocrinology and Metabolism, describe flexibility as the capacity to adjust fuel oxidation to fuel availability, and inflexibility as the failure of that adjustment. The classic laboratory signature is a body that, when given a large dose of glucose, fails to increase its rate of glucose oxidation the way a healthy body does. It stays locked in the wrong gear.

Two everyday transitions test this capacity constantly. The first is the fasted-to-fed transition: what happens when food arrives after a period without it. The second is the rest-to-exercise transition: what happens when demand suddenly spikes. Smith and colleagues (2018), in a comprehensive review in Endocrine Reviews, trace flexibility across the liver, the fat tissue, and the muscle, showing that these organs must coordinate their sensing, trafficking, storage, and use of fuel for the whole system to respond well. Flexibility is not one switch. It is an orchestra staying in time.

How do researchers actually measure flexibility rather than just describe it in general terms? The main tool is a technique called indirect calorimetry, which measures the volume of oxygen you consume and the volume of carbon dioxide you produce while you breathe into a mouthpiece or under a ventilated hood. Burning fat and burning carbohydrate consume oxygen and release carbon dioxide in different fixed ratios, because fat molecules carry proportionally more hydrogen and less oxygen of their own than carbohydrate molecules do, so more external oxygen is needed to fully combust them. The ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed is called the respiratory exchange ratio (abbreviated RER), and it acts as a window onto the fuel mixture in real time. An RER near 0.70 indicates that fuel is coming almost entirely from fat; an RER near 1.00 indicates fuel coming almost entirely from carbohydrate; values in between reflect a blend. To test flexibility, researchers apply a defined challenge, either a carbohydrate-rich meal or a step up in exercise intensity, and track how quickly and how far the RER moves in the appropriate direction. In a flexible person the RER swings promptly and substantially. In an inflexible person it barely moves, which is the laboratory signature of a fuel-selection system that is not responding to a changed demand.

The biochemical machinery behind this switching has a name: the glucose-fatty acid cycle, first described by the physiologist Philip Randle and colleagues in the 1960s and often called the Randle cycle. Its core idea is competition between fuels for the same combustion machinery. When fatty acid delivery and oxidation are high, breakdown products of fat metabolism accumulate inside the muscle cell and interfere with enzymes in the glucose-oxidizing pathway, biasing the cell toward burning relatively more fat and less glucose. When carbohydrate availability rises instead, most prominently after a meal that raises insulin, the bias reverses through a specific molecular lock. An enzyme called acetyl-CoA carboxylase produces a small molecule called malonyl-CoA, and malonyl-CoA inhibits an enzyme called carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (abbreviated CPT-1), which is the gatekeeper enzyme that shuttles fatty acids across the mitochondrial membrane so they can be oxidized inside. Block CPT-1 and fatty acids cannot enter the furnace no matter how much fat is circulating outside it, which nudges the cell toward burning the newly arrived glucose instead. At the level of a single muscle cell, metabolic flexibility is the prompt, well-tuned operation of this switching mechanism in both directions. Metabolic inflexibility is this same mechanism getting stuck, most often locked toward carbohydrate use even when fat would be the more appropriate fuel, or unable to ramp up carbohydrate use when a meal or a hard effort specifically calls for it.

Consider the fasted-to-fed transition in this more mechanistic light, since it is one you live through several times a day. In the fasted state, insulin is low, fat tissue releases fatty acids into the blood, and the liver and muscle lean substantially on fat oxidation, which spares glucose for tissues such as the brain and red blood cells that depend on it more strictly. When a meal arrives, rising blood glucose triggers the pancreas to secrete insulin, which accomplishes several things at once: it suppresses further release of fatty acids from fat tissue, it drives glucose transporter proteins to the surface of muscle cells so they can take up circulating glucose, and, through the malonyl-CoA and CPT-1 mechanism just described, it dials down fat oxidation so the muscle burns the incoming glucose rather than continuing to burn fat while glucose accumulates unused in the blood. A metabolically flexible person completes this handoff within roughly one to two hours of eating. A metabolically inflexible person keeps oxidizing a similar amount of fat even after the meal, which leaves the newly arrived glucose with nowhere useful to go except storage, or, if storage capacity is also strained, an elevated and lingering blood glucose level.

Why Flexibility Matters for Effort and Fatigue

Flexibility is not an abstract laboratory measurement. It has direct consequences for how long you can keep going and how hard you can push. Recall from earlier chapters that your carbohydrate stores are small and precious. A well-fueled person carries only enough muscle and liver glycogen to sustain hard endurance exercise for roughly ninety minutes to two hours before those stores run critically low. Fat, by contrast, is essentially limitless for the purposes of exercise: even a lean athlete carries tens of thousands of stored energy units in fat, enough in principle for days of moderate effort.

Here is the payoff. If your body is good at burning fat during low and moderate effort, it spares that limited carbohydrate for the moments when only carbohydrate will do, such as a hill, a surge, or a finishing sprint. The metabolically flexible endurance athlete essentially budgets carbohydrate wisely, drawing on fat when the pace allows and reserving the fast fuel for when the pace demands it. The inflexible person burns through carbohydrate early even at gentle paces, arrives at the hard part of an effort with the tank already low, and fatigues.

That is the honest mechanistic picture. It is worth pausing here to correct a stubborn myth, because it touches this exact system. Many people believe that the burning sensation in hard-working muscles is caused by lactic acid, and that lactic acid is a waste product that later causes next-day muscle soreness. Both halves are wrong. Lactate is not a waste product at all; it is a usable fuel that your muscles, heart, and liver recycle, and the fittest people produce and clear it most effectively. The delayed soreness you feel a day or two after unfamiliar exercise is caused by microscopic mechanical damage to muscle fibers and the inflammation that follows, not by any acid lingering from the workout. Keep these separate in your mind. Flexibility and fatigue are about fuel supply and pathway capacity, not about an acid villain.

There is a well known term for what happens when carbohydrate reserves are prematurely exhausted during a long effort: hitting the wall, or "bonking" in cycling slang. The mechanism is now reasonably well characterized. As muscle and liver glycogen fall toward depletion, the muscle's capacity to regenerate adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the molecule that directly powers muscle contraction, at a high rate falls with it, because fat oxidation alone, however efficient, simply cannot resupply ATP as quickly as combined fat and carbohydrate oxidation can. Pace inevitably drops. At the same time, falling liver glycogen threatens blood glucose itself, and because the brain depends heavily on a steady glucose supply, the sensation of hitting the wall typically includes a cognitive fog and a demoralizing heaviness that many athletes describe as disproportionate to the purely muscular fatigue they feel. Researchers continue to debate how much of endurance fatigue originates in the muscle itself, called peripheral fatigue, and how much originates in the brain moderating effort to protect the body from harm, an idea popularized as the central governor hypothesis. That debate remains genuinely unresolved and sits beyond the scope of this chapter, but both camps agree on the fact that matters here: running low on carbohydrate degrades performance through more than one route at once, and metabolic flexibility, simply by delaying that depletion, defers all of it.

What Impairs Flexibility, Told Honestly

If flexibility can be strong, it can also be degraded. Two influences dominate the evidence, and they are worth separating from the many claims that are not well supported.

Chronic physical inactivity

The single most robustly documented cause of metabolic inflexibility in otherwise healthy people is simply not moving. The most striking evidence comes from bed-rest studies, in which volunteers lie down for days or weeks under careful supervision while researchers measure how their metabolism changes. Bergouignan and colleagues (2011), reviewing sixty years of such studies in the Journal of Applied Physiology, argue that physical inactivity by itself is a primary cause of metabolic inflexibility. They document a consistent set of consequences: the muscle becomes less responsive to insulin (the hormone that signals cells to take up glucose), fat handling in the blood becomes disordered, the whole system shifts toward relying on glucose rather than fat, and muscle fibers begin to change character while fat accumulates in places it should not, including inside the muscle itself.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is counterintuitive. Fat stored inside muscle fibers, which scientists call intramyocellular lipid, is not automatically bad; well-trained endurance athletes carry a lot of it and use it well as a convenient local fuel. The problem is fat that accumulates in an untrained, inactive muscle that cannot burn it. Galgani and colleagues (2008) tie this stagnant intramuscular fat directly to insulin resistance and inflexibility. The fuel is present but the machinery to use it is idle, and the byproducts of that idle fuel interfere with the insulin signal. Inactivity is not neutral. It actively reshapes the engine toward being stuck.

The molecular link between stagnant intramuscular fat and a jammed insulin signal is now reasonably well mapped. When fatty acids arrive at the muscle faster than the available mitochondria can oxidize them, or when there are simply too few mitochondria to keep pace in the first place, the surplus does not sit inertly as stored triglyceride. Some of it is diverted into two classes of bioactive lipid byproducts, diacylglycerol (abbreviated DAG) and ceramide, which interfere directly with the signaling chain that insulin normally uses to move glucose transporter proteins to the muscle cell surface. In parallel, mitochondria that are overloaded relative to their oxidative capacity leak more reactive oxygen species (abbreviated ROS), reactive molecules that, in excess, damage cellular proteins and further blunt insulin signaling. Researchers sometimes summarize this as a mismatch, mitochondria that are present but relatively too few for the amount of fat being delivered to them, rather than a simple absence of mitochondria. This mismatch framing matters practically, because it implies the fix is not primarily fasting or restricting fat intake; it is restoring the oxidative machinery itself, chiefly through aerobic exercise, so the mitochondria already on hand can once again keep pace with the fuel arriving at the cell.

Clinically, researchers quantify this kind of inflexibility with a technique called the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp. Insulin is infused intravenously at a constant, elevated rate while glucose is infused separately, in whatever amount is needed, to hold blood glucose steady at its normal level; indirect calorimetry then tracks how much the resulting forced rise in insulin actually pushes glucose oxidation upward. In a metabolically flexible person, glucose oxidation rises sharply under this forced insulin stimulus, since the muscle should switch decisively toward burning the fuel that insulin is signaling to be abundant. In an inflexible person, glucose oxidation barely moves, a direct laboratory demonstration of the stuck engine described throughout this section.

Consistent energy oversupply

The second well-supported influence is chronically taking in more energy than you use. When fuel arrives constantly and in excess, the systems that should sense scarcity and abundance lose their edge, much as a smoke alarm that goes off every time you cook eventually gets ignored. Smith and colleagues (2018) connect persistent energy oversupply to the loss of flexibility that characterizes metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes, a condition in which blood glucose stays elevated because cells no longer respond properly to insulin. Goodpaster and Sparks (2017) similarly describe the metabolic inflexibility that accompanies obesity and type 2 diabetes, in which the body neither ramps up glucose oxidation after a meal nor draws down on fat during fasting the way a flexible body does.

Mechanistically, chronic energy surplus produces a version of the same overload problem described above for inactivity, but arriving from the opposite direction. Rather than too few mitochondria for a given fat delivery in an idle muscle, there is simply more fuel, both fat and carbohydrate, arriving continuously than the existing oxidative machinery, however large it happens to be, can fully process. Expanded fat tissue itself compounds the problem: once fat cells enlarge substantially, fat tissue becomes a site of low-grade, chronic inflammation, releasing signaling molecules that further interfere with insulin sensitivity throughout the body, not just locally. The result converges on the same signature described for inactivity: blunted glucose oxidation after a meal, blunted fat oxidation during fasting, and a system that increasingly burns whatever happens to be circulating rather than what the moment actually calls for.

What is not your job to diagnose

Here we must be careful and honest. Genuine metabolic disorders exist. Type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and dysfunction of the thyroid gland (which sets your baseline metabolic rate) can all impair how your body handles fuel. But these are clinical conditions, and this chapter is education, not diagnosis. If you suspect something is genuinely wrong with your metabolism, that is a matter for a qualified healthcare professional who can run appropriate tests, not for self-diagnosis based on how tired you feel or how a workout went.

This is also the place to retire another myth. You may have heard that eating too little throws your body into "starvation mode," a supposed state in which metabolism crashes so severely that you stop losing fat or even gain it. The real physiology is more modest. When energy intake drops, your body does reduce its energy expenditure somewhat, partly because you carry less mass and partly through a genuine but limited efficiency adjustment. This is real and measurable, but it is not a magic lock that halts fat loss, and it is not the dramatic collapse the myth describes. Frame low energy availability, if it is prolonged and severe, as a genuine health concern worth professional attention, not as a metabolic curse.

The Adaptation That Changes Everything: Mitochondrial Biogenesis

Now we arrive at the heart of the chapter. We have seen that flexibility can be lost. It can also be built, and the central mechanism for building it is one of the most important adaptations in all of exercise physiology.

Recall from Chapter 4 that mitochondria are the small structures inside your cells where fat and carbohydrate are burned aerobically, meaning with oxygen, to produce usable energy. They are the aerobic furnace. Here is the key fact for this chapter: the number and total capacity of your mitochondria are not fixed. Repeated aerobic training causes your muscle cells to build more of them and to make each one more capable. This process is called mitochondrial biogenesis, from "bio" meaning life and "genesis" meaning creation: the creation of new mitochondria.

We have known this for a surprisingly long time. In 1967 John Holloszy published a paper in the Journal of Biological Chemistry that founded the entire field of exercise biochemistry. He trained rats on treadmills and then measured their muscle mitochondria. Endurance training roughly doubled the capacity of the muscle to oxidize fuel and increased the activity of the respiratory enzymes, the protein machines inside mitochondria that carry out aerobic energy production, by about two-fold. Holloszy had shown, for the first time, that exercise causes muscle to manufacture new mitochondrial protein. The engine was building itself more furnaces.

How the signal works

How does a muscle "know" to build mitochondria? Each bout of aerobic exercise stresses the muscle cell in specific ways: energy charge drops as fuel is spent, calcium sweeps in and out with every contraction, and the balance of certain molecules shifts. These disturbances are detected by a set of internal sensors. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials identified the key signaling molecules involved, including a fuel-gauge enzyme called adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (abbreviated AMPK), a stress-signaling protein called p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (abbreviated p38MAPK), calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (abbreviated CaMK), and others.

These sensors all converge on one master coordinator. Its name is unwieldy, so we will spell it once and then use the short form: peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator one-alpha, abbreviated PGC-1 alpha. Lira, Benton, and Yan (2010), reviewing the topic in the American Journal of Physiology: Endocrinology and Metabolism, describe PGC-1 alpha as the central regulator of the muscle's response to endurance training. When activated repeatedly, it switches on the genes needed to build new mitochondria, to grow new blood vessels feeding the muscle, and to place more glucose transporters at the cell surface. The 2025 meta-analysis found a large pooled effect of endurance exercise on PGC-1 alpha expression (reported as a standardized effect size, Hedges' g, of 1.17), with both interval training and continuous training producing large effects. In plain terms: aerobic exercise reliably turns on the master switch for building mitochondria, and it does so whether you train in intervals or at a steady pace.

AMPK: sensing that the tank is running low

Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase deserves a closer look because it illustrates just how elegantly this sensing works. AMPK is activated when the ratio inside the cell of adenosine monophosphate (AMP) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the molecule that stores and transports usable chemical energy, rises. That ratio rises precisely when a muscle fiber is contracting repeatedly and spending ATP faster than it can instantly replace it. Rising AMP binds directly to AMPK, changing its shape, and also makes it a better target for an upstream enzyme called liver kinase B1 (abbreviated LKB1), which attaches a phosphate group to AMPK and switches it into its active form. In other words, AMPK functions as a literal fuel gauge built out of molecules: the more depleted the muscle fiber's immediate energy reserve becomes during a bout of exercise, the more strongly AMPK is activated. Once active, AMPK does two things relevant to this chapter. Within the same exercise session, it reaches for fast fixes, increasing glucose uptake into the muscle and stimulating fat oxidation to help meet the immediate shortfall. Across repeated sessions, it also adds a phosphate group directly to PGC-1 alpha, which is one more converging signal nudging the mitochondrial-biogenesis program toward being switched on.

Calcium: sensing that the muscle is contracting

Calcium provides a second, largely independent line of evidence that the muscle has been doing work. Every single contraction requires calcium ions to flood out of an internal storage compartment inside the muscle fiber called the sarcoplasmic reticulum, briefly raising the calcium concentration around the contractile proteins before pumps return the calcium to storage. Repeated contractions therefore produce repeated calcium transients, and several calcium-sensitive enzymes read this rhythm as a signal. Calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CaMK) and a calcium-sensitive enzyme called calcineurin both respond to sustained or repeated elevations in calcium, and both converge on activating PGC-1 alpha, partly by switching on the transcription factors that turn on the PGC-1 alpha gene itself and partly through direct effects on the PGC-1 alpha protein once it is made. A related enzyme, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase (abbreviated CaMKK), can itself activate AMPK, so the calcium-sensing pathway and the energy-charge-sensing pathway described above are not fully separate wires but a small network that cross-talks and reinforces itself. The practical upshot is that a single muscle contraction is read simultaneously through at least two largely independent sensors, energy charge and calcium flux, both of which land on the same downstream regulator. This redundancy is likely why the biogenesis response to exercise is so reliable across so many different kinds of training: it does not depend on any single fragile signal.

From PGC-1 alpha to an actual new mitochondrion

PGC-1 alpha itself is not an enzyme and it does not touch deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) directly. It functions as a coactivator, a helper protein that binds to and substantially boosts the activity of other proteins, called transcription factors, that do bind DNA and switch genes on. Its two most important partners for this story are nuclear respiratory factor 1 and nuclear respiratory factor 2, abbreviated NRF-1 and NRF-2, which together switch on the genes for many of the protein components of the electron transport chain, the assembly line of protein complexes embedded in the mitochondrial inner membrane that carries out oxidative phosphorylation. PGC-1 alpha, acting through NRF-1, also switches on the gene for a protein called mitochondrial transcription factor A, abbreviated TFAM. TFAM matters because mitochondria carry their own small loop of deoxyribonucleic acid, an evolutionary remnant of their distant origin as free-living bacteria that were absorbed by an ancestral cell, and TFAM is required to copy and express that mitochondrial DNA. PGC-1 alpha therefore coordinates a genuine two-genome construction project: it switches on the nuclear genes that encode most mitochondrial proteins, and, through TFAM, it switches on the replication and expression of the mitochondria's own small genome, so that the nuclear and mitochondrial contributions to a new, functional mitochondrion are produced together and in the correct proportion.

Building new mitochondria is only half of the turnover story. Cells also continuously remove damaged or superfluous mitochondria through a quality-control process called mitophagy, a specialized version of the cell's general recycling system known as autophagy. Healthy mitochondrial adaptation to training reflects a shift in the balance between biogenesis and mitophagy, not simply an unchecked increase in raw numbers: aerobic training tends to raise both the construction of new mitochondrial components and the turnover of older ones, which is thought to keep the resulting mitochondrial population not just larger but of higher average quality.

Matching the training dose to the signal

Because the trigger for this entire cascade is a disturbance to the muscle's energy charge and calcium handling, the size of the training stimulus matters. The 2025 meta-analysis referenced above found that both interval training, which alternates hard efforts with recovery, and continuous moderate training produced large increases in PGC-1 alpha expression, which tells you there is more than one valid route to the same molecular destination. Interval training reaches a given cumulative disturbance faster, by working hard in short bursts, while continuous training reaches a comparable cumulative disturbance more slowly, by working moderately for longer. What the evidence does not support is the idea that gentle, barely perceptible activity produces an equivalent effect: the sensors described above respond to the size of the perturbation, and an effort too easy to meaningfully lower the muscle's energy charge or repeatedly cycle its calcium will only weakly engage them. This is also why the adaptation is progressive and cumulative rather than a single-session event. One bout of exercise transiently raises PGC-1 alpha activity and the expression of some mitochondrial genes for roughly a day or two, and it is the repeated accumulation of many such bouts, each nudging the same machinery again, that gradually raises the resting mitochondrial content of the muscle over weeks. This is exactly the timeline Holloszy's original rats followed, and it is the timeline the training widget below lets you move through.

Why building mitochondria matters beyond the racecourse

Although this chapter frames mitochondrial biogenesis primarily through the lens of endurance performance, the same adaptation is central to long-term metabolic health, and the two threads are not actually separate. Insulin resistance, the impaired insulin signaling that defines type 2 diabetes and contributes to metabolic syndrome, is strongly associated with reduced mitochondrial oxidative capacity in skeletal muscle, which is the same idle-fat problem described earlier in this chapter under chronic inactivity. Aerobic training that increases mitochondrial density is one of the most reliable non-pharmacological interventions for improving insulin sensitivity, precisely because more and more capable mitochondria can keep pace with the fat and glucose arriving at the muscle rather than letting either one accumulate and interfere with insulin signaling. Lira, Benton, and Yan (2010) make this connection explicit, describing the PGC-1 alpha program as improving both muscle oxidative function and whole-body insulin sensitivity through a shared mechanism. This is a useful way to hold the whole chapter together: mitochondrial biogenesis is not two separate benefits, one for endurance and one for metabolic health, arriving by coincidence. It is one adaptation, more capable aerobic machinery, expressed in two domains that were never really separate to begin with.

How More Mitochondria Reshape the Thresholds You Already Know

This is where the chapter ties back to everything you learned before, and where fixed features turn out to be trainable. Holloszy and Coyle (1984), in a landmark synthesis in the Journal of Applied Physiology, laid out the metabolic consequences of having more mitochondria, and they map directly onto concepts from Chapters 4 and 5.

First, consider fuel use at a given pace. With more mitochondria, a muscle can burn more fat, because fat can only be oxidized aerobically and mitochondria are where that happens. More furnaces mean more fat-burning capacity. So at any given submaximal intensity, the trained muscle uses more fat and less carbohydrate. Holloszy and Coyle documented exactly this: slower glycogen use and greater fat oxidation at a given exercise intensity after training.

Second, consider the crossover point. Because the trained muscle can now cover a higher intensity while still relying substantially on fat, the intensity at which carbohydrate takes over, the crossover point, shifts upward. You can work harder before your fuel mix tips toward carbohydrate. The crossover you learned as a fixed feature in Chapter 5 is in fact movable, and endurance training moves it in your favor.

To make this concrete, imagine a runner whose crossover point sits at roughly fifty percent of maximal aerobic capacity before a structured training block begins. After several months of consistent aerobic training, that same runner might find that fat continues to supply most of the fuel up to sixty or sixty-five percent of that same capacity. Practically, this means a training pace or race pace that used to tip the runner into heavy carbohydrate dependence, and the eventual fatigue that follows from it, now falls comfortably inside the fat-dominant zone, leaving glycogen in reserve for later in the effort. The exact numbers vary between individuals and training histories, but the direction of the shift, rightward along the intensity axis, is the reliable and reproducible part.

Third, consider the lactate threshold. Recall that the lactate threshold is the intensity above which lactate accumulates faster in the blood than the body can clear it, marking the ceiling of sustainable hard effort. With more mitochondria, the muscle relies less on the fast, lactate-producing breakdown of carbohydrate at any given intensity, and it clears lactate more effectively. Holloszy and Coyle reported reduced lactate production at a given intensity in trained muscle. The practical result is that the lactate threshold shifts to a higher intensity. You can hold a faster pace before crossing into unsustainable territory.

The reason lactate production falls with more mitochondria is a matter of straightforward respiratory control, and it is worth walking through once because it ties this chapter directly back to the biochemistry of Chapter 4. Mitochondria ramp up ATP production in response to the local concentration of adenosine diphosphate (ADP), the spent form of ATP that accumulates as the cell does work; more ADP present drives oxidative phosphorylation to run faster. A muscle with abundant mitochondria can absorb a given rate of ADP production across a much larger total pool of respiratory machinery, so each individual mitochondrion, and the muscle fiber as a whole, needs only a small rise in ADP concentration to produce a given amount of ATP. A small rise in ADP means only a small push toward the fast, oxygen-independent route through glycolysis, which is the route that generates pyruvate faster than under-supplied mitochondria can absorb it, and therefore the route that generates lactate as a pressure-relief valve. Fewer mitochondria for a given power output means a larger swing in ADP concentration and a larger glycolytic, lactate-producing response; more mitochondria for the same power output means a smaller swing and less lactate. This is also why researchers use enzyme activity as a practical stand-in for mitochondrial content in human muscle-biopsy studies. Citrate synthase, an enzyme that sits at the entry point of the citric acid cycle inside the mitochondrial matrix, and succinate dehydrogenase, an enzyme embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, both scale closely with the total volume of mitochondria present in a tissue sample, so their measured activity in a small biopsy serves researchers as a reliable proxy for how much aerobic machinery a muscle has actually built.

Put these three together and you have the physiological basis for endurance improvement. The person who caught the train had a higher crossover point, a higher lactate threshold, and a greater capacity to use fat, all downstream of having built more and better mitochondria. And there is a bonus: the very same adaptation improves metabolic flexibility in daily life. Lira, Benton, and Yan (2010) note that the PGC-1 alpha program that builds mitochondria also improves insulin sensitivity and restores flexibility. The endurance training that makes you faster also makes your engine better at reading the situation and choosing the right fuel, whether you are exercising or simply living your day.

What Is Actually Fixed, and What Is Not

It is worth being precise about which features of the engine are genuinely fixed and which are not, because this chapter's title promises an adaptable engine, and an honest account has to say plainly where that adaptability runs out. Some features of skeletal muscle are set early in life and change only modestly with training. The proportion of your muscle fibers that are the fast, more glycolytic type versus the slower, more oxidative type is influenced substantially by genetics and shifts only mildly even with years of specific training; nobody converts a muscle that is mostly fast-twitch into one that is mostly slow-twitch through endurance work alone. Likewise, the upper ceiling on how much oxygen your heart and lungs can deliver to muscle, a quantity called maximal oxygen uptake and abbreviated VO2max, which the next chapter treats in detail, responds meaningfully to training but within a genetically bounded range that varies substantially from person to person.

Mitochondrial density is a different kind of feature entirely. It is not fixed in that same sense; it is a continuously adjustable property of the muscle fibers you already have, built up or allowed to decline in direct proportion to how much aerobic signaling the muscle receives, on a timescale of weeks rather than years, and in both directions. Holloszy's original rats demonstrate the upward direction over a matter of weeks; the bed-rest studies discussed earlier in this chapter demonstrate the downward direction over a matter of days. When earlier chapters in this course described the crossover point and the lactate threshold as features of "the engine," that description was accurate for a snapshot in time, but it can be misread as suggesting these are permanent properties of a person in the way eye color is permanent. They are not. They are properties of a specific, current mitochondrial population, and that population is rebuilt continuously by how the muscle is actually used. This is the honest resolution to the apparent tension between earlier chapters and this one: the fixed vocabulary, fuels, pathways, thresholds, described real and useful concepts, but the specific values attached to those concepts for any given individual are outputs of recent training history, not fixed constants of that person's underlying physiology.

Grading the Evidence

It is worth being clear about how strong these claims are, since a hallmark of advanced study is knowing the confidence behind each statement. Mitochondrial biogenesis from endurance training is among the most secure findings in the field. It rests on a chain of evidence running from Holloszy's original 1967 rat experiments through decades of human muscle-biopsy work to the 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials, which quantified a large and consistent effect. The role of PGC-1 alpha is supported by animal and cellular genetic models, as Lira, Benton, and Yan (2010) review, which is a strong form of mechanistic evidence because it manipulates the proposed cause directly.

One caveat is worth naming precisely, because this chapter leans on it heavily: Holloszy's foundational 1967 work was conducted in rats, not humans. Extrapolating from rodent muscle to human muscle is a common and generally reasonable practice in exercise biochemistry, since the core molecular machinery of oxidative phosphorylation is highly conserved across mammals, but rodent training protocols are typically more intense relative to the animal's own capacity than most human training programs are, and rodent muscle fiber composition differs somewhat from human muscle composition. The subsequent decades of human muscle-biopsy research, including the biopsy work underlying Holloszy and Coyle's 1984 synthesis, confirm that the same direction of adaptation, more mitochondria, more fat oxidation, less lactate production, occurs in trained humans as well, which is why the finding as a whole is considered secure. But the original demonstration and the later human confirmation are different kinds of evidence gathered decades apart, and a careful reader keeps track of which specific claim rests on which body of evidence.

Metabolic flexibility as a concept is well established but more contested at its edges. Researchers still debate exactly how best to measure it and how tightly its impairment causes disease versus accompanies it. Galgani and colleagues (2008) explicitly discuss the methodological difficulty of assessment and propose new approaches, which tells you the measurement is not yet settled. The link between inactivity and inflexibility, drawn from bed-rest studies, is compelling but comes with a tradeoff: bed rest is an extreme, artificial model chosen precisely because it isolates inactivity, so it demonstrates a clean causal effect while not perfectly mirroring ordinary sedentary life. Good science and good learning both require holding these distinctions in mind rather than flattening everything into equal certainty.

Key Takeaways

  • Metabolic flexibility is the capacity to switch cleanly between burning fat and burning carbohydrate to match the moment: fat at rest and low intensity, carbohydrate when intensity spikes or a meal arrives.
  • Flexibility matters for performance because burning fat at easy paces spares limited carbohydrate for the moments only carbohydrate can cover, delaying fatigue.
  • The two best-supported causes of impaired flexibility in healthy people are chronic physical inactivity and consistent energy oversupply; genuine metabolic or thyroid disorders warrant professional evaluation, not self-diagnosis.
  • Correct the myths: lactate is a usable fuel, not the cause of next-day soreness (that is mechanical damage), and "starvation mode" is a modest, real efficiency adjustment, not a lock that halts fat loss.
  • Mitochondrial biogenesis, the creation of new mitochondria driven by repeated aerobic training through the master regulator PGC-1 alpha, is a robust and well-documented adaptation.
  • Two independent sensors, AMPK reading the cell's falling energy charge and calcium reading repeated muscle contraction, both converge on activating PGC-1 alpha, which is why a training stimulus has to genuinely disturb the muscle to produce a strong adaptation.
  • Building more mitochondria shifts the crossover point higher, raises the lactate threshold, and increases fat-oxidation capacity, proving that the thresholds of Chapter 5 and the aerobic furnace of Chapter 4 are trainable, not fixed.
  • Muscle fiber type proportion and the outer ceiling of maximal oxygen uptake are comparatively fixed by genetics, while mitochondrial density is continuously rebuilt within weeks in either direction, which is the real distinction between a fixed engine and an adaptable one.
Looking Ahead

You now understand the engine as something that responds to how you use it. In the next chapter we turn from the muscle cell outward to the whole-body delivery system: the heart, lungs, and blood vessels that carry oxygen to those newly built mitochondria. We will see how maximal oxygen uptake sets the outer ceiling of aerobic power, and how it too, like the mitochondria within, can be reshaped by training.
