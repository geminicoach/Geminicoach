Chapter 3: Correct the Locker-Room Explanation

An athlete's explanation of soreness is built on three linked myths. Untangle them, and know when a detail changes the conversation.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 20 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Devon, 26, recreational CrossFit competitor
After every hard session, Devon takes an ice bath because "it flushes the lactic acid out of my legs so I'm not sore tomorrow." During today's workout, Devon felt a strong burning sensation in the final set and says, "that's the lactic acid building up, that's what's going to make me sore tomorrow too." In passing, Devon also mentions that after an unusually brutal session last month, the soreness lasted over two weeks and their urine looked noticeably dark for a day or two, which they assumed was "just normal, from being so sore."

Your Tasks

  1. Correct Devon's belief that muscles produce "lactic acid" and that lactate is something to be "flushed out." Explain what lactate actually is and does.
  2. Explain what actually causes the burning sensation Devon felt in the final set, naming the real mechanism.
  3. Explain what actually causes the soreness felt a day or two after a hard session, and why the timeline rules out lactate as the cause.
  4. Address the dark urine and unusually prolonged soreness from last month. Decide whether this is a detail you can wave off or one that changes your response, and explain why.

Your Response

Where education ends
You are demonstrating mechanistic understanding of glycolysis and lactate metabolism, not diagnosing Devon. A responsible response explains the physiology correctly, and recognizes that unusually severe or prolonged soreness together with dark urine is a red flag pattern that warrants prompt evaluation by a qualified clinician, not a training-log explanation.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Corrects "lactate equals lactic acid" and explains lactate is a fuel used via the lactate shuttle, not a waste product.
  • Explains the actual mechanism of the burn: hydrogen ion accumulation from high ATP turnover during glycolysis, not lactate itself.
  • Explains the DOMS mechanism: eccentric-contraction microdamage and an inflammatory repair process appearing one to three days later, unrelated to lactate.
  • Notes the timeline mismatch: lactate clears within thirty to sixty minutes, long before DOMS appears.
  • Gives a scoped, practical response about the ice-bath belief and correctly flags the dark-urine detail as warranting professional evaluation.
