Chapter 4 : The Slow Furnace

Huge capacity, low power. Mitochondria, oxygen as the final electron acceptor, and how fat gets unlocked.

The Five Stages of Aerobic ATP Production

1. Fuel entersPyruvate (from glycolysis) crosses into the matrix via pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH); fatty acids cross via the carnitine shuttle (CPT1 / CPT2).
2. Acetyl-CoABoth fuels are converted into the same two-carbon fragment, acetyl coenzyme A, the common currency the furnace accepts.
3. Citric acid cycleAlso called the TCA or Krebs cycle. Strips high-energy electrons onto NADH and FADH2, releases CO2, makes one GTP per turn.
4. Electron transport chainFour complexes pump protons across the inner membrane; oxygen is the final electron acceptor, only at Complex IV, forming water.
5. ATP synthaseProtons flow back through this rotary motor, spinning it to force ADP and phosphate together into ATP (chemiosmosis).

Key Structures

  • Mitochondrion : the organelle where aerobic metabolism happens.
  • Cristae : folds of the inner membrane; pack in surface area.
  • Matrix : fluid interior; site of the TCA cycle and beta-oxidation.
  • ATP synthase : rotary enzyme; sometimes called Complex V.

Why the Numbers Differ

  • Anaerobic glycolysis nets about 2 ATP per glucose.
  • Aerobic metabolism nets roughly 30 to 32 ATP per glucose, not the old textbook 36 to 38.
  • NADH yields about 2.5 ATP; FADH2 yields about 1.5 ATP (enters the chain lower down).
  • Aerobic is about 15 times more efficient, but far slower to deliver ATP per second.

Burning Fat: Beta-Oxidation

  • Fat is stored as triglycerides; fatty acid chains are the energy-rich part.
  • Beta-oxidation snips a fatty acid chain two carbons at a time into acetyl-CoA.
  • The carnitine shuttle (CPT1, CPT2) is the bottleneck that gets long-chain fatty acids into the matrix; it cannot ramp up instantly.
  • Only the aerobic system can burn fat. Anaerobic systems cannot touch it at all.

Power Versus Capacity

  • Aerobic system: huge capacity (fat and carbohydrate, hours of fuel), low maximum rate.
  • Phosphagen and glycolytic systems: tiny capacity, very high maximum rate.
  • A sprint needs ATP faster per second than the aerobic system, or fat's slow entry route, can ever supply, no matter how many mitochondria a person has.
