Part A · The Ceiling, Not the Target

The chapter builds the dose-response idea into one practical number, the tolerable upper intake level (UL).

  1. Explain what a UL actually is, and why it is set below the dose where harm was observed rather than at it. Name the safety margin that creates that gap.
  2. A learner reads that a UL is a ceiling and treats the gap between their current intake and that ceiling as "headroom to fill." Explain, using the dose-response curve, why that is the exact wrong reading.
  3. The chapter says there are only three numbers worth knowing for any nutrient you supplement. Name them and explain what each one tells you about where to land.

Part D · The Antioxidant Paradox

Whole-food antioxidants track with better health, yet high-dose isolates failed in large trials. Reason through why.

  1. Eating carotenoid-rich vegetables tracks with lower lung cancer risk, yet a beta-carotene pill raised lung cancer risk in smokers. List two ways a nutrient in whole food differs from the same nutrient as a high-dose isolate, and explain which difference could flip a benefit into a harm.
  2. Explain hormesis in the training context: why can a large dose of vitamin C and E around workouts quietly work against the adaptation you are training for?

Part E · Interactions and the Geometry of Risk

The most underappreciated risk surface is what happens when compounds talk to each other.

  1. Distinguish a pharmacokinetic interaction from a pharmacodynamic one. Give one worked example of each from the chapter (for instance, St. John's wort and warfarin plus vitamin E) and name which mechanism each illustrates.
  2. The medications most often implicated (warfarin, insulin, digoxin) share a property that makes a small interaction decisive. Name that property and explain why it connects back to the optimal-window idea.
  3. Use the combinations formula for risk surface. A stack has 4 products; going from 4 to 5 items adds how many new possible pairings? From 8 to 9? Show the arithmetic and explain why "just one more" is cheaper in feeling than in fact.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds supplement literacy, not a license to diagnose, prescribe, or manage a medication, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how dose, accumulation, competition, and interactions work, and treat anything clinical (a medication in the picture, a high-risk situation such as pregnancy or liver or kidney disease, or an alarm sign such as unusual bleeding) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician or pharmacist. For interactions and higher-risk groups, professional input is the control measure, not a formality. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician or pharmacist works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose or self-prescribe.
