Part C · Vitamin D in Context

  1. The chapter says vitamin D fills a genuine, widespread gap but is oversold for the wrong outcomes. Name the groups the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline supports for empiric supplementation, and explain why a healthy 35-year-old under 75 with regular sun sits in a different position.
  2. Explain why the chapter recommends against routine 25(OH)D testing for most healthy adults, and name one situation where the test genuinely earns its cost.
  3. A defensible repletion dose sits around 1000 to 2000 IU per day. Explain why "more is better" is exactly backwards for vitamin D, using the fact that it is fat-soluble.

Part D · The Performance Pair: Creatine and Caffeine

Both earn a place by shifting a measurable outcome rather than filling a gap. Answer in full sentences.

  1. In plain terms, explain how creatine lets someone build more muscle without building muscle directly. Name the fuel system it tops up.
  2. Loading creatine is described as faster, not better. Explain what that means for a person deciding whether to load.
  3. Caffeine does not make the work lighter. Explain, using adenosine, what it actually changes, and why keeping it to the first half of the day matters.
  4. Explain the tolerance catch: why the heavy daily user gets less from a given dose than an occasional user.

Part E · Read the Label, Read the Claim

Use the "associated with is not fixes" and "source and dose" logic from the chapter.

  1. A magnesium label lists a long roster of diseases tied to low magnesium and implies the pill prevents them. Explain, in a sentence or two, why that association list is a good reason to eat more magnesium-rich food but a much weaker reason to expect the supplement to prevent those diseases.
  2. An omega-3 label brags about its omega-3 content, but the source is flax. Explain what the buyer is actually mostly getting, and what source you would look for instead if the goal is EPA and DHA.
  3. A protein powder is marketed as a muscle-builder. Using the roughly 1.6 grams per kilogram plateau, reframe what the powder honestly does and when the next scoop is just expensive food.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds supplement literacy, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the evidence works and where a gap is plausible, and treat anything that looks clinical (symptoms of toxicity, an existing kidney, liver, or metabolic condition, pregnancy, medication interactions, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose or self-prescribe.
