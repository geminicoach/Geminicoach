Chapter 4 : Test, Don't Guess

Iron, B12, and vitamin D are where measuring beats guessing, because both deficiency and excess carry real costs.

What Makes a Nutrient "Measure First"

1. Asymmetry of consequenceBeing low hurts you and being high also hurts you, so guessing can be wrong in a damaging direction.
2. Identifiable risk groupsThe likely-deficient are nameable in advance from diet, life stage, and physiology, not random.
3. A real biomarkerA blood test tracks status well enough to turn the question into a number.
  • All three stacked = testing is smart. Creatine lacks the first (harmless excess), so it is "just try it," not "test first."

Iron: The Textbook Case

  • Both ends bite : deficiency causes fatigue; excess has no easy exit and free iron drives tissue-damaging reactive oxygen species.
  • Deficiency before anemia : stores (ferritin) drain first, so a normal CBC can read fine while you run on empty.
  • Marker : ferritin first-line, but it rises with inflammation and hard training, so pair with transferrin saturation and retest in context.
  • Hidden risk : undiagnosed hemochromatosis carriers must never load blind.

B12: Predict It From Diet and Age

  • Stakes : deficiency can cause irreversible nerve damage that need not wait for anemia.
  • Risk by intake : plant-based and even plant-predominant diets routinely fall short; the source is essentially absent from plants.
  • Risk by absorption : older adults, low stomach acid, long-term acid suppressants, metformin.
  • Marker : total serum B12 misleads; holo-TC and MMA give the honest read.

Vitamin D: Biomarker Settled, Threshold Not

  • Marker : 25(OH)D is agreed, but its outcome-predictive value in replete people is weak.
  • Guidance : rigorous guidance advises against routine general-population screening.
  • Empiric groups : defined groups (per the Endocrine Society guideline) may supplement a modest dose without testing.
  • Why : if the result would not change the sensible maintenance move, the test is not earning its cost.

Reference Range vs Decision Limit

  • Reference range : a statistical crowd (middle 95%), answers "is this common," not "is this good for you."
  • Decision limit : set where a bad outcome or diagnosis climbs; anchored to consequences.
  • Most micronutrient "ranges" are descriptive, and are method- and population-specific, so a boundary-hugging single value is weak evidence.
  • Be suspicious of any "optimal" target that arrives attached to a product for sale.

Four Ways Guessing Goes Wrong

  • Overshooting : surplus accumulates (stored nutrients, iron, fat-soluble vitamins).
  • Toxicity : sharp dose-dependent harm at the top (acute iron poisoning; vitamin D via high calcium).
  • Masking : folate hides B12 anemia while nerve damage continues.
  • Opportunity cost : a bottle buys the feeling of acting and delays finding the real cause.

The Test-First Decision Flow

  • 1. Am I in a nameable risk group? If a clean no, usually stop; the "just in case" impulse has nothing to stand on.
  • 2. Would a result change what I do? Iron: almost always yes. B12 (plant-based): diet already decided, test confirms repletion. Vitamin D (healthy adult): often no.
  • 3. Am I getting the right test, not the cheapest? The easy number can be the falsely reassuring one.
  • 4. Act, then re-measure in context. Retest away from the last dose and acute inflammation; refill the tank and ease off. Testing is a tool, not a virtue.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of when measuring beats guessing, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe. Ordering and interpreting lab tests and setting a dose from a result belong to a qualified clinician; a learner does not diagnose from labs. A flagged or abnormal result, neurological signs, symptoms of toxicity, an existing medical condition, or pregnancy all warrant evaluation. Work within your role, whatever it is.
