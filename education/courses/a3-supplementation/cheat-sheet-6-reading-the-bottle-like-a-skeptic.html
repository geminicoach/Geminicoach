Chapter 6 : Reading the Bottle Like a Skeptic

The label is a claim, not a measurement. Four questions, all of which must pass, decide whether a bottle can work.

The Four Questions (all must pass)

1. Right ingredient?The specific molecule with credible evidence, not a cousin that shares the name.
2. Effective dose?The amount that moved an outcome in human trials, not a token sprinkle.
3. Usable form?A bioavailable salt, ester, or preparation that actually gets absorbed.
4. Independent check?A real third-party lab verified it, not the marketing department.
  • Conjunctive, not a scorecard. Three of four still fails. A single failure is enough to put the bottle back.

Supplement Facts Panel

  • Serving size : read it first; it scales every number. Three capsules per serving means "500 mg" is across all three.
  • %DV : quantity vs an average day, not proof of benefit. A high %DV often just gets excreted.
  • Blank %DV / dagger : normal for botanicals; hands the dose question back to you.
  • Other Ingredients : fillers and carriers; scan for allergens and for filler dwarfing the actives.

Effective Dose vs Label Dose

  • A famous ingredient at a trace amount is unstudied, not "a little bit of the effect."
  • Fairy dusting : a dose too small to act, added so the name can appear on the label.
  • Dose math: per-serving amount x servings per day, compared to the effective range you looked up.
  • Below the floor, it is decoration. Far above the ceiling is its own problem.

Proprietary Blends

  • Several ingredients under one total weight; individual amounts hidden. Legal under DSHEA.
  • Descending-order tell : listed by mass, so the first is largest and the last is smallest.
  • The named, impressive ingredient is often listed last, behind cheap bulk.
  • The word "blend," "matrix," or "complex" is itself a risk flag. Treat a hidden dose as absent.

Third-Party Seals

  • Real marks (NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport, USP Verified) verify identity, purity, and dose accuracy.
  • No seal verifies that the product works for your goal. Honesty, not efficacy.
  • Decoration: "lab tested," a "GMP" graphic, "doctor formulated" with no named body or registry. Treat as artwork.
  • For high-risk categories and any tested athlete, a real seal is mandatory.

Contamination & "Natural"

  • "Natural" carries no safety guarantee; a "natural" product can still be spiked with a real drug.
  • Risk concentrates in weight-loss, "energy," sexual-enhancement, and muscle-building products.
  • A fast, strong, drug-like effect from a botanical is a reason for more suspicion, not less.
  • Tested athletes: strict liability, so contamination is a career-level risk. Demand a sport seal.

Claims & the Disclaimer

  • Structure-function : "supports a healthy immune system." Vague and legal, promises nothing measurable.
  • Disease claim : "prevents colds and flu." Prohibited on a supplement; means walk away.
  • The FDA disclaimer is honest text. Its absence, where a claim is made, is the red flag.

Name the Marketing Move

  • "Supports" weasel word : an association, not an improvement.
  • Proxy outcome : "raises antioxidant levels" swapped in for the goal you wanted.
  • Borrowed authority : "clinically studied" describes the ingredient, not this bottle or its dose.
  • Before-and-after : an uncontrolled, selected anecdote. Zero evidence.

The Thirty-Second Shelf Protocol

  • One: read the front claim and translate it. Real measurable benefit, or a "supports" haze over a proxy?
  • Two: find the Supplement Facts and scan for "blend / matrix / complex." Hidden amounts mean default to skip.
  • Three: if amounts are disclosed, do the dose math against the effective range you looked up.
  • Four: check serving size for inflation, then look for a real, verifiable seal (honesty, not efficacy). Fail any one and put it down without guilt.
Where education ends   This builds label literacy, not a license to diagnose, treat, or clear anyone for use. A strong adverse reaction, a suspected undeclared drug, a medication interaction, or a medical condition behind the question warrants a qualified clinician. Whether a person needs a supplement at all is a separate decision. Work within your role, whatever it is.
