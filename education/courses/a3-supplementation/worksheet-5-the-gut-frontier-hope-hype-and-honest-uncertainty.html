Part A · Fiber Usually Wins

Fiber is the best-supported product on the shelf, and the reason is both the evidence and the gap. Answer in full sentences.

  1. The chapter calls fiber "the headline act." Name the kind of evidence that puts it there (the scale of the Reynolds 2019 data) and one concrete outcome that intake was associated with.
  2. Describe the fiber gap in numbers: roughly what do most people eat per day, what is the target range, and why does that gap make fiber "the textbook setup for a real supplement decision"?
  3. Here is the twist the chapter insists on. The same evidence that justifies caring about fiber also argues for closing the gap with food rather than a powder. Explain why food is the better fix for most people.

Part B · "Prebiotic" and the Tolerance Ceiling

Fiber is not just bulk. It is the raw material for a metabolic supply chain, and it comes with a speed limit.

  1. Define a prebiotic mechanically. What does fermentation of these fibers produce, and why does butyrate matter to the cells lining the colon?
  2. Someone ramps their fiber from 12 grams to 28 grams in two days and feels wrecked by bloating, then concludes "fiber does not agree with me." Explain the actual mechanism, and give the applied rule that fixes it.
  3. Psyllium is one of the few gut-aisle supplements that clears the evidence bar. Name two reproducible physiological effects it has, and state the one condition under which a psyllium powder genuinely earns its place over food.

Part C · Probiotics Are Not One Thing

This is the section where precision is the whole point. Draw the line the chapter draws.

  1. The chapter says "probiotic" is about as informative as the word "drug." Using the three levels of identification (genus, species, strain), explain at what level probiotic effects actually live and why a benefit for one strain tells you almost nothing about another.
  2. A label reads "clinically studied probiotic, 10 strains, 50 billion CFU." Give two reasons this claim tells you far less than it appears to, and name the one piece of information that would actually let you check whether it helps with a specific symptom.
  3. The strain-specificity rule cuts both ways. Name one condition where specific strains genuinely earn their keep, and explain what the "colonization problem" (Zmora et al., 2018) reveals about why a daily general probiotic "to keep your gut balanced" is a weak proposition.

Part E · Honest Uncertainty and the Four-Step Method

The real product of this chapter is a way of reasoning when the evidence is genuinely mixed. Put it to work.

  1. A consumer microbiome test promises to "map your gut and optimize it." Give two reasons the chapter treats the report as "a claim to verify, not an answer," and name the single question worth asking before spending on one.
  2. Write out the four-step reasoning the chapter uses on any mixed-evidence category, one line each (best evidence, level the effect lives at, whether you have the condition, and the default move).
  3. A friend with no diagnosis feels bloated and asks whether to buy a 50-billion-CFU probiotic, an enzyme blend, or a fiber tub. Run the four steps out loud: where does each product land, and what would you suggest they change first before buying anything?
  4. The chapter ends by refusing to moralize digestion. Explain in your own words why treating the gut as "plumbing rather than virtue" helps you reason more clearly.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds supplement literacy, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the evidence works and where a product does or does not earn its place, but interpreting symptoms, ordering tests, and setting a treatment belong to a qualified clinician. Treat anything that looks clinical (a persistent change in digestion, bleeding, unexplained weight loss, a diagnosed condition, pregnancy, or medication interactions) as warranting evaluation, not a heavier supplement stack. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose or self-prescribe.
