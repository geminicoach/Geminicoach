Chapter 3 : Powders, Pumps, and What the Evidence Shows

Three molecules, two kinds of question: one fills a gap, two produce an effect. Strong evidence and absurd marketing share the same label.

Two Kinds of Thing

Protein powderFood wearing a label. Fills a dietary gap. Ask: do I have a protein shortfall, and is powder the practical way to close it?
CreatineErgogenic compound, not food. Produces an effect. Ask: does the effect matter to me, at a dose I tolerate, for a cost I accept?
CaffeineErgogenic compound, not food. A dose with a response curve, not a personality or ritual.

Protein: The Numbers

  • Active range: about 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/day (ISSN).
  • Training benefit of added protein plateaus near 1.62 g/kg/day; above it, extra scoops are expensive redundancy.
  • Total daily intake beats timing or source. The "anabolic window" is a barn door, not a keyhole.
  • Powder earns its place only when a real gap exists and it is the practical way to close it.

Formats, Not a Hierarchy

  • Whey : fast, sharp rise in amino acids; usually cheapest per gram of complete protein.
  • Casein : slow-release; small real edge for pre-sleep (30 to 40 g).
  • Plant blend : pea + rice complement each other; complete when dosed and blended.
  • Animal-over-plant edge for synthesis is modest and context-dependent, not a moral ranking.

Creatine: What It Does

  • Strongest evidence in the field. Dose: 3 to 5 g/day plain monohydrate; no fancy form beats it.
  • Enlarges the phosphocreatine buffer: more ATP regeneration in short, hard efforts.
  • Real gains in strength, power, repeated-effort capacity, and lean mass; useful for aging strength too.
  • The early water gain is inside muscle cells, a real benefit, not bloating.

Creatine: The Myths

  • Does not damage healthy kidneys (safe up to 30 g/day studied for years).
  • Is not a steroid; it is an amino-acid derivative you already eat.
  • Does not reliably cause cramps; the hair-loss claim is unreplicated.
  • Same finding, two spins: "insane pumps" and "dangerous bloating" describe one real effect.

Caffeine: A Dose

  • Performance range: 3 to 6 mg/kg, about an hour before effort.
  • Above 6 mg/kg you get jitters, not more performance. More is just more side effects.
  • Moderate doses (about 32 to 300 mg) aid vigilance, reaction time, and sleep-loss recovery.
  • Half-life about 5 hours: an afternoon dose still shadows bedtime. Tolerance makes the daily habit buy back stolen alertness.

The Second Tier: Real but Narrow

  • Beta-alanine : buffers acidity; small real benefit only for efforts about 30 s to 10 min, at 3 to 6 g/day loaded over weeks. Tingle is harmless, not the effect.
  • Citrulline : the "pump" ingredient; evidence thin and inconsistent (about 8 g studied). Visible pump is mostly training itself.
  • Pre-workout : a delivery vehicle. Felt kick is mostly caffeine; proprietary blends hide the doses. Judge it by its parts.

The Useless Tier: Fails by Design

  • BCAAs : only 3 of 9 essential amino acids; cannot build alone. Complete protein already supplies them.
  • Glutamine : fills a pool a well-fed person never empties; no gym payoff.
  • Test boosters : do not reliably raise testosterone, and small in-range changes would not build muscle anyway.
  • Fat burners : mild stimulants; cannot replace an energy deficit.

The Screen You Can Run Before Any Study

  • Ask what the product is mechanically supposed to do, then whether that mechanism survives contact with how the body works.
  • If you already eat enough complete protein, BCAAs and glutamine are redundant by definition. If you are not in an energy deficit, a fat burner has nothing to burn.
  • Strong evidence and absurd marketing live on the same label, for the same compound, at the same time. Effectiveness and false claims are not opposites.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of what these compounds do and what the evidence earns, not a license to diagnose, prescribe, or treat. A diagnosed condition, medication interactions, chest pain, a racing heart, severe anxiety, or other alarm signs are reasons to consult a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
