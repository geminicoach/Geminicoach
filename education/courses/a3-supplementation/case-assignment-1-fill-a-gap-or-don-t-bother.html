Chapter 1: Interrogate a Real Cabinet

Take the four-gate pathway and run it on a real shelf of "can't hurt," in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Hank, 38
Consider Hank, who works an indoor desk job at a high latitude and has built up a shelf of nine supplements over a few years. Each one arrived the same way: a podcast, a coworker, a headline. He takes a multivitamin, a separate "immune support" blend, vitamin D at a high daily dose, magnesium "for sleep and stress," a greens powder, an omega-3, a standalone B-complex "for energy," zinc lozenges most winter days, and turmeric capsules "for inflammation." He eats a mixed diet but no oily fish. He feels mostly fine. He has also taken a proton-pump inhibitor for reflux for the past three years, and has never had any nutrient level checked. Asked why he takes each product, his answer is almost always the same: "it can't hurt." He wonders: "Is any of this doing anything, and am I missing something?"

Your Tasks

  1. Run Hank's cabinet through the chapter's interrogation and four gates. Sort the nine items into piles, and show which ones fail at the very first question, name the specific shortfall, and why.
  2. Address "it can't hurt" directly. Point to at least one place where overlapping products or an upper limit makes that phrase a reasoning failure rather than a safe default.
  3. Identify the items that are genuinely plausible but unconfirmed, and say what verdict the chapter gives for those (food-first and "test and target") instead of guessing in either direction.
  4. Name the most defensible gap in Hank's whole situation, including the one he does not currently address, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Your Response

Where education ends
You are demonstrating reasoning and plain-language communication, not diagnosing or prescribing. A supplement is not a treatment. A responsible response runs the pathway, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose or recommend a specific dose to correct a suspected deficiency. A suspected deficiency and a drug-nutrient interaction belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Runs the gap principle and interrogation, showing most items fail at "name the specific shortfall."
  • Identifies manufactured-need and optimization items, and names the overlapping-dose or upper-limit risk that makes "it can't hurt" wrong.
  • Applies food-first and "test and target" to the genuinely plausible items (vitamin D, omega-3) rather than guessing.
  • Identifies the drug-induced gap (long-term proton-pump-inhibitor use lowering nutrient absorption) as the strongest, most defensible issue.
  • States that a suspected deficiency or drug-nutrient interaction warrants a clinician, and does not diagnose or prescribe.
