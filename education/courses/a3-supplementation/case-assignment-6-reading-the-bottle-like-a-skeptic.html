Chapter 6: Take a Real Bottle Apart

Run the four questions on a real-looking label and explain, in plain language, whether it earns a place in the cart.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Lea, 34
Consider Lea, who wants steadier energy and a little more focus for long workdays and keeps getting served the same capsule on her feed, promoted by people she follows. The front reads "CLARITY PLUS, Supports Energy, Focus & Immune Defense," with "NOW WITH CLINICALLY STUDIED ASHWAGANDHA" and a row of star icons reading "Lab Tested, GMP, Doctor Formulated." She turns it over. The Supplement Facts list a single line, "Focus & Immune Matrix, 900 mg," containing, in order, vitamin C, taurine, caffeine anhydrous, and ashwagandha root extract, at a serving size of two capsules. It costs twenty-nine dollars, and she is about to buy a three-month supply. She asks a friend who is taking this course: "This looks legit and it is not even expensive. Is there any reason not to get it?"

Your Tasks

  1. Run the four questions on this label and state, question by question, whether it passes or fails and how you know.
  2. Explain the proprietary-blend problem here: what the "Focus & Immune Matrix, 900 mg" line hides, what the descending order tells you about the ashwagandha the product is named after, and why a hidden dose should be treated as an absent dose.
  3. Evaluate the marks and the claims: are "Lab Tested, GMP, Doctor Formulated" third-party verification or decoration, how would you sort "Supports Energy, Focus & Immune Defense" as a claim, and what does the two-capsule serving size do to the 900 number?
  4. Give Lea a plain-language recommendation she can act on, and state clearly where general education ends and professional evaluation begins.

Your Response

0 words
Where education ends
You are demonstrating label literacy and plain-language communication, not diagnosing or clearing anyone for use. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Whether a person needs a supplement at all, and any medical condition, adverse reaction, or medication interaction behind the question, belongs to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Runs the four questions and correctly concludes the product fails at least one of them.
  • Explains that the proprietary blend hides every per-ingredient amount, and that descending order puts the named ashwagandha last and therefore smallest, so a hidden dose is treated as absent.
  • Distinguishes the decorative marks from real third-party verification, sorts the front claim as structure-function rather than disease, and notes that the two-capsule serving inflates the 900 number.
  • Gives a practical, plain-language, in-scope recommendation (for example, a single-ingredient product with printed doses and a verifiable seal).
  • States where general education ends, flags what warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose.
