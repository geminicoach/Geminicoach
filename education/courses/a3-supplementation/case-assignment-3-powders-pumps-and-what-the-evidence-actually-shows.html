Chapter 3: Read the Shelf Like a Label

Take the cold-evidence logic of the chapter and apply it to a real cabinet: sort the categories, name the mechanisms, and stay inside general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Ravi, 38
Consider Ravi, who is not a competitive athlete but wants to hold onto his strength as he ages and feel less tired during the day. He lifts twice a week and eats reasonably well, landing around 130 grams of protein a day at roughly 80 kilograms. His shelf has grown over the past year: a tub of whey, a pre-workout he scoops most mornings for the energy even on days he does not train, a jar of BCAAs he sips during workouts, glutamine "for recovery," a test booster he started after a podcast ad, and a fat burner he takes when he feels soft. His progress has flattened, his supplement bill is climbing, and lately he is sleeping badly. He is convinced the answer is a better stack. He asks: "What should I add? And is any of this actually doing something?"

Your Tasks

  1. Sort Ravi's shelf into the chapter's categories. Which items answer a gap question, which answer an effect question, and is his protein filling a real shortfall or adding redundant grams? Show your reasoning with his numbers.
  2. For each product that is redundant or ineffective for him, name the specific mechanistic reason it fails. The reasons should not all be the same.
  3. Ravi scoops a pre-workout most mornings and now sleeps poorly. Explain the caffeine half-life and tolerance story, and say what you would flag about that habit.
  4. Name the one compound with the strongest evidence for his actual goal that is missing from his shelf, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing, prescribing, or managing anyone's health. A responsible response explains the mechanism and the evidence, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation (for example, persistent poor sleep, a diagnosed condition, medication interactions, or stimulant symptoms like chest tightness or a racing heart), and does not diagnose or prescribe. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Sorts the shelf into gap-filler versus effect-producer and recognizes that Ravi's protein is already adequate, so the whey is convenience near or past the 1.62 g/kg/day plateau, not a gap being filled.
  • Gives the correct distinct mechanistic reason each redundant product fails: BCAAs supply only 3 of 9 essential amino acids and his complete protein already covers them; glutamine fills a pool a well-fed person never empties; the test booster targets normal hormones with ingredients that do not reliably raise testosterone; the fat burner is a mild stimulant that cannot replace an energy deficit.
  • Explains caffeine's roughly five-hour half-life and tolerance, and links the all-day pre-workout habit to his poor sleep and the loop it creates.
  • Identifies creatine (3 to 5 g/day monohydrate) as the missing strong-evidence compound for preserving strength and lean mass with age.
  • Frames the whole analysis as general education, identifies what warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose or prescribe.
