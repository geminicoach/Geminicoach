Chapter 5: Un-Flatten the Gut Aisle

Take a real 200-dollar gut plan apart with the four-step reasoning, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Tom, 34
Consider Tom, who works long hours at a desk and eats most meals on the go: coffee and a pastry in the morning, a turkey sandwich on white bread at lunch, takeout for dinner, and chips when he is stressed. He feels bloated most afternoons and generally "sluggish," and he is convinced his gut is "out of balance." After an evening of research he lands on a plan: a mail-in microbiome test (90 dollars), a 50-billion-CFU, 12-strain probiotic that the test company recommends (45 dollars a month), and a digestive enzyme blend to take with meals (30 dollars a month) so he can "digest better." He has not changed a single thing about what he eats. He asks: "Is this a smart spend before I commit?"

Your Tasks

  1. Run each of the three purchases through the four-step reasoning from the chapter (how strong is the best evidence, at what level does any real effect live, does Tom actually have the condition it targets, and what is the default move).
  2. Explain why the 12-strain, 50-billion-CFU number and the enzyme blend are weak buys for Tom specifically, and why the microbiome test is "precision theater" rather than precision medicine in his case.
  3. Name the highest-leverage change that is not on Tom's shopping list at all, say why the evidence points there, and describe how you would have him make it without getting chased off by bloating in the first week.
  4. State clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins here, and do it without moralizing Tom's diet or his gut.

Your Response

0 words
Where education ends
You are demonstrating evidence reasoning and plain-language communication, not diagnosing, treating, or prescribing. A responsible response weighs the evidence, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose or set a treatment. Persistent, changing, or worrying digestive symptoms are a reason to see a qualified clinician, not a reason to buy a supplement.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Applies the four-step reasoning explicitly to all three purchases on Tom's list.
  • Identifies fiber's far stronger evidence base and Tom's likely fiber gap as the highest-leverage move.
  • Explains that strain count and CFU are mostly theater without a named strain, outcome, and dose, and that enzymes target a deficiency Tom has no reason to think he has.
  • Recommends food-first, ramped gradually with water, with cheap psyllium only as a defensible backup.
  • Refuses to moralize digestion, does not diagnose or prescribe, and flags when a clinician is warranted.
