Part B · Sleep, the Master Regulator

Sleep touches more axes at once than any other single behavior. Reason through each readout.

  1. Explain why the timing and quality of a night matter, not only its total duration. Tie your answer to the slow-wave-linked growth-hormone pulse and to why a long but fragmented night can still underfeed it.
  2. Short sleep lowers leptin and raises ghrelin. State the direction of each arrow and explain, in your own words, why that combination makes late-night hunger a signaling problem rather than a discipline failure.
  3. The chapter says sleep loss degrades insulin sensitivity "without adequate beta-cell compensation." Explain what that clause means and why it matters that the volunteers were healthy and young.
  4. One week of short sleep dropped testosterone by roughly ten to fifteen percent in healthy young men. Use this to explain why a "testosterone support" product bought by a chronically underslept person is fighting the wrong lever.

Part C · Stress: Acute Versus Chronic

Almost every confused claim about cortisol collapses two very different situations into one word. Keep them apart.

  1. Trace the HPA cascade from stressor to cortisol (name CRH, ACTH, cortisol) and explain what the negative-feedback loop does. Why is a healthy stress response defined as much by how it ends as by how it fires?
  2. Explain why the "adrenal fatigue" story does not fit the wiring. What actually happens to the cortisol rhythm under chronic stress, and why does that make a "reset" powder answer a question the biology did not ask?
  3. Describe cross-axis suppression: what does a chronically active stress system do to the reproductive, growth, and thyroid axes, and why is that logic sensible in a real emergency but costly when the threat never resolves?

Part D · Nutrition as Raw Material and Signal

Nutrition does two jobs at once. Keep the raw-material role and the signal role separate.

  1. Every steroid hormone is built on a cholesterol backbone. Explain why this does not mean "eat more cholesterol to boost hormones," and how chronic under-eating (low energy availability) can suppress sex-hormone production even in a careful, disciplined eater.
  2. Macronutrients "speak different molecular languages." Pick two (for example protein and incretins/mTOR, or fat type and PPAR signaling) and explain what each signals, in a sentence or two.
  3. Explain why the "clean versus dirty" framing of food is mechanistically empty, and what three variables the chapter says we should reason with instead.

Part E · Training and the Two Timescales

  1. Distinguish the acute hormonal response to a hard session from the chronic adaptations that matter most. Name one durable adaptation (for example androgen-receptor upregulation or improved insulin sensitivity) and explain why it matters more than the post-workout spike.
  2. Walk through the marketing move slowly: a real study shows training acutely raises testosterone, and a product implies its powder therefore builds muscle. Where exactly does that chain snap, and why?

Part H · Scope of Practice

Knowing the line between general education and clinical care is a core competency, not a formality. The four levers act on the normal, modifiable inputs to a healthy system. Supplements and lifestyle protocols are not treatments, and conditions are diagnosed by clinicians with tests, never by a course like this one. Where education ends
This worksheet builds lifestyle-and-hormone literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the levers work, and treat anything that looks clinical (persistent insomnia or suspected sleep apnea, clinical anxiety or depression, disordered eating or low energy availability, disrupted cycles, unexplained weight change, or stalled progress that persists or worsens) as signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Supplements and lifestyle protocols are not treatments, and conditions are diagnosed by clinicians with tests. Work within your role, whatever it is: refer when a situation leaves ordinary physiology, work within your license if you hold one, and do not self-diagnose.
