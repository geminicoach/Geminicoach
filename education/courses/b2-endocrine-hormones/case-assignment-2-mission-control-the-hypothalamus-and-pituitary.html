Chapter 2: Reason Through a Broken Floor

Take the command chain and use it to explain, in plain language, how an axis reveals where a fault lives, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 20 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Owen, 34
Consider Owen, who is a fellow learner in this course and has just finished the chapter on axes. A friend showed him two numbers from a report: a final hormone that reads low, and the upstream pituitary signal that drives it, which reads high. Owen remembers the phrase "one number is a symptom, two numbers are a location," but he is not sure he can explain why the pattern points where it does, or how to talk about it without overstepping. He asks you: "What does this pattern actually mean, and how far can I take that reasoning?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, why negative feedback lets two readings (the final hormone plus the upstream signal) locate a fault that one reading alone cannot.
  2. Interpret Owen's specific pattern (low final hormone, high upstream signal) and say which floor of the axis it points to and why.
  3. Contrast it with the opposite pattern (low final hormone, low upstream signal) so the reasoning is complete, and name one real-world caveat (such as pulsing, timing, or lab variation) that makes the tidy picture messier.
  4. Draw the line clearly: state what this reasoning is good for as general education, and where interpreting real results and deciding what they mean becomes clinical work.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose, in yourself or anyone else. Interpreting a real result and deciding what it means for a real person belongs to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains the generic axis and negative feedback as the basis of the two-reading trick.
  • Reads low output with a high upstream signal as a gland-level (primary) problem, correctly reasoned.
  • Reads low output with low upstream signals as an upstream (secondary or tertiary) problem, correctly reasoned.
  • Notes a real-world caveat (pulsing and timing, medications, or lab variation) that blurs the tidy pattern.
  • Identifies what belongs to a clinician and does not diagnose or self-diagnose.
