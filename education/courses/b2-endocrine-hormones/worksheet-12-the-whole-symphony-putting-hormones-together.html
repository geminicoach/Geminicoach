Part B · One Network, Not Twelve Topics

The classic textbook draws one axis per page. The body does not. Reason through the coupling.

  1. Explain why a single hormone level is better read as a snapshot of a conversation than as a verdict on a gland. Give one reason a value could be high that has nothing to do with the gland shouting.
  2. Trace how a chronically active stress axis reaches across the network. Name what happens to reproduction, thyroid output, and growth signaling, and explain why that coordinated shift is sensible in a real emergency but costly when the demand never resolves.
  3. The chapter argues the axes share one energy budget. Use that idea to explain why libido, sleep, blood-sugar control, and concentration tend to slide together rather than one at a time.

Part C · Setpoints That Move on Purpose

Pure negative feedback is not the whole story. Setpoints adapt.

  1. Distinguish homeostasis (defending one fixed number) from allostasis (stability through change). Give one example from the course where the body moves a setpoint deliberately rather than defending it.
  2. Explain allostatic load in your own words, and say why "chronic stress is bad for you" is more precisely stated as a network being asked to hold a temporary adjustment permanently.

Part D · The Levers as the Interface

If individual hormones are mostly off-limits to direct manipulation outside medicine, what can an informed person actually influence?

  1. Name the input layer a non-clinician can touch, and explain why changing those inputs ripples across several axes at once instead of poking one hormone.
  2. State the honest limit of this interface. Explain why the inputs are the highest-leverage move for a fundamentally intact system, yet are not a treatment for a diagnosed condition.

Part E · Read a Claim Like a Skeptic

A post reads: "Your fatigue, weight gain, and low mood are all caused by one hormone being out of balance, and this protocol resets it." Grade it using the four habits from the chapter. Write a sentence or two for each, not single words.

  1. Number or conversation: does the claim treat the hormone as a verdict on a gland, and what questions does it skip?
  2. Association or causation: where does the pitch jump from "we measured X" to "X is your problem"?
  3. Respects the network or pretends it away: why does isolating one hormone to fix a whole cluster fight the architecture?
  4. Source, incentives, and what is left out: what red flag is hiding in the silence about red flags?

Part F · Two Myths, Down to the Studs

Both big myths fail for the same underlying reason. Take each apart.

  1. The "hormones are destiny" myth. Explain why a system built to respond to its inputs is, by that very design, one your choices reach, and why "constrained and influenceable" is a different claim from "fixed and hopeless."
  2. The "one intervention overrides the fundamentals" myth. Explain why a shared budget and coupled axes make it impossible to supplement your way out of chronically broken inputs.
  3. State, in a single sentence, the one fact about the system that makes both myths collapse.

Part G · Scope of Practice

Knowing the line between general education and clinical care is a core competency, not a formality. Understanding the whole network still does not qualify anyone to tell a specific person what their labs mean, whether they need a hormone, or what to do about a symptom. Conditions are diagnosed by clinicians with tests and the individual's full picture, never by a course like this one. Where education ends
This worksheet builds whole-system hormone literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the network works, and treat anything that looks clinical (a symptom cluster that persists or worsens, unexplained weight change, a growing lump, resting palpitations, disrupted cycles, or any lab value someone wants interpreted for a specific person) as a sign that warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician. Understanding the system in general is education; judging, diagnosing, or treating an individual is clinical care. Work within your role, whatever it is: refer when a situation leaves ordinary physiology, work within your license if you hold one, and do not self-diagnose.
