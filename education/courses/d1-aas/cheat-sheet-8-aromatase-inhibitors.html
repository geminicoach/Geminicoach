Chapter 8 : Aromatase Inhibitors

Aromatase inhibitors reduce an enzyme's output, not receptor signaling, and estrogen in men follows a U-shaped harm curve, not a one-way threat.

The Pathway

  • Aromatase (CYP19A1): the single enzyme converting aromatizable androgens into estradiol (E2), expressed in fat, testes, bone, brain, and skin.
  • A higher-dose aromatizable androgen means more substrate through the same enzyme, and proportionally more estradiol, by simple mass action.
  • An ancillary aromatase inhibitor (AI) acts upstream of the androgen receptor entirely, reducing how much estradiol is made from a given androgen supply. It does not touch AR signaling.

Two Inhibitor Families

  • Non-steroidal, reversible (anastrozole, letrozole): triazole molecules that occupy the heme active site competitively; effect tracks blood level and reverses as the drug clears.
  • Steroidal, irreversible (exemestane): a false substrate that forms a permanent covalent bond, destroying that enzyme molecule for good. Recovery needs new enzyme synthesis.
  • "Steroidal" is not "gentler." A suicide inhibitor is, mechanistically, the more aggressive intervention.

On-Label vs. Off-Label

  • AIs are developed and approved for aggressive, near-maximal estrogen suppression in postmenopausal HR+ breast cancer, monitored, with bone-protective therapy alongside.
  • Using an AI to manage estrogen from an aromatizing androgen is a different, off-label goal: toward the normal range, not toward zero.
  • de Ronde (2007): AI use for adult male estrogen management "is therefore not yet recommended," a verdict from a monitored population, not unsupervised use.

The U-Shaped Curve

  • Too high: gynecomastia risk, water retention, emotional lability.
  • Too low: reduced bone mineral density and turnover below roughly the Finkelstein et al. (2016) threshold, regardless of testosterone dose; unfavorable HDL/LDL shift (Bell et al., 2011); joint pain (AIMSS, Hyder et al., 2021); plausible libido, mood, and cognitive effects, with thinner evidence.
  • Testosterone cannot substitute for estrogen's job: Burnett-Bowie et al. (2009) showed rising testosterone with falling spine BMD once aromatization was blocked.

Reading the Evidence Honestly

  • The strongest human data comes from short, monitored clinical trials in men with diagnosed low testosterone, not from typical unsupervised, longer-duration use.
  • Routine clinical estradiol assays are often calibrated for female-range concentrations and can lack precision at the lower male-range values an AI produces.
  • Symptoms are not a reliable substitute for measurement: bone loss can be silent, while unrelated causes can mimic estrogen-deficiency symptoms.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of a two-directional mechanism, not a license to diagnose, treat, or set a suppression target. Persistent joint pain, mood change, or other new symptoms are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
