Anabolic-Androgenic Steroids · Chapter 7

The 17-Alpha-Alkylated Orals

One carbon-position modification lets a steroid survive being swallowed. That same modification is the reason the compounds carrying it share a liver and lipid risk signature no marketing name can chemically separate them from.

52 min read 7 cited sources

Griff, 31, had done his homework, or thought he had. Three months into researching anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS) before ever discussing anything with a physician, he had settled on what felt like the cautious, sensible entry point: an "oral-only" approach. No needles, no injection-site technique to learn, no sterility questions, nothing that felt as clinical or as committing as drawing up a syringe. In his mind, oral compounds were the training-wheels version of this whole subject, a way to dip a toe in before ever considering anything injectable. He had a shortlist too, methandrostenolone, oxymetholone, stanozolol, names he had seen recommended constantly as beginner-friendly because they were pills. If one of them turned out to be too harsh, he reasoned, he would simply try a different one from the list, since they were clearly separate products with separate reputations.

Nearly everything in that reasoning rests on a category error, and this chapter exists to correct it precisely, because the error is common and the stakes of getting it wrong are real. Griff was sorting these compounds by delivery route, pill versus injection, when the far more important dividing line in pharmacology runs through a single carbon atom. Methandrostenolone (Dianabol, often shortened to Dbol), oxymetholone (Anadrol), stanozolol (Winstrol), methasterone (Superdrol), and chlorodehydromethyltestosterone (Turinabol, often shortened to Tbol) are not five unrelated products that happen to share a route of administration. They are five different decorations built on the same one chemical trick, a modification at a specific carbon position that lets each of them survive the trip through the gut and the liver intact enough to reach circulation as an active hormone. That trick has a name, 17-alpha-alkylation, and it does not come free. The very feature that makes these compounds swallowable is the feature that makes the liver, and the blood lipid panel that reflects the liver's downstream work, a stakeholder in the decision to take any of them, and that is true of all five, regardless of how different their marketing names sound or how differently their potency, water retention, or androgenic strength gets described online.

This chapter is about that shared mechanism, not about ranking the five compounds against each other. We will define exactly what 17-alpha-alkylation is and why it exists at all, walk the actual biochemical route by which it damages liver tissue, trace the parallel and less-discussed effect it has on cholesterol transport in the blood, and use both threads to build the single teaching point this chapter is built around: this is a class that shares a risk signature, not a shelf of interchangeable options where one name is inherently "safer" than the next because it sounds gentler. We will follow Griff's shortlist through each section, because the corrections that untangle his reasoning are close to universal among people encountering this specific corner of the subject for the first time.

What 17-alpha-alkylation actually is

Start with the carbon position itself, because the entire chapter hangs on it. Testosterone, as established earlier in this course, carries a hydroxyl group, an oxygen-hydrogen pair, attached at carbon 17 of its four-ring steroid frame. That hydroxyl group is not a decorative detail. It is a chemical handle, and the liver is extremely good at grabbing chemical handles like it and using them to inactivate a molecule. An enzyme family in the liver, principally a set of hepatic hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases working alongside the cytochrome P450 system, oxidizes that carbon-17 hydroxyl, converts it toward a ketone, and in doing so converts testosterone into forms the body can conjugate, water-solubilize, and clear out through bile or urine. This is not a design flaw. It is exactly how the body is supposed to handle a lipid-soluble steroid hormone once it has done its job, and it is also exactly why swallowing a tablet of unmodified testosterone accomplishes almost nothing pharmacologically. Absorbed testosterone passes from the gut directly into the portal vein, which carries blood straight to the liver before it ever reaches the rest of the body, a routing called first-pass hepatic metabolism, and by the time that first pass through the liver is finished, the overwhelming majority of an oral dose of unmodified testosterone has already been chemically dismantled. Almost none of it survives to reach a muscle cell, a hair follicle, or anywhere else carrying an androgen receptor (AR).

17-alpha-alkylation is the chemist's fix for exactly that problem, and the fix is mechanically simple even though the consequences are not. A small alkyl group, either a single methyl group (one carbon and three hydrogens) or, in a smaller number of compounds, an ethyl group (two carbons), is attached directly at carbon 17, on the alpha face of the steroid ring, immediately next to that vulnerable hydroxyl. This is a matter of a few added atoms, nothing structurally dramatic when drawn on paper, but it changes the molecule's fate completely. The added alkyl group creates steric hindrance, a crowding effect that physically blocks the liver's oxidizing enzymes from reaching and acting on the carbon-17 position the way they normally would. The hydroxyl group is still there, but it is now shielded, and the enzymes that would otherwise convert it and mark the molecule for rapid breakdown largely cannot get a grip on it. The steroid survives its first pass through the liver in far greater proportion than an unmodified molecule would, emerges into systemic circulation still intact, and only then goes on to bind AR in muscle, skin, the prostate, the scalp, or anywhere else the receptor is expressed, exactly the way this course's earlier chapters describe.

It is worth being precise about what this modification does and does not accomplish, because Griff's assumption that oral compounds were somehow gentler almost certainly traces back to imagining 17-alpha-alkylation as some kind of protective or stabilizing change, in the sense of making the molecule safer. It is not that. It is a change that makes the molecule harder for the liver's ordinary inactivating machinery to disassemble, and that is a completely different thing from making the molecule safe for the liver to be around. The modification does not make the steroid invisible to the liver. Hepatocytes, the liver's primary working cells, still take the molecule up, still attempt to metabolize it, and still have to process it directly inside their own cytoplasm, at concentrations considerably higher than the liver typically encounters for a steroid molecule that is esterified and injected into muscle or fat tissue rather than absorbed through the gut. According to Niedfeldt (2018), it is precisely the combination of oral absorption and this specific structural resistance to hepatic clearance that produces a compound the liver has to work unusually hard on, repeatedly, every single time a dose passes through, and that repeated, concentrated hepatic exposure is the root of everything the rest of this chapter covers.

It helps to hold the contrast in concrete terms. An unmodified oral dose of testosterone is, for practical purposes, pharmacologically wasted, the overwhelming majority of it is inactivated before it ever leaves the liver on that first circuit, which is exactly why testosterone itself is never marketed as a simple swallowed tablet anywhere in the world. A 17-alpha-alkylated compound flips that outcome, retaining a meaningfully larger share of each dose across that same first pass, without this course needing to lean on a precise, unverified percentage to make the point land. The relative shift, from a dose that is mostly destroyed to a dose that is mostly preserved, is the entire practical reason chemists reached for this specific modification in the first place, decades before any of the five compounds named in this chapter existed as marketed products.

Quick Definition

First-pass hepatic metabolism refers to the fact that anything absorbed from the gut travels through the portal vein straight to the liver before reaching the rest of the bloodstream. It is the liver's built-in first opportunity to inactivate a swallowed molecule before it can act anywhere else in the body, and it is the exact obstacle 17-alpha-alkylation is chemically engineered to get around.

Griff's core assumption, that oral meant gentler, has the underlying chemistry backwards. The reason a compound can be formulated as a tablet at all, rather than requiring injection directly into muscle or fat tissue to bypass the gut and liver entirely, is precisely the structural feature that concentrates hepatic exposure. Injectable esters, covered in an earlier chapter of this course, are absorbed slowly from a tissue depot into the bloodstream and never have to survive a first pass through the portal vein at pharmacologically meaningful concentrations the way an oral dose does. Oral 17-alpha-alkylated compounds are, by the very design that makes them swallowable, the category of AAS asking the most of the liver, not the least. Delivery route and hepatic burden are not independent variables here. They are directly, mechanically linked.

Five names, one modification: the class roster

With the mechanism defined, the five compounds on Griff's shortlist, plus one more this course will return to, stop looking like five unrelated products and start looking like exactly what they are: variations decorated onto the same 17-alpha-alkylated foundation. Walking through each briefly, by structure and by the plain-language reason it is used, makes that shared foundation concrete rather than abstract.

Methandrostenolone (Dianabol, Dbol)

Methandrostenolone is testosterone with a 17-alpha-methyl group added and a second double bond introduced into the steroid's A-ring, a modification that also happens to make it a substrate for aromatase, the enzyme that converts androgens toward estrogen, a topic covered in full in Chapter 3 of this course. It was one of the earliest widely distributed synthetic AAS, developed in the 1950s, and it carries a reputation in strength and bodybuilding communities as a fast-acting compound for rapid gains in size and strength, a claim consistent with its high oral bioavailability and its aromatizable structure driving water retention alongside genuine tissue-building signaling. That reputation is a description of an intended use pattern, not a safety claim, and the evidence grade behind its specific liver and lipid effects sits on the same shared 17-alpha-alkylated foundation as every other compound in this section.

Oxymetholone (Anadrol)

Oxymetholone adds a hydroxymethylene group at carbon 2 on top of the 17-alpha-alkylation, a modification originally developed for treating certain anemias by stimulating red blood cell production, a use this course's chapter on erythropoiesis and boldenone touches on from a different angle. In performance and bodybuilding contexts it has a reputation as one of the most potent compounds for rapid mass and strength gain available in oral form. That same reputation for potency tracks closely with a reputation, well supported in clinical literature, for being one of the more consistently hepatotoxic compounds in this class, a pattern this chapter's mechanism section will explain rather than merely assert.

Stanozolol (Winstrol)

Stanozolol is structurally distinct from the two above in an important way: it is a dihydrotestosterone (DHT) derivative rather than a direct testosterone modification, built with a pyrazole ring fused onto the steroid's A-ring in place of the usual 3-ketone, in addition to carrying the 17-alpha-alkyl group that makes it orally active. Because it is DHT-derived, it does not aromatize to estrogen, and it carries a reputation in bodybuilding communities for producing a leaner, harder look without the water retention associated with aromatizable compounds, a reputation this course's chapter on DHT-derivatives covers in more depth. What that non-aromatizing structure does not change is the 17-alpha-alkylation sitting on the same molecule, and the fatal case report from Shaha, Nagappan, and Badhe (2023), describing cholestatic liver injury and hepatic necrosis in a bodybuilder after several months of oral stanozolol use, is a direct, sobering illustration that a favorable reputation around water retention and aromatization says nothing about hepatic risk, because hepatic risk is driven by an entirely separate structural feature.

Methasterone (Superdrol)

Methasterone carries both a 17-alpha-methyl group and a 2-alpha-methyl modification, a double methylation that intensifies its resistance to hepatic metabolism even further than a single methylation alone. It circulated for a period as an unregulated "prohormone" or "designer supplement," marketed with exactly the kind of softened, non-pharmaceutical language that tends to make a compound sound less serious than its chemistry actually is. Users have reported it as unusually potent for lean mass gain at comparatively low oral doses, a reputation directly explained by that same intensified structural resistance to breakdown, the identical structural feature this chapter identifies as the driver of hepatic strain.

Chlorodehydromethyltestosterone (Turinabol, Tbol)

Chlorodehydromethyltestosterone combines the standard 17-alpha-methyl group with a chlorine atom added at carbon 4, a modification that blocks aromatization while still permitting AR binding. It has a documented history: it was administered, largely without athletes' informed knowledge, as part of the East German state-sponsored doping program described in later-declassified sports medicine literature, valued at the time for producing steady, non-aromatizing strength gains without the pronounced water retention seen with methandrostenolone. Its contemporary reputation as a "milder" oral persists in the same communities Griff was reading, and once again, that reputation speaks to androgenic and estrogenic side effects, not to the 17-alpha-alkylation it shares with every other name on this list.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A "milder" reputation almost always describes androgenic side effects, water retention, or aromatization, the things a user can feel or see directly. It is not a statement about hepatic risk. Chlorodehydromethyltestosterone's reputation for being gentler on androgenic side effects and stanozolol's reputation for producing a "dry" look are both genuinely rooted in real structural differences elsewhere on the molecule. Neither difference touches the carbon-17 modification, and neither compound is meaningfully exempt from the hepatic mechanism this chapter covers next.

Notice the pattern across all five. Each compound differs from the others in a genuinely meaningful way, aromatization potential, DHT-derived versus testosterone-derived structure, degree of alkylation, added ring modifications, and those differences do produce real, distinguishable effects on androgenic side effects, water retention, and potency. None of those differences touch the one structural feature every single compound on this list has in common. All five carry a 17-alpha-alkyl group. All five survive first-pass hepatic metabolism because of it. All five, as a direct consequence, place a repeated, concentrated processing burden on liver tissue that a non-alkylated, injectable compound simply does not. That shared feature, not any individual compound's specific reputation, is the organizing idea for the rest of this chapter.

It is also worth naming why this particular list of five compounds tends to circulate together in the first place, rather than treating their frequent appearance side by side as a coincidence of forum culture. Each one solves the same practical problem, oral availability without an injection, using the identical structural solution, which means each one appeals to the same practical motivation Griff described at the start of this chapter, convenience and a lower perceived barrier to entry. That shared motivation is exactly why a learner is more likely to encounter these five names clustered together in a beginner-oriented conversation than scattered among the injectable compounds covered elsewhere in this course. The clustering is a real pattern. It is just a pattern about delivery convenience and audience, not a pattern about differing hepatic safety.

Why the liver becomes a stakeholder: the hepatotoxicity mechanism

Understanding that 17-alpha-alkylation forces repeated hepatic exposure is only half the picture. The other half is what that exposure actually does inside a liver cell, and the honest answer, according to Niedfeldt (2018), is that the full mechanism is not entirely settled, but the major documented pathways are well characterized enough to teach clearly and to take seriously.

The first and most consistently observed pathway is cholestasis, impaired bile flow. Bile is produced continuously by hepatocytes and exported across the cell's canalicular membrane, the surface facing tiny channels called bile canaliculi, by a set of active transport proteins embedded in that membrane. Alkylated androgens interfere with this export process, and the practical result is that bile acids and bilirubin, a breakdown product of red blood cells that bile is supposed to carry out of the body, back up inside hepatocytes and in the bloodstream instead of being cleared normally. Clinically, this shows up as jaundice, a yellowing of the skin and eyes from elevated circulating bilirubin, along with itching (pruritus) from bile acids accumulating in the skin, and dark urine as the kidneys attempt to clear some of that backed-up bilirubin. Niedfeldt (2018) describes this pattern as one of the best-documented forms of AAS-related liver injury specifically tied to the 17-alpha-alkylated compounds, distinct in its presentation from other forms of drug-induced liver injury.

The second pathway is oxidative stress. Hepatocytes processing an alkylated steroid at sustained, elevated intracellular concentrations generate reactive oxygen species, unstable molecules that damage cell membranes, proteins, and DNA faster than the cell's normal antioxidant defenses can neutralize them. This oxidative burden stresses the hepatocyte's own membrane integrity, and a stressed or damaged hepatocyte membrane becomes measurably leaky. That leakiness is exactly what shows up on a routine blood panel as elevated liver enzymes, specifically alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), two enzymes that normally live inside hepatocytes and only appear in meaningful concentration in the bloodstream when those cells are under enough strain or damage to leak their contents outward. Transaminase elevation is, in this sense, not a separate injury from the cholestatic pattern described above, it is a second, complementary signal of the same underlying hepatocyte stress, and clinicians reviewing bloodwork for someone using a 17-alpha-alkylated compound typically look at both markers, elevated bilirubin alongside elevated ALT and AST, together rather than in isolation, because they can reflect overlapping and reinforcing processes.

Both of these pathways, cholestasis and oxidative stress, are dose-related and largely reversible in the majority of documented cases once the compound is discontinued, a genuinely important and non-alarmist point that Niedfeldt (2018) makes directly: liver enzyme elevations and cholestatic markers in AAS users very often normalize after cessation. But "often reversible" is not the same as "without risk," and the same body of literature documents a smaller set of rarer, more serious outcomes tied specifically to this class. Peliosis hepatis is one of them, a condition in which blood-filled cavities form within liver tissue, effectively replacing normal hepatic architecture with pockets of pooled blood. According to Kou, Watanabe, and Yazumi (2012), documenting a case of hepatic failure during anabolic steroid therapy, and according to the imaging review by Hui and Lee (2019), peliosis hepatis is a recognized, if uncommon, complication of prolonged exposure to 17-alpha-alkylated androgens, and it carries a genuinely serious risk profile because the blood-filled cavities can rupture, causing internal hemorrhage. Hepatic adenoma, a benign liver tumor, is the other rare but serious outcome documented in this literature. Hui and Lee (2019) note that anabolic steroid use is specifically associated with a subtype of hepatic adenoma driven by a particular molecular pathway (beta-catenin activation) that carries its own separate risk of malignant transformation into hepatocellular carcinoma, distinguishing steroid-associated adenomas from the subtype more commonly seen with oral contraceptive use. Neither peliosis hepatis nor hepatic adenoma is common, and both are described in the literature primarily through case reports rather than large controlled trials, an evidence grade this course is obligated to state honestly rather than either dismiss or dramatize. But both outcomes trace back to the same starting point as the reversible, common changes: a molecule that survives first-pass metabolism specifically because it resists the liver's normal processing, and a liver that has to work directly on that molecule, repeatedly, as a structural consequence of the very modification that makes it swallowable.

Think About It

Cholestasis and elevated transaminases are both downstream signals of the same root cause, a liver cell working directly on a molecule it cannot easily disassemble. Before assuming a normal ALT and AST result rules out meaningful hepatic strain, consider what a bilirubin or alkaline phosphatase panel might catch that a transaminase panel alone would not, and why a clinician interpreting bloodwork for someone using one of these compounds would want to see more than one marker before drawing a conclusion either way.

The parallel signature: what these compounds do to blood lipids

Liver strain is the more visible half of this class's risk profile, but the lipid signature that runs alongside it deserves equal weight, partly because it is quieter, and quiet risks are the ones people talk themselves out of taking seriously. The core finding, replicated across decades of research on androgen use in athletes, is that 17-alpha-alkylated compounds produce a distinctly negative shift in cholesterol transport, marked suppression of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) alongside a rise in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).

HDL particles are often described in plain terms as carrying cholesterol away from tissue and back to the liver for disposal, a process called reverse cholesterol transport, which is part of why higher HDL levels are generally associated with lower cardiovascular risk. LDL particles do the reverse, delivering cholesterol to tissue, including, when in excess, to the walls of arteries, where they contribute to the buildup of atherosclerotic plaque over time. A shift that simultaneously suppresses HDL and raises LDL moves a person's overall lipid profile in the least favorable direction on both sides of that ledger at once, and that is exactly the pattern documented with oral 17-alpha-alkylated AAS use.

The classic demonstration of this effect comes from Hurley and colleagues (1984), published in JAMA, comparing bodybuilders and powerlifters before and after a period of androgen use. Their results are worth stating plainly because the magnitude is genuinely striking: androgen use lowered HDL-C and its HDL2 subfraction by roughly 55 percent, while raising LDL-C by roughly 61 percent and the LDL-to-HDL ratio, a commonly used shorthand for cardiovascular risk, by roughly 280 percent. Those are not small or borderline shifts. According to Hartgens and Kuipers (2004), reviewing the broader athlete literature, this HDL-suppressive, LDL-elevating pattern is one of the most consistently reproduced findings across the AAS research base as a whole, and it is specifically and repeatedly linked to oral 17-alpha-alkylated compounds more strongly than to injectable esters, a distinction that tracks closely with the hepatic mechanism described earlier in this chapter, since the liver is also the organ primarily responsible for regulating the production, packaging, and clearance of lipoprotein particles.

The mechanistic link between the hepatic and lipid stories is not incidental, and it is worth naming directly rather than treating the two as separate topics that happen to share a chapter. The liver is where HDL and LDL particle metabolism is substantially regulated, including the activity of hepatic lipase, an enzyme that breaks down HDL particles, and one that 17-alpha-alkylated androgens are understood to upregulate as part of the same hepatic processing burden that also drives the cholestatic and oxidative changes covered above. A liver cell working overtime to process a steroid molecule it cannot easily disassemble is not doing that work in isolation from its other jobs, one of which is managing the lipoproteins that carry cholesterol through the bloodstream. This is precisely why the two risk signatures, hepatic and lipid, travel together across this entire class rather than showing up independently in some compounds and not others. According to Niedfeldt (2018), the lipoprotein changes associated with 17-alpha-alkylated AAS use are, like the hepatic transaminase elevations, generally reported to move back toward baseline after the compound is discontinued, but that reversibility, again, describes a return to a prior state after exposure ends, not an absence of risk during the period of active use, particularly for anyone with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors that this course's later chapter on systemic risk and monitoring will cover in full.

Triglycerides, a third lipid marker measured on the same standard panel as HDL-C and LDL-C, show a less uniform picture across the literature than the sharp, consistent HDL and LDL shifts described above, and this course should not overstate that piece of the picture. Some studies report modest triglyceride elevation alongside the HDL and LDL changes, while others report little meaningful movement, a pattern Hartgens and Kuipers (2004) attribute partly to differences in diet, training volume, and body composition across the study populations rather than to the androgen exposure alone. The honest summary is that HDL suppression and LDL elevation are the reproducible core of this class's lipid signature, while triglyceride behavior is a real but less consistently documented piece of the same picture, worth mentioning to a learner precisely so they do not mistake a normal triglyceride reading for evidence that the broader lipid shift has not occurred.

Evidence Grade

The HDL-suppression and LDL-elevation pattern associated with oral 17-alpha-alkylated AAS is supported by a consistent, decades-deep body of human research, including controlled comparisons in athletes, making it one of the better-established risk signatures in this entire subject area. The rarer hepatic outcomes, peliosis hepatis and hepatic adenoma, are documented primarily through case reports and case series rather than large trials, a real and lower evidence grade that this course states plainly rather than either overstating the rarity or overstating the certainty.

Same modification, same signature: the class-level lesson

Pull the two threads of this chapter together and the actual teaching point comes into focus, and it is worth stating in the most direct terms this course can manage: methandrostenolone, oxymetholone, stanozolol, methasterone, and chlorodehydromethyltestosterone are not five independent risk profiles that a person can shop between the way one might compare five different brands of the same over-the-counter product. They are five expressions of one shared structural modification, and that modification is what generates both the hepatic risk signature covered in this chapter's middle section and the lipid risk signature covered just above. A compound's reputation for potency, its water retention profile, its androgenic strength, and its aromatization behavior are all genuinely variable across this list, driven by genuinely different structural features layered on top of the 17-alpha-alkyl group. None of that variability changes the fact that the carbon-17 modification itself is present, unmodified in its basic mechanism, in every single one of them, and that modification is what is doing the work described in this chapter's two main sections.

This is the sense in which "class" is not a loose organizational convenience for this chapter, it is a biochemical claim. When a compound is 17-alpha-alkylated, it inherits the hepatic and lipid risk signature that comes with that modification, the same way inheriting a gene means inheriting whatever that gene actually does, regardless of what else varies elsewhere in the organism. A learner evaluating a new, unfamiliar oral compound they encounter after this course is complete does not need to look up that specific compound's individual reputation to make an informed first guess about its hepatic and lipid behavior. They need to ask one structural question: is this molecule 17-alpha-alkylated. If the answer is yes, the risk signature this chapter describes is very likely to apply, regardless of how the compound is marketed, how "mild" its reputation sounds, or how different its androgenic and aromatization profile is from the five compounds named here by name.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A frequent claim holds that supportive supplements, most often milk thistle (silymarin) or ursodeoxycholic acid derivatives like TUDCA, meaningfully offset or cancel out the hepatic risk of a 17-alpha-alkylated compound, turning an otherwise risky choice into a manageable one. The honest evidence-graded answer is that these supplements have some biological plausibility, largely from antioxidant or bile-flow-supporting mechanisms, but they do not alter the underlying structural fact that drives the risk, the molecule's resistance to hepatic clearance. Supportive supplementation is not the same claim as risk elimination, and no supplement changes a molecule's carbon-17 structure.

Case study: Griff and the false comfort of the pill

Case

Griff is 31, a recreational lifter with no diagnosed medical conditions, who spent roughly three months researching AAS before this course, without yet having spoken to a physician about anything specific to his own situation. He gravitated toward an "oral-only" approach because it felt lower-commitment and less clinical than injections, and he treated methandrostenolone, oxymetholone, and stanozolol as a menu of separate options he could rotate through if one felt "too harsh," reasoning that a bad experience with one compound would not predict anything about a different one on the list. He arrived at this chapter wanting to understand what actually differentiated these oral options from each other and from injectable compounds, beyond the needle.

Running Griff's assumptions back through this chapter resolves them directly. His first assumption, that oral meant gentler because it avoided needles, gets corrected by the mechanism in this chapter's opening section. The only reason a steroid can be formulated as an oral tablet at all is a structural modification, 17-alpha-alkylation, engineered specifically to survive the liver's first-pass metabolism, and that same modification is what forces liver cells to process the molecule directly and repeatedly. Oral delivery and hepatic burden are not separate variables he could evaluate independently. The convenience of a pill is mechanically inseparable from the hepatic cost.

His second assumption, that a bad experience with one oral compound would not predict anything about another, gets corrected by this chapter's roster and class-level sections. Methandrostenolone, oxymetholone, and stanozolol differ from each other in aromatization behavior, in whether they are testosterone-derived or DHT-derived, and in their specific androgenic reputations, but every one of them carries the identical 17-alpha-alkyl group, and every one of them, according to Niedfeldt (2018) and the case literature this chapter cites, is capable of producing the same cholestatic and oxidative hepatic pattern and the same HDL-suppressing, LDL-raising lipid pattern. Switching from one to another within this class is not switching away from the hepatic risk. It is switching to a different decoration on the identical structural cause of that risk.

His third assumption, implicit rather than stated outright, was that the liver risk and the lipid risk were separate concerns he could weigh independently, perhaps worrying about one more than the other. This chapter's fourth section corrects that directly: the same hepatic processing burden that damages liver tissue is mechanistically connected to the same organ's role in regulating lipoprotein metabolism, which is why the two risk signatures move together across this entire class rather than appearing separately in different compounds. What Griff leaves this chapter with is not a verdict telling him which of the five names is acceptable and which is not, this course does not issue verdicts of that kind. He leaves with the actual analytic tool: the question is not which brand name sounds mildest, it is whether the molecule carries a 17-alpha-alkyl group, because that one structural fact predicts the shared risk signature far more reliably than any compound's individual reputation does.

Where this course stops: monitoring is not a substitute for judgment

Everything in this chapter is mechanism and evidence, not a warning label meant to frighten a learner away from further study, and not permission either. The cholestatic and oxidative hepatic pathways, the HDL-suppressing and LDL-raising lipid pattern, and the rarer outcomes documented in case literature are all real, evidence-graded findings tied to a specific, identifiable structural feature shared across this class of compounds. Understanding that mechanism is genuinely useful, whether the person doing the learning ever takes any of these compounds, is trying to make sense of someone else's choices, or simply wants to evaluate a confident claim on a forum with real knowledge behind the evaluation, the same reasons this course's first chapter named for Priya apply here for Griff.

What this chapter cannot do, and does not attempt to do, is tell any specific individual whether their own liver and lipid profile can tolerate exposure to a 17-alpha-alkylated compound, at what threshold their own bloodwork would signal a problem worth stopping for, or how their personal cardiovascular risk factors interact with the lipid shifts described above. Those are exactly the questions that require an individual's actual medical history, actual bloodwork drawn and interpreted over time, and a clinician's judgment applied to that specific person, not a mechanism chapter applied in the abstract. Bloodwork monitoring, covered in more depth in this course's chapter on systemic risk, exists precisely because the mechanism described here is real enough to require tracking in a real human being, not because tracking numbers on a lab report substitutes for the decision itself.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

For Griff specifically, the honest, non-clinical answer to "which of these three is safest for me" is that this chapter cannot answer that question, and no amount of forum comparison or compound-specific research can substitute for what only a clinician evaluating his actual liver function, lipid panel, and personal and family cardiovascular history could tell him. What this chapter can responsibly leave him with is the ability to recognize, immediately, when a claim about one of these compounds is describing androgenic reputation rather than hepatic or lipid risk, and the discipline to ask the structural question, is this molecule 17-alpha-alkylated, before trusting any brand name's reputation for being the gentle one on the list.

Key Takeaways

  • 17-alpha-alkylation attaches a methyl or ethyl group at carbon 17 of the steroid ring, sterically shielding that position from hepatic oxidizing enzymes so the molecule survives first-pass hepatic metabolism and can be taken orally rather than only by injection.
  • Methandrostenolone (Dianabol), oxymetholone (Anadrol), stanozolol (Winstrol), methasterone (Superdrol), and chlorodehydromethyltestosterone (Turinabol) differ in aromatization, DHT-derivation, and androgenic reputation, but all share the identical 17-alpha-alkyl group and, with it, the same hepatic and lipid risk signature.
  • The same structural resistance to hepatic clearance forces liver cells to process these molecules directly and repeatedly, driving two documented pathways: cholestasis (impaired bile export, producing jaundice and pruritus) and oxidative stress (producing elevated ALT and AST transaminases). Rarer, more serious outcomes include peliosis hepatis and hepatic adenoma, documented mainly through case literature.
  • The same class consistently produces a negative lipid shift, marked HDL-C suppression alongside LDL-C elevation, most dramatically documented by Hurley and colleagues (1984), and mechanistically linked to the liver's role in regulating lipoprotein metabolism, the same organ under strain from the hepatic pathway above.
  • A compound's marketed reputation for being "milder" almost always describes androgenic side effects or water retention, not hepatic or lipid risk. The relevant predictive question is structural: is the molecule 17-alpha-alkylated, not what its brand name or forum reputation suggests.
  • These findings are evidence-graded rather than uniform: the lipid pattern rests on decades of consistent human data, while the rarest hepatic outcomes rest on a thinner, case-report-driven literature. Neither status changes the underlying mechanism or the clinician boundary around any individual's own risk.
Looking Ahead

Every chapter so far has shown androgens shaping a downstream system, gene transcription, hepatic metabolism, blood lipids, that a learner cannot see or feel directly without bloodwork. Chapter 8 turns to a hormone shift this course has referenced repeatedly but not yet examined on its own terms: estrogen. Aromatase inhibitors like anastrozole, letrozole, and exemestane are used specifically to manage the estrogenic effects that arise from AAS use, and understanding why estrogen matters at all, and what actually happens when it is suppressed too aggressively, is the next mechanism this course needs before its later chapters on SERMs and the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis (HPTA) will make full sense.
