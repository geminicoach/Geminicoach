The Scenario

Scenario · Emeka, 29
Emeka has been reading forum posts that describe combining two different oral 17-alpha-alkylated compounds at once as a way to "spread the risk out," on the logic that each individual compound can then be used at a lower amount than either one would need alone, so the liver supposedly never has to handle a large dose of any single molecule. Emeka finds this logic intuitive and wants to know whether it is mechanistically sound: does using two oral compounds together actually divide the hepatic workload compared to using one, or is something else going on?

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanistically, what 17-alpha-alkylation forces the liver to do with any compound carrying it (steric hindrance, first-pass survival, direct hepatocyte processing).
  2. Evaluate Emeka's "spreading the risk" logic directly: does combining two 17-alpha-alkylated orals divide the hepatic burden, or does it concentrate exposure at the hepatocyte level? Explain why.
  3. Name the two documented hepatic pathways this combined exposure drives, and connect the shared lipid signature to the same underlying strain.
  4. State plainly where this explanation ends: this is a mechanism correction, not an endorsement or design of any combined-use approach, and note what would warrant a qualified clinician's involvement.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating mechanistic understanding, not designing or endorsing a way to combine compounds. A responsible response corrects the reasoning error, explains the shared hepatic and lipid mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains 17-alpha-alkylation and why it forces direct hepatocyte processing.
  • Explains why combining two 17-alpha-alkylated orals concentrates, rather than divides, hepatic exposure.
  • Names the two documented hepatic pathways (cholestasis, oxidative stress) tied to that combined exposure.
  • Connects the shared lipid signature (HDL suppression, LDL elevation) to the same hepatic strain.
  • Identifies red flags warranting clinical referral, and does not diagnose or endorse a stacking approach.
