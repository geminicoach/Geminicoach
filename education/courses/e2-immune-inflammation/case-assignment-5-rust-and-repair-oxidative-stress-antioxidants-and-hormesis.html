The Scenario

Scenario · Devon, 34
Devon has a diagnosed autoimmune condition managed with medication and has recently started training for a first half-marathon. A fitness influencer's post convinced Devon to start taking very high daily doses of vitamin C and vitamin E, timed right before and after every run, reasoning: "Hard exercise creates free radicals that damage my cells, so loading up on antioxidants around my workouts should protect me and help me recover faster." Devon asks: "Is this a smart strategy, or is there a downside I am not seeing?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanistically, what reactive oxygen species actually do during and after a workout, distinguishing their signaling role from pure damage.
  2. Explain hormesis and mitohormesis, and use that framework to evaluate whether "more antioxidants around training equals better adaptation" is a sound assumption.
  3. Describe what the research on high-dose antioxidant supplementation around exercise has generally found, and what that implies for Devon's specific timing strategy.
  4. Give one practical, general-education suggestion, and state clearly where general education ends and where Devon's diagnosed condition and supplement questions require a qualified clinician or registered dietitian.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing or prescribing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Supplement dosing decisions, especially alongside a diagnosed condition and medication, belong to a qualified clinician or registered dietitian.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains that exercise-generated reactive oxygen species act as signals (for example, through the Nrf2 pathway or mitochondrial biogenesis), not only as damage.
  • Explains hormesis and mitohormesis, and applies the dose-response logic to Devon's "more must be better" assumption.
  • Describes the general finding that high-dose stoichiometric antioxidant supplementation around training can blunt adaptive signaling.
  • Offers a practical, general-education distinction between ordinary food-based antioxidant intake and megadose supplement stacking.
  • Identifies that Devon's diagnosed condition and specific supplement dosing require a qualified clinician or registered dietitian, and does not diagnose or prescribe.
