Part A · Define the Core Terms

In your own words, define each term and note why the distinction matters.

  1. Reactive oxygen species (ROS): what makes a molecule "reactive," and name one example.
  2. Oxidative eustress versus oxidative distress: what separates the two, given that the molecule involved can be identical?
  3. Redox balance (or redox homeostasis): what is being balanced, and what happens when the balance tips too far in either direction?
  4. Hormesis: define the biphasic dose-response pattern in one or two sentences.

Part C · Reason About Signal Versus Damage

  1. Explain, mechanistically, how hydrogen peroxide can act as a cell-signaling molecule rather than only as a damaging agent. Name one process it helps regulate.
  2. The chapter defines oxidative stress as an imbalance, not merely the presence of oxidants. Why does that distinction matter for how you should read a headline claiming a food "fights oxidative stress"?
  3. What is reductive stress, and why is "just take more antioxidants" not automatically a safe strategy?

Part D · Trace a Scenario

A previously sedentary adult begins a new, moderate exercise habit: brisk walks three times a week, gradually increasing over two months. Using the hormesis framework, describe what is happening at each stage.

  1. During each walk, what happens to reactive oxygen species production in working muscle, and why?
  2. In the hours after each walk, what adaptive signaling is triggered (name at least one pathway or transcription factor), and what is the net effect on the body's defenses?
  3. Over the two months, what would you expect to happen to this person's baseline antioxidant capacity, and why?

Part E · Reason About a Bottleneck

Use the dose-response logic from the chapter, and the Ristow research on antioxidant supplementation described in class.

  1. A person training for a race decides to take very high daily doses of vitamin C and vitamin E, reasoning that "exercise creates free radicals, so more antioxidants must be better." Using the mitohormesis concept, predict what could happen to their training adaptations, and explain the mechanism.
  2. Why is a difference in dose, timing, and duration more important than the simple presence or absence of an oxidant when predicting whether an exposure helps or harms?

Where education ends
This worksheet builds literacy in redox biology, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe supplement doses, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, and treat any question involving a diagnosed condition, medication interaction, or specific supplement dosing as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician or registered dietitian. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-prescribe.
