Chapter 10: Grade a Real Claim

Apply the five-question framework to an actual online claim, and separate what the evidence shows from what the marketing promises.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 20 min

The Scenario

Scenario · A wellness post shared by a friend
A popular wellness account posts: "Megadose vitamin C and zinc together and supercharge your immune system, stop colds in their tracks!" The post cites "studies" without naming them, links to a supplement bundle, and includes testimonials. Your friend, who has read this chapter, forwards it and asks: "Is this actually true? Should I be doing this every day?"

Your Tasks

  1. Separate the claim into its testable pieces (vitamin C's effect on colds; zinc's effect on colds) and grade each one honestly using the evidence from the chapter.
  2. Identify at least two of the five grading questions (what was measured, what was the design, how large/consistent, in whom, who paid) and apply them explicitly to the evidence you cite.
  3. Name the reasoning error in "supercharge your immune system" as a phrase, connecting it to the mechanism-versus-outcome distinction from the chapter.
  4. Write a responsible response to your friend. If you mention any dose, tie it to the specific study that used it, and pair it with a clear confirm-with-a-clinician caveat rather than a recommendation to follow the post's regimen.

Your Response

Where education ends
You are demonstrating evidence-grading, not issuing a personal supplement recommendation. Any dose you cite is a description of what a specific study used, never a suggestion for your friend to follow. The only responsible next step for someone considering a supplement regimen, especially combined or high-dose, is to confirm with a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Correctly grades vitamin C (no general-population incidence benefit; modest duration effect; subgroup benefit under extreme physical stress).
  • Correctly grades zinc (real but heterogeneous signal, I² = 95%, notable adverse events).
  • Names the mechanism-versus-outcome conflation in "supercharge your immune system."
  • Explicitly applies at least two of the five grading questions.
  • Ties any mentioned dose to its source study and pairs it with a confirm-with-a-clinician caveat, not a recommendation.
