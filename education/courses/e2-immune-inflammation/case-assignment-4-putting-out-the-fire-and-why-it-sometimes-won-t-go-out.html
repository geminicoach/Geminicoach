Chapter 4: Reason Through a Real Complaint

Take the model of resolution and chronic inflammation and use it to explain a real complaint, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 20 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Priya, 38
Priya read an article online claiming that "all inflammation is bad and silently damaging your body." Worried, she started taking a daily over-the-counter anti-inflammatory supplement, even though she has no diagnosed condition and no current pain or injury. She tells you, "I figure if I just suppress inflammation all the time, I'll avoid whatever damage it's doing." She asks whether this is a good long-term strategy.

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, why resolution of inflammation is an active process, using specialized pro-resolving mediators, efferocytosis, and the macrophage phenotype switch from the chapter.
  2. Distinguish the acute, protective inflammation Priya is picturing from chronic low-grade inflammation, the type actually linked to diseases such as atherosclerosis and type 2 diabetes.
  3. Evaluate Priya's plan to suppress inflammation daily and continuously without a diagnosed condition. What does the chapter's framing of resolution suggest about permanently blocking inflammation rather than supporting its resolution?
  4. Give one practical, general-education suggestion for Priya, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Your Response

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Whether to start, stop, or continue any supplement or medication, and whether chronic low-grade inflammation is a concern for a specific person, are questions for a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains resolution as an active process driven by specialized pro-resolving mediators.
  • Distinguishes acute, protective inflammation from chronic low-grade inflammation.
  • Correctly identifies efferocytosis and the M1 to M2 macrophage switch as central to clean resolution.
  • Explains why "all inflammation is bad" is an oversimplification, without dismissing legitimate concern.
  • Identifies that evaluating and addressing chronic low-grade inflammation requires clinician involvement, and does not diagnose or recommend self-treatment.
