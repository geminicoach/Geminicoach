The Loudspeaker and the Signal

Why the motor unit, not the muscle, is the thing you have actually been training all along.

60 min read 8 cited sources

You hand someone a coffee cup and they lift it without a thought. Hand the same person a loaded barbell and their whole body reorganizes around the task: the grip tightens, the trunk braces, the face changes. Same arm. Same muscles. The difference between those two moments is not that the second one used "more muscle" in some vague, hydraulic sense. It is that the nervous system reached deeper into a filing cabinet of options and pulled out heavier drawers. The muscle is the loudspeaker. What you are actually training, measuring, and fatiguing is the signal that drives it.

For years you have almost certainly thought about strength in tissue terms: fibers, cross-section, soreness, pump. That vocabulary is not wrong, but it starts one step too late. This course begins one step earlier, at the smallest packet of force the body can actually issue as a command, because nearly every practical question you have about warmups, effort, fatigue, and adaptation gets sharper the moment you move upstream from the muscle to the nerve that runs it.

Throughout this chapter you will follow one learner, Dario, a strength coach with a decade of experience who enrolled in this course chasing a specific frustration. Two of the lifters he trains could post nearly identical numbers on a one-rep max test, yet respond to the same program in completely different ways: one built strength quickly and stalled at moderate loads, the other progressed slowly but kept setting personal records long after the first had plateaued. Dario's usual explanations, better genetics, more discipline, a faster metabolism, never quite closed the gap. He is not a case this chapter will diagnose, and nothing here is a verdict on any specific person he trains. He is a stand-in for an honest, common confusion: mistaking the muscle for the thing you are actually coaching. By the end of this chapter you should understand his lifters' training more clearly than the vocabulary he started with ever allowed.

This matters more for an advanced audience than a beginner one, and it is worth saying why up front. A beginner adapts to almost anything: nearly any reasonable program recruits new territory in an untrained nervous system, so progress arrives regardless of how precisely the stimulus is aimed. Once someone has trained seriously for a few years, that margin for error shrinks. Programming stops being about whether a stimulus works at all and starts being about whether it reaches the specific population of motor units a given goal actually requires. A coach who still thinks entirely in muscle-level language, more sets for more growth, more intensity for more strength, runs out of resolution exactly where advanced training gets interesting. The vocabulary in this chapter is the missing resolution.

The unit you have been ignoring

Here is the reframe, front-loaded so you can act on it immediately: the functional unit of force is not the muscle, it is the motor unit (MU). A motor unit is one motor neuron, a nerve cell whose cell body sits in the spinal cord, plus every muscle fiber that neuron's axon reaches and innervates. When that neuron fires, every fiber it controls contracts together, in one all-or-nothing pulse. There is no such thing as half a motor unit firing gently. It is on, fully, or it is off.

This single fact reframes what "using a muscle" even means. A muscle is not one dial you turn up and down like a volume knob. It is a population of motor units, each an independent little engine with its own on-off switch, and the muscle's total output at any instant is simply the sum of whichever units happen to be switched on. When you produce a small force, most of the units available to that muscle are silent. When you produce a large force, a much larger share of them are active. "Trying harder" is not a fuzzy act of willpower. Mechanically, it is the recruitment of additional motor units and the driving of already-active ones to fire faster, a second lever we will name properly later in this chapter.

The size of a motor unit, meaning how many muscle fibers one motor neuron commands, varies enormously across the body, and that variation is not random. In muscles built for precision, the small muscles that aim your eyes or move a fingertip, a single motor neuron might command only a handful of fibers, sometimes fewer than ten. In muscles built for gross power, the large movers of the thigh and calf, one motor neuron can command hundreds or even a couple of thousand fibers. According to Heckman and Enoka (2012), this ratio of fibers to controlling neuron, called the innervation ratio, is one of the most useful numbers available for predicting what a given motor unit is actually for. Few fibers per neuron buys fine, gradable control. Many fibers per neuron buys a big, coarse, powerful lump of force delivered by a single command.

Think About It

Before reading on: if one motor neuron controls 1,000 muscle fibers, what is the smallest increment of force that muscle can add by recruiting exactly one more unit? Now compare that to a muscle where each motor neuron controls only 8 fibers. Which muscle can whisper a small adjustment, and which one can only shout in large steps?

How researchers actually count a living motor unit

It is worth pausing on how anyone knows any of this, because the method shapes how much confidence the number deserves. A single motor neuron and its fibers cannot be seen with the naked eye inside a living, working muscle, so researchers rely on electrical signatures instead. Needle electromyography can record the distinctive electrical fingerprint of a single motor unit as it fires, called a motor unit action potential, and by carefully varying the recording site and the effort level, researchers can estimate how many separate units exist in a given muscle and roughly how large each one is. A family of techniques grouped under the term motor unit number estimation applies statistical sampling to this same electrical evidence, comparing the size of a whole-muscle response to the size of a typical single-unit response to infer an approximate total count. High-density surface electromyography, the technology behind the human recruitment studies discussed later in this chapter, takes a different approach: an array of electrodes on the skin decomposes the combined electrical signal of a contracting muscle back into the individual units that produced it, letting researchers watch dozens of specific motor units switch on and off in real time during an actual voluntary effort. None of these methods is perfect. Estimating motor unit counts and thresholds from surface signals is genuinely difficult, and figures you will see in textbooks, including the ones in this chapter, are best treated as well-supported ranges rather than exact totals. The underlying order they reveal, however, is remarkably consistent across methods and across decades of independent replication.

Three flavors of engine

Motor units are not simply big or small. Their muscle fibers differ in character too, and the two kinds of variation line up with each other in a strikingly clean way. Burke and colleagues (1973), working in the cat gastrocnemius muscle, produced the classification scheme that still organizes the field five decades later. They sorted motor units into three physiological types based on how the units contracted, how forcefully, and how long they could sustain that effort before tiring.

Slow (S) units are small: few fibers, low force per unit, slow to contract, and remarkably resistant to fatigue. They can fire for long stretches without meaningfully tiring. These are the body's all-day engines. Fast fatigue-resistant (FR) units sit in the middle of the spectrum: more fibers, more force, a quicker contraction, and still enough endurance to sustain real effort for a good while. Fast fatigable (FF) units are the giants of the pool: many fibers, high force, fast and explosive contractions, and a tendency to fatigue quickly. They can produce spectacular output for seconds, not minutes.

Notice the relationship that repeats across all three categories: force capacity and fatigue resistance trade off against each other. The units capable of the most force are exactly the units that tire soonest. The units that almost never tire are exactly the units that produce the least force. This is not a design flaw to be trained around. It is the whole point of the arrangement, and by the end of this chapter you should see why it is close to the smartest possible solution to the problem of controlling a body.

The physiological categories Burke described line up closely with a classification you have likely already met in other contexts: the muscle fiber typing done by staining tissue for the enzyme myosin ATPase or, in more recent work, by identifying the specific myosin heavy chain protein a fiber expresses. Slow, fatigue-resistant motor units are built almost entirely from what are commonly labeled Type I, or slow-twitch, fibers. Fast fatigable units are built from Type IIx fibers, sometimes still called fast-twitch in casual usage, with fast fatigue-resistant units sitting on Type IIa fibers in between. This is a genuinely useful cross-reference, because it means the fiber-type language you may already use in coaching conversations is not a separate idea from the motor unit framework in this chapter. It is the same underlying biology described from the muscle side rather than the nerve side. The nerve decides the order of recruitment. The fiber type that nerve happens to innervate determines how that unit performs once it is switched on.

Henneman's size principle: the rule that runs the whole course

If motor units come in a range of sizes, the obvious next question is: in what order does the nervous system switch them on? The answer is the single most important idea in this course, and it is almost suspiciously simple once you see it.

In 1965, Elwood Henneman and colleagues discovered that recruitment order is neither random nor chosen moment to moment by conscious intention. It is fixed by the physical size of the motor neuron itself. Small motor neurons are recruited first. Large motor neurons are recruited last. As demand for force climbs, units come online in a strict, repeatable order, from smallest to largest. According to Henneman, Somjen, and Carpenter (1965), this ordering, now known as Henneman's size principle, held across the motoneuron pools they studied regardless of which specific muscle or reflex pathway was being examined.

The mechanism behind the rule is elegant and worth carrying in your head, because it explains why the order is so difficult to override on purpose. A small motor neuron has a small cell body, and a small cell body has higher electrical input resistance. By a basic relationship from electrical circuits, voltage equals current multiplied by resistance, so the same amount of incoming synaptic current produces a larger voltage swing in a small, high-resistance neuron than in a large, low-resistance one. Small neurons therefore reach their firing threshold on less synaptic drive. They fire first not because the central nervous system decides to be gentle with them, but because they are simply the easiest cells in the pool to ignite. Larger neurons require more excitatory drive before they cross threshold, so they only join the effort once demand is already high (Henneman et al., 1965; Heckman & Enoka, 2012).

Henneman's original experiments were done in reduced animal preparations using the stretch reflex, a simple, well-controlled circuit in which sensory fibers from a muscle spindle synapse directly onto the motor neurons of the same muscle. By recording how much synaptic input from those sensory fibers it took to make different motor neurons fire, Henneman, Somjen, and Carpenter could rank neurons by excitability directly, rather than inferring it indirectly from behavior. What they found was not a loose tendency but a remarkably orderly relationship: neuron size predicted recruitment threshold with enough consistency that the size principle held up as one of the more reproducible findings in twentieth-century neurophysiology. That reliability is exactly why the rule has survived, largely intact, across sixty years of technological change in how it gets tested.

One more piece of the mechanism deserves a place here, because coaches often ask about it directly: does intending to fire a large unit first, gritting your teeth and trying to summon raw power from a standstill, actually change the order? According to Heckman and Enoka (2012), the size principle is remarkably resistant to override by simple effort or intention in most ordinary voluntary contractions. The ordering is a property of the motor neuron pool's basic electrical architecture, not a preference the nervous system can simply choose to ignore. There are documented exceptions under specific, unusual conditions, certain fast ballistic movements and some reflexes triggered by skin stimulation can produce a degree of reordering, which later research has refined without overturning the general rule. For the loads, tempos, and intents used in ordinary strength and power training, size-ordered recruitment is the default you should assume is running underneath every rep.

Think About It

The size principle is a rule about the size of the neuron, and small neurons happen to command small, fatigue-resistant muscle fibers, while large neurons happen to command large, fatigable ones. Why is that pairing so convenient for the body? What would go wrong if the smallest, easiest-to-fire neurons happened instead to command the biggest, most fatigable fibers?

From cats to humans

You might reasonably ask whether a rule discovered in anesthetized cat preparations survives contact with real, voluntary human movement. It does, and the evidence for that is one of the more satisfying threads in this field. Milner-Brown, Stein, and Yemm (1973) recorded from a small hand muscle, the first dorsal interosseous, while people produced graded voluntary contractions on command. They found that the twitch tension of each recruited motor unit scaled in an orderly, roughly linear way with the force level at which that unit was recruited. In plain terms, the harder a person pushed, the bigger the units that came online, always in the same order. The size principle is not a laboratory curiosity confined to reduced preparations. It is running in you right now, as you read this sentence and as your postural muscles quietly hold you upright.

Modern high-density surface electromyography (EMG), a technique that records the electrical activity of many individual motor units at once through electrodes on the skin, has let researchers watch dozens of motor units firing simultaneously during real voluntary effort. According to Farina, Merletti, and Enoka (2016), the orderly recruitment scheme has largely held up under this closer scrutiny, refined at the edges by newer technology but intact at its core. Computational modeling tells a compatible story from a completely different direction. When Fuglevand, Winter, and Patla (1993) simulated a pool of 120 motor units mathematically, the arrangement that best reproduced real recorded EMG and force output was one with many low-threshold units and comparatively few high-threshold units, recruited strictly in size order. Cat physiology, human recording, and computer simulation all converge on the same answer.

It would be dishonest, and unnecessary, to pretend the picture is perfectly tidy. Farina, Merletti, and Enoka (2016) are careful to note that finer-grained, technology-driven observation has surfaced genuine complexity underneath the broad rule: recruitment thresholds for a given unit are not perfectly fixed from one contraction to the next, and the exact ordering can shift slightly depending on the specific task, the muscle involved, and the speed of the contraction. None of that complexity overturns the size principle as the organizing rule for how muscles are controlled. It simply means the rule describes a strong, dominant tendency rather than a rigid, unbreakable law enforced down to the last motor unit. For a coach applying this material, the practical takeaway does not change: small units first, large units only under real demand, with fine-grained exceptions that matter far more to a neurophysiology laboratory than to a training program.

Why this feels the way it does

Here is where the abstraction pays off in things you and the people you train have actually felt in a session. Because small, fatigue-resistant units are recruited first, light tasks are cheap and nearly endless. Picking up a coffee cup, standing at a counter, walking across a room, these live almost entirely in the slow, tireless units. A person can repeat them all day precisely because the body never had to touch its expensive, high-threshold engines to do them.

Now push toward a near-maximal effort, the kind that shows up at the end of a heavy set. To reach it, the nervous system has to climb the entire recruitment ladder and switch on the fast fatigable units waiting at the top, the big, powerful, quick-to-tire ones. Those units exist to be spent, not saved. So a maximal effort cannot be sustained for long: the body is running on tissue that was built to quit. That is not weakness or a failure of will. It is the size principle expressing itself as the felt difference between an easy warmup set and a grinding final rep.

The force-versus-endurance trade-off that Burke and colleagues identified in 1973 now reads as strategy rather than compromise. Clamann (1993) put the underlying logic sharply: because motor units differ so enormously in the tension each can generate, the order in which they are recruited profoundly shapes the resulting force curve for the whole muscle. Orderly, size-ranked recruitment produces a roughly exponential rise in force output, a very fine adjustment available near the bottom of an effort, and a large, rapid surge available near the top. A muscle built instead from identical, compromise-sized units, all roughly the same middling size, would be mediocre at everything: too coarse to make a delicate adjustment, too weak to produce a real peak. The size principle is what lets one muscle serve as both a scalpel and a sledgehammer, depending only on how hard the nervous system asks it to work.

This also explains a training phenomenon many coaches have noticed without a name for it: why the last few repetitions of a demanding set feel qualitatively different from the first few, not just harder in degree but different in kind. Early repetitions in a challenging set are largely handled by units already recruited near the start of the effort. As fatigue accumulates in those units across the set, force output starts to drop unless the nervous system compensates, and it compensates by recruiting additional units, including higher-threshold ones, to keep the required force steady. This is part of why sets taken close to failure can reach fast fatigable units even at loads that would not have recruited them in a single fresh repetition. Fatigue itself becomes a second route to the top of the ladder, alongside raw load. That single idea underlies a great deal of the ongoing debate about how heavy a set needs to be to build maximal strength, a debate later chapters in this course will take up directly.

Two levers, not one

One clarification will save a lot of confusion later in this course. The nervous system has two distinct ways to increase force, and recruitment is only the first of them. It can (1) recruit additional motor units that were previously silent, and it can (2) drive already-recruited units to fire faster, a mechanism called rate coding. At low-to-moderate force levels, recruitment does most of the work of adding force. As an effort climbs toward maximal, once nearly all available units are already switched on, further increases in force come mostly from firing those units faster rather than from finding new ones to recruit (Heckman & Enoka, 2012). Chapter 2 of this course is built entirely around rate coding and how it interacts with recruitment. For today, hold onto the headline: force is built primarily by adding motor units, in a fixed order, from small to large.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

Someone Dario trains once insisted that a warmup set performed with an empty bar "wakes up the fast-twitch fibers" so they are ready for the heavy sets to follow. Using the size principle, what is actually being recruited during a light warmup set, and what is almost certainly staying switched off the entire time? An empty-bar set lives in the small, fatigue-resistant units near the bottom of the ladder. The large, fast, high-threshold units that produce a genuine one-rep max are not touched until the load, or the fatigue, or the intent behind the lift climbs high enough to demand them. A light warmup readies the joints, the technique, and the nervous system's attention. It does not, by itself, recruit the units that will actually be tested later in the session.

You train motor units, not muscles

Say the reframe plainly, because it is the organizing idea of this entire course: you do not train a muscle. You train a population of motor units housed inside that muscle, and different intensities, tempos, and volumes reach different slices of that population. "Training the muscle" is a convenient shorthand, and it is not going away from everyday coaching language, but underneath that shorthand a much more precise process is always happening, one you can now name and manipulate on purpose.

Three practical implications follow immediately from everything covered so far.

First, intensity is a recruitment statement. When you or a learner in this course prescribes a load or a target level of effort, you are effectively choosing how far up the recruitment ladder the working muscle climbs. A light, submaximal effort stays parked in small, fatigue-resistant units no matter how many repetitions are piled on top of it. If a training goal genuinely requires the high-threshold fast fatigable units, true maximal strength, real explosive power, the effort has to be high enough, or driven far enough by accumulated fatigue, to actually recruit them. There is no shortcut around the order. Volume cannot substitute for intensity when the goal is reaching units that only respond to intensity.

Second, endurance and peak force live at opposite ends of the same ladder. The tissue that lets a person stand or walk for hours is not the tissue that lets that same person jump as high as possible or set a personal record. The size principle explains directly why a single training stimulus cannot maximize both qualities at once. This is the seed of the specificity that the rest of this course will develop in far more detail, chapter by chapter.

Third, "try harder" now has a precise, mechanical meaning. When effort increases, something concrete and specific happens inside the nervous system: more motor units come online, larger units join the pool, and already-active units fire faster. That reframing replaces a lot of vague coaching language with a mechanism you can actually target, describe, and eventually manipulate through how you structure a session. We will lean on this idea hard again in a later chapter, where the idea of strength as a trainable skill turns out to be, in large part, skill at recruiting units efficiently and on demand.

This reframe also clarifies a distinction that advanced coaches eventually have to make explicit: the difference between adaptations that happen inside the muscle fiber and adaptations that happen in how the nervous system commands that fiber. Hypertrophy, an increase in the cross-sectional size of individual fibers, is a property of the tissue itself and accumulates with sufficient training volume across a wide range of intensities. Recruitment efficiency, how reliably and how quickly the nervous system can reach a given motor unit and drive it hard, is a property of the connection between neuron and fiber, and it responds specifically to practice at high effort and high intent. Two lifters can add the same amount of muscle cross-section over a training block and still express very different amounts of strength from it, because strength depends not only on how much contractile tissue exists but on how completely and how quickly the nervous system can command it. This is part of why early strength gains in a beginner often outpace visible muscle growth: a large share of that early progress is the nervous system learning to reach territory it was already capable of using, well before much new tissue has been built at all.

The line we will not cross: education versus performance direction

Before we go further, it is worth naming the frame that governs this entire course, because it shapes exactly what these chapters are for and what they deliberately are not for. This is education, not diagnosis. We explain mechanisms, we sharpen the questions a learner asks, and we point toward qualified professionals when a specific person's situation calls for individualized judgment. We do not diagnose or treat anyone, whether that is yourself or someone you coach.

That boundary is not legal boilerplate tacked onto the end of a chapter. It follows directly from the physiology just covered. Understanding the size principle can tell you, in general, why a heavy set recruits units a light set does not, and why a person cannot sustain a maximal contraction. It cannot tell you whether a specific twinge someone feels during a heavy pull is ordinary fatigue in a fast fatigable unit finally reaching threshold, or whether it is a sign of something that needs a closer look. Unusual, persistent, or sharp pain, sudden and unexplained weakness, numbness, or a strength asymmetry that will not resolve with normal training variation are signs that warrant professional evaluation, not signs to interpret confidently from a mechanism you learned in a chapter like this one. The nervous system's engineering explains how force is built. It does not replace the judgment of a qualified clinician when something in a specific body has gone wrong.

Hold onto why this boundary protects rather than limits. Coaches who speak with the most confidence about a nagging symptom are often the ones least burdened by any sense of where their expertise ends. Confidence sells, and caution rarely does. But a learner who knows exactly where their understanding stops is more trustworthy than one who does not, precisely because they will not turn someone else's real symptom into an improvised, self-directed experiment. Knowing the edge of what you know is not a weakness in this field. It is one of the most professional things you can carry out of this course, because it is what keeps a genuinely useful mechanism from being misapplied to a question it was never built to answer.

It also helps to see, briefly, what the other side of that referral actually involves, because it clarifies just how different a clinician's toolkit is from a coach's observation. When unexplained or persistent weakness genuinely needs investigation, a qualified clinician can draw on tools far beyond what a training log or a coach's eye can offer: manual muscle testing performed against a standardized protocol, nerve conduction studies that measure how quickly an electrical signal travels along a specific nerve, and clinical needle electromyography that can detect the electrical signature of motor units that have been damaged or lost, sometimes long before strength changes become obvious in the gym. These tools exist precisely because distinguishing ordinary fatigue in a fast fatigable unit from a genuine neuromuscular problem is not something that can be settled by watching a lift or asking how a set felt. That is not a criticism of coaching. It is a description of two different jobs that happen to sit near each other, and part of doing the coaching job well is knowing exactly when to hand a concern to the job next door.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Case study: Dario and the two lifters

Case

Dario coaches two lifters, both intermediate, both with nearly identical one-rep max numbers on the squat. The first, working with roughly 60 to 70 percent of that max for high-volume sets, builds strength quickly and adds weight to the bar almost every week, but progress stalls once loads climb past about 85 percent, no matter how much accessory volume Dario adds. The second improves more slowly early on but keeps setting personal records deep into a training block, particularly on singles and low-rep top sets close to true maximal effort. Dario had chalked the difference up to genetics or work capacity. He arrived at this course wanting a better explanation, one he could actually use in programming rather than shrug at.

Run Dario's situation through the size principle and a plausible, mechanism-level explanation emerges, not a diagnosis of either lifter, which is not something a chapter like this one can offer, but a genuinely useful reframe of what each program was actually asking the nervous system to do. Moderate loads in the 60 to 70 percent range recruit a meaningful share of the pool, including slow and fast fatigue-resistant units, and high volume at that intensity trains those units hard and often. It rarely, by itself, drives the nervous system all the way up the ladder to the largest fast fatigable units, the ones that dominate performance near a true one-rep max. According to Clamann (1993), it is specifically the order and threshold of recruitment, not sheer training volume, that determines which units get trained at all.

That is a plausible account of the first lifter's plateau: an enormous amount of well-earned adaptation in the units that moderate loads reach, and comparatively little direct practice recruiting and coordinating the largest, highest-threshold units that near-maximal singles specifically demand. The second lifter's slower early progress and later, more durable gains fit the same lens from the other direction. Frequent exposure to loads near true maximal effort forces the nervous system to climb the entire ladder regularly, including the fast fatigable units that a program built mostly around moderate-load volume may rarely, if ever, reach.

None of this tells Dario which program is objectively better, and it is not this chapter's place to say. Genuine strength development in most contexts benefits from both ends of that ladder: enough moderate-load work to build a base of well-conditioned fatigue-resistant units, and enough genuine near-maximal exposure to teach the nervous system to reach the top of the ladder efficiently and with good technique when it actually matters. What the size principle gives Dario is not an answer but a sharper question: for a given lifter, is the current program actually reaching the units the goal requires, or is it training the same accessible slice of the pool over and over while the units that decide a true maximal effort stay largely untouched?

It is worth naming what Dario actually walked away with, because it is the real deliverable of this section. He did not leave with a verdict on either lifter, and he did not leave with a rule that near-maximal singles are always superior to high-volume moderate work, which the evidence does not support either. He left with a working model: force is built by recruiting motor units in a fixed order, different training intensities reach different slices of that order, and a stalled lifter's plateau is, at minimum, worth examining through the lens of which units the program has actually been asking to work. That is a sturdier starting point for a programming conversation than genetics or work capacity ever was, and it is exactly the kind of question this course exists to sharpen.

There is a second layer to Dario's situation worth drawing out, because it shows the limits of the model as clearly as its strengths. The size principle explains why different loads reach different slices of the motor unit pool. It does not, by itself, explain everything about why one lifter adapts faster than another at a given slice, and Dario would be overreaching if he treated recruitment as the entire story. Recovery capacity, technique efficiency at a given joint angle, sleep, prior training history, and ordinary biological variation all shape how quickly gains accumulate once the relevant units are actually being reached. The size principle answers the question of which units a program is training. It does not answer every question about why training works, and a careful coach holds both facts at once rather than treating one elegant mechanism as a complete theory of performance.

Think About It

Key Takeaways

  • The motor unit, one motor neuron plus all the muscle fibers it innervates, is the true functional unit of force. Muscles are populations of these units, and total force is the sum of whichever units are currently switched on.
  • Units contract all-or-nothing. Force is graded mainly by recruiting more units, and secondarily by firing already-active units faster, a mechanism called rate coding.
  • Motor units trade force capacity against fatigue resistance: small slow (S) units are weak but tireless, large fast fatigable (FF) units are powerful but quit quickly (Burke et al., 1973).
  • Henneman's size principle states that recruitment proceeds in a fixed order from small to large, set by motor neuron size and electrical input resistance, not by conscious choice (Henneman et al., 1965). This order has been confirmed in voluntary human contraction (Milner-Brown et al., 1973) and in high-density electromyography (Farina et al., 2016).
  • This fixed order is why light tasks are endlessly repeatable and maximal efforts cannot be sustained, and why one muscle can be both precise at low force and powerful at high force (Clamann, 1993).
  • Education has a boundary. Understanding recruitment explains mechanisms in general. Persistent pain, unexplained weakness, or an unresolving asymmetry in a specific person are signs that warrant professional evaluation, not signs to interpret from a chapter alone.
