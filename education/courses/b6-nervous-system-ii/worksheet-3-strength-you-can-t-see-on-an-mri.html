Part A · The Two Halves of the Strength Equation

Ground yourself in the chapter's working model before applying it.

  1. State the chapter's working model for strength in your own words: strength equals what, multiplied by what? Explain briefly what each half of that equation actually refers to.
  2. Roughly during which weeks of a new training stimulus does the neural factor account for the larger share of a strength gain, and which study first put numbers on that timeline? What did a more recent systematic review and meta-analysis add to that picture?
  3. Explain why the popular understanding of strength training has historically overweighted muscle growth as the explanation for early progress, even though the evidence points elsewhere.

Part B · Early Gains Before Visible Hypertrophy

Reason about how a strength gain can be genuine before any tissue has visibly changed.

  1. A learner presses meaningfully more weight after five weeks of training, but a tape measurement of the limb shows almost no change. Explain, in your own words, why this is not a contradiction.
  2. Name two distinct research methods used to detect neural adaptation when muscle size has not changed, and explain briefly what each one actually measures.
  3. Why does the chapter treat these two methods, one reading electrical signal amplitude and one comparing voluntary force against externally stimulated force, as a particularly strong basis for confidence in the neural explanation?

Part D · Cross-Education

Use the chapter's evidence to explain one of the clearest demonstrations that strength lives partly in the nervous system.

  1. Define cross-education, and describe the evidence that shows the effect originates in the nervous system rather than in the muscle itself.
  2. Roughly what share of the trained limb's strength gain transfers to the untrained limb, and what did more recent motor-unit level research add to the explanation of how that transfer happens?
  3. Describe one practical, real-world situation in which knowing about cross-education could meaningfully change how a person approaches training or recovery.

Part E · Strength as a Trainable Skill

Connect specificity and deliberate practice to the idea that strength behaves like a learned skill, not only a matter of tissue.

  1. Name and briefly explain the three specificities described in the chapter, exercise-type, joint-angle, and velocity, and explain what each one implies for how carryover works between a trained movement and an untrained one.
  2. Explain why the chapter describes heavy, well-executed early training as, in a real sense, "a skill session dressed up as a strength session."
  3. Given that strength is specific and trainable like a skill, describe one concrete implication for how a training plan should actually be designed.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds understanding of how the nervous system contributes to strength, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, and treat anything that looks clinical, persistent joint pain, sudden sharp pain, swelling, or pain that does not settle with rest, as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
