Chapter 8 : Waking Up the System

Two curves, one difference: net readiness is potentiation minus fatigue, and performance lives in the window where potentiation wins.

RAMP: The Four Stages

1. RaiseElevate temperature, blood flow, and arousal. General, cheap, and essentially non-negotiable. Also speeds nerve conduction.
2. ActivateWake the muscles that will carry the coming pattern; bridges general work into specific work.
3. MobilizeTake the joints through the range the task will demand.
4. PotentiateOne optional high-intensity, low-volume conditioning effort, used only for explosive tasks with a well-trained performer, followed by rest that lets the window open.

PAP vs PAPE

  • Post-activation potentiation (PAP) : transient rise in contractile performance right after a hard contraction; mechanism is myosin regulatory light chain phosphorylation; half-life roughly twenty-eight seconds.
  • Post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) : the practical, minutes-long version; driven mostly by neural drive (recruitment, firing frequency, synchronization), plus temperature and fluid shifts.
  • Both are triggered by the same conditioning effort; they run on different clocks.

The Trade-off

  • Net readiness = potentiation minus fatigue.
  • Intensity mostly buys potentiation; volume mostly buys fatigue.
  • Rest is the timing dial: roughly three to twelve minutes for most trained performers, longer for heavier or higher-volume conditioning.
  • Better-trained performers potentiate more and fatigue less from an identical effort.

Designing the Sequence

  • General raise (about three to six minutes).
  • Activate and mobilize (about two to five minutes).
  • Movement-specific rehearsal, at climbing intensity.
  • Potentiating effort, if used: one high-intensity, low-volume effort, then rest.
  • The attempt, placed at the peak of net readiness.

Common Failure Cases

  • Too much volume in the conditioning effort buries the potentiation under fatigue.
  • Too little rest means attempting on top of unresolved fatigue.
  • Too much rest means the window has already closed.
  • The stacking error: repeating the same conditioning effort before every attempt in a session, without accounting for fatigue that accumulates across the session.
  • Applying a heavy single-effort tool to an under-trained system, where the window may never open at all.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of priming, potentiation, and warmup design, not a license to diagnose, clear someone to train, or prescribe an individualized program. Anything touching injury history, pain, or clearance to load heavily is a question for a qualified professional working directly with that person. Work within your role, whatever it is.
