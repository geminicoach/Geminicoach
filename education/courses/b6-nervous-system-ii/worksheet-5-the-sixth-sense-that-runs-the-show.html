Part B · The Stretch Reflex and the Loop That Beats Thought

Answer each question in full sentences.

  1. Describe the stretch reflex step by step, from a tendon tap to a muscle contraction. Name the receptor involved and state how many synapses the signal crosses inside the spinal cord.
  2. Explain reciprocal inhibition in your own words. Why would a reflex that only switched the stretched muscle on, without also relaxing its opposite, be a clumsy design?
  3. Roughly how fast does the short-latency stretch reflex fire compared to a deliberate, consciously chosen reaction? Why does that gap matter for stabilizing a joint after an unexpected stumble?
  4. The chapter describes a second, slightly slower burst of muscle activity that can follow a sudden stretch. What is different about this second loop compared to the single-synapse stretch reflex, and why does that difference matter?

Part C · Feedforward Versus Feedback Control

Use the vocabulary from the chapter to reason through control, not just define it.

  1. In your own words, distinguish feedforward control from feedback control, and give one example of each drawn from the chapter.
  2. Explain what an internal forward model is and how it allows a movement to be corrected before it is even finished, rather than only after an error is felt.
  3. Trace a scenario: a runner's foot is about to land on uneven, rocky ground. Describe, in order, what happens from just before ground contact through the moment just after contact, naming which parts of the sequence are feedforward and which are feedback.

Part D · The Stretch-Shortening Cycle (SSC)

Reason through the mechanics, not just the vocabulary.

  1. Define the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) in a sentence or two, and name its two engines.
  2. Explain why a deep, slow countermovement produces a weaker jump than a moderate, quickly reversed one, using the idea of elastic energy decaying over time.
  3. Explain why an extremely shallow countermovement also produces a weak jump, even though it is fast.
  4. A drop jump from too high a box produces a long, cushioned landing and a poor rebound. Name two separate things that were lost by letting the ground contact get slow, and explain each.

Part E · Reason Through a Case

Apply the chapter's mechanisms to a described situation. This is reasoning practice, not a diagnosis exercise.

  1. A person's ankle strength returns to normal within weeks of an injury, but they still feel unsteady on uneven ground eight months later. Using the distinction between muscle strength and joint position sense, explain why this is not a contradiction.
  2. A product claims it can retrain someone's nervous system with generic indoor balance drills in six weeks. Using the concept of specificity in proprioceptive training, explain why that claim deserves skepticism for someone who wants confidence on unpredictable outdoor terrain specifically.
  3. Name one thing this chapter's mechanisms can reasonably tell us about a case like this, and one thing they cannot determine. Explain why the second one is out of reach from mechanism alone.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds understanding of proprioception and reflex control, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, and treat anything that looks clinical (persistent unsteadiness after a cleared injury, numbness, tingling, locking, swelling, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
