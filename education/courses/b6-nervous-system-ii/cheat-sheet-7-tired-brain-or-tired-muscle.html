Chapter 7 : Tired Brain or Tired Muscle?

One stalled rep, two possible places it broke: the drive from the nervous system, or the tissue answering it.

Two Places a Rep Can Fail

Central fatigueA reduction in the drive the nervous system actually sends. Fewer motor units recruited, lower firing rates. The muscle has force in reserve, but the command asking for it has shrunk.
Peripheral fatigueA failure at or below the muscle. The command arrives essentially intact, but the contractile tissue itself cannot convert it into the same amount of force.
The split pointThe neuromuscular junction. Upstream of it (cortex, spinal cord, motoneuron) is central territory. At and inside the fiber is peripheral territory.

How Each Is Identified

  • Interpolated twitch technique : an electrical stimulus during a maximal effort. A large superimposed twitch means reserve is being held back centrally; a small one means the drive is nearly maxed out.
  • Perceived effort : generated from a copy of the outgoing command, the corollary discharge, not from feedback sent by the muscle. A bigger command feels harder, even if the muscle has barely changed.
  • Field markers : build a case from converging signs. One signature alone is a clue, not a verdict.

Signatures That Point Peripheral

  • Force loss tracks the local metabolic work; the muscle that did the grinding is the one that is weak.
  • Rest restores force on a metabolic timeline, minutes to hours, longer with real muscle damage.
  • The muscle feels genuinely worked: pumped, burning, shaky, local to that group.
  • It is dose-dependent on the exercise stress just performed.

Signatures That Point Central

  • Maximal and skilled efforts fail before submaximal work does.
  • Effort feels disproportionate to output; last week's easy load feels like a fight today.
  • It responds to non-metabolic cues: a break, competition, a change of task.
  • Context predicts it: poor sleep, heavy prior cognitive load, monotony, low arousal.

The Mechanisms

  • Peripheral : metabolite buildup, impaired calcium handling, a degraded membrane signal inside the fiber.
  • Central, the governor : Group III and IV muscle afferents report metabolic stress back to the central nervous system (CNS) and inhibit motoneurons, protecting the body before a genuinely catastrophic failure of internal balance.
  • Second wind : the governor loosening its grip when the perceived balance of risk and reward shifts, proof the reserve was there all along.

Matching the Recovery to the Evidence

  • If peripheral : substrate and time. Fueling, hydration, sleep for tissue repair, and enough rest for the metabolic or structural cost to actually clear.
  • If central : state, not tissue. Reduce cognitive and emotional load, protect sleep, manage arousal. Can sometimes lift within a session.
  • Applying the wrong fix in either direction wastes recovery time, or in the peripheral case, risks turning fatigue into injury.

Overreaching to Overtraining: the Timescale That Tells Them Apart

Functional overreaching (FOR)A short, deliberate overload. Followed by adequate recovery, it typically produces a rebound above the starting point, within days.
Nonfunctional overreaching (NFOR)The overload goes further, or recovery does not happen. Performance decline and fatigue now take weeks, often with mood disturbance and disrupted sleep.
Overtraining syndrome (OTS)A maladaptive state across the nervous, hormonal, and immune systems that can take months to resolve.
The tellNot the size of one bad session. It is duration and pattern across weeks that separates a rough patch from a developing problem.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how central and peripheral fatigue work, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. A pattern of stalled progress, disrupted sleep, and flattening mood that persists for weeks despite adequate rest, or unexplained tiredness that will not lift, is a signal that warrants evaluation by a qualified professional. Work within your role, whatever it is.
