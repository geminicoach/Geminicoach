Strength You Can't See on an MRI

Why a large share of strength is neural, not muscular, and how to train it as a skill rather than only as tissue.

60 min read 8 cited sources

A learner walks into week one of a strength program able to press a modest load. Six weeks later that same learner is pressing dramatically more, moving with obvious confidence, and their sleeve still fits exactly the same. Measure the arm with a tape and you would find almost nothing new. Scan it with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the tool researchers actually use to measure muscle cross-sectional area, and the tissue would look nearly identical to where it started. And yet the strength is real. It is repeatable, it is measurable on a bar or a machine, and it did not come from nowhere. Where did it come from?

It came from the nervous system. In the first weeks of a new training stimulus, the biggest lever on strength is not new tissue. It is a better signal reaching the tissue a person already has. This is the most counterintuitive claim in this course, and it is one of the most useful things you will ever be able to explain to yourself or to someone you train alongside: strength is, in large part, a skill, not only a matter of building bigger muscle.

Later in this chapter you will meet Malachi, a learner whose early progress in a training program becomes the thread we use to test every idea in this chapter against a real situation. His confusion is common, understandable, and, by the time you reach the end of this chapter, easy to untangle.

It is worth being clear from the start about who this idea applies to, because the popular image of neural strength gains is almost always a young, healthy person in a gym chasing a bigger number on a barbell. That image is far too narrow. The same neural drive that lets a beginner press more weight without new muscle is the thing an older adult relies on to rise from a chair without using their arms, the thing a person recovering from a cast leans on to regain grip strength before the surrounding tissue has caught up, and the thing a physical therapist is quietly training every time they ask someone to practice a movement pattern slowly and with full attention rather than simply adding load. Strength as a skill is not a niche idea for competitive lifters. It is a description of how the nervous system and the muscular system actually divide the labor of producing force, in everyone, across every stage of life.

The claim, stated plainly

Here is the working model for this chapter, stated up front so you can hold it in mind as everything else gets layered on top of it:

Strength equals how much force-producing tissue a person has, multiplied by how well the nervous system can drive that tissue. Chapters 1 and 2 built the machinery underneath that second term: motor units, the size principle, and the two dials of force, that is, how many motor units get recruited and how fast those units fire once they are recruited. This chapter is about what happens to that second half of the equation in the early weeks of training, because for roughly the first month of any new training stimulus, it is doing most of the work.

Moritani and deVries (1979) put numbers on this claim in a study that still anchors the field decades later. Over eight weeks of elbow-flexor training in young adults, they mathematically separated the measured strength gain into what they called a neural factor and a hypertrophic factor. For roughly the first three to five weeks, the neural factor accounted for the larger share of the increase. Only after that early window did muscle growth take over as the dominant driver of further gains. A more recent systematic review and meta-analysis reaches essentially the same conclusion with far more modern tools: early-phase strength gains are driven primarily by reduced motor-unit recruitment thresholds, increased firing rates, and improved synchronization among units, with structural remodeling of the muscle becoming the dominant contributor only as training continues over subsequent months (Rong et al., 2025).

It is worth pausing on why the popular understanding of strength training got this backward for so long. Hypertrophy is visible. A person can look in a mirror, take a photograph, or wrap a tape measure around a limb and see a number change. Neural drive produces no equivalent artifact a person can look at directly. It has to be inferred from performance, from electrical recordings, or from careful laboratory comparisons between voluntary and involuntary muscle activation. Visible evidence tends to dominate a story even when it is not the whole story, and for decades the fitness industry built almost its entire vocabulary, pump, definition, growth, around the part of strength adaptation that happens to be photographable. None of that vocabulary is wrong exactly. It is simply describing the second half of the timeline while mostly ignoring the first.

Why "neural" is not hand waving

When strength gains get described as neural, it can sound like a shrug, a placeholder for we are not sure. It is not that. There is direct, converging evidence behind the claim, and the logic behind it is refreshingly simple. Sale (1988) laid out the reasoning, later formalized further by Gabriel, Kamen, and Frost (2006): if strength rises while muscle size does not, something other than the tissue itself must have changed. That something is the nervous system's ability to activate the muscle it already has.

The primary research tool used to observe this is surface electromyography (EMG), a technique that records the electrical activity arriving at a muscle from the nerves that drive it, picked up through electrodes placed on the skin. Across a wide range of studies, early strength gains track closely with increases in EMG amplitude recorded from the prime movers, and that pattern is interpreted as increased neural drive, meaning a larger and more forceful volley of nerve impulses reaching the muscle for a given intended effort (Gabriel et al., 2006).

Surface electromyography is not the only line of evidence, and it helps to know at least one more, because it comes at the same question from a completely different angle and lands on the same answer. Researchers can compare the force a person produces when asked to contract a muscle as hard as they voluntarily can against the force the same muscle produces when it is stimulated directly through the skin by an external electrical current, a method generally called the twitch interpolation technique. In an untrained person, the voluntary effort typically falls noticeably short of what the muscle is actually capable of producing under direct stimulation. Training closes a meaningful portion of that gap, which is exactly what you would expect if part of getting stronger is learning to voluntarily access a larger share of the force a muscle already had available. Two independent methods, one reading electrical signal amplitude and one comparing voluntary force against externally stimulated force, converge on the same conclusion, which is a strong reason for confidence in the underlying claim rather than treating it as a single study's fluke.

You do not need access to an EMG lab or a stimulation device to make use of any of this. What you need is the underlying mental model: a person can get measurably stronger by delivering a cleaner, larger, and better-timed signal to muscle tissue they already own, with no new tissue required at all. That single idea reframes a great deal of what heavy, maximal-effort training actually accomplishes in its first several weeks.

The four neural contributors to strength

These are the specific levers that make up neural drive. Each one is trainable, each maps directly back to the machinery introduced in Chapters 1 and 2, and each gives you a concrete way to think about what a hard, well-executed set of exercise is actually rehearsing.

1. Fuller recruitment of high-threshold units

Recall the size principle from Chapter 1: motor units are recruited in order from smallest to largest as the demand for force rises. The largest, highest-threshold units, the ones attached to the most powerful and fastest-contracting muscle fibers, function as a kind of reserve. Most untrained people cannot voluntarily access all of this reserve on command. There is a measurable gap between the force a person can produce voluntarily and the force the same muscle can produce when it is maximally stimulated by an outside source in a laboratory setting. Training narrows that gap over time. Part of what it means to get stronger is simply learning to recruit deeper into the available pool of motor units, more completely and more reliably, whenever the task demands it. This is the first dial from Chapter 2, recruitment, and heavy, maximal-effort training is, in part, practice at reaching further into it.

2. Increased firing rate

Once a motor unit has been recruited, the force it contributes still depends on how fast it fires, which is the second dial from Chapter 2, usually called rate coding. Higher discharge rates translate directly into more force, and, just as importantly, into force that arrives faster. Training studies show that resistance exercise shifts motor-unit discharge behavior toward higher sustained rates and, in some cases, produces brief bursts of very high-frequency firing right at the onset of a contraction that helps force build more quickly (Duchateau et al., 2006; Gabriel et al., 2006). This is a large part of why trained people do not just produce more peak force. They produce force faster, which shows up as an improved rate of force development, a quality that matters enormously any time a movement has to be fast rather than merely maximal.

3. Reduced antagonist co-contraction

Every joint in the body is spanned by muscles that pull in opposing directions. When the elbow flexes, the biceps acts as the agonist, the muscle producing the movement, while the triceps acts as the antagonist, the muscle that would extend the elbow if it contracted instead. An untrained nervous system, cautious and protective by default, tends to co-activate the antagonist to some degree during a movement, quietly braking the very motion it is trying to produce. It behaves a little like driving with the parking brake partially engaged. A more skilled, better-trained nervous system learns to dial down that unnecessary co-contraction, so that a larger share of the agonist's force actually shows up as usable force at the joint rather than being spent fighting itself (Sale, 1988). Notice what this means in practice: a person can become measurably stronger without producing one additional unit of muscular effort, simply by removing a brake that was never doing anything useful in the first place.

4. Improved intermuscular coordination

No compound movement is ever a single muscle acting in isolation. A squat, a press, or a deadlift is a coordinated conversation among prime movers, stabilizing muscles, and synergists, all of them timed and scaled correctly relative to one another. Learning to sequence and blend these separate contributions is a coordination skill, and like most coordination skills it improves with deliberate practice. Better intermuscular coordination means the whole system delivers force more efficiently as a unit, even when no single muscle involved has changed in size at all (Gabriel et al., 2006).

Think About It

Of these four contributors, which two could plausibly improve within a single training session, well before any structural change in tissue is even biologically possible? What does that imply about the value of a person's very first working set as a kind of primer for the nervous system, rather than as wasted effort before the "real" work begins?

Why this matters well beyond the weight room

It is easy to read the four contributors above as a story about competitive lifting, but the same machinery is doing quiet, essential work in far more ordinary settings. Consider an older adult who has become noticeably weaker over several years, not because their muscle fibers have vanished, though some fiber loss is a normal part of aging, but because the nervous system has grown less efficient at recruiting the fibers that remain and less practiced at coordinating them across a joint. This is a large part of why structured strength training in older populations so often produces meaningful, functional improvement, easier stairs, a steadier gait, a safer transfer out of a chair, well before any noticeable change in muscle bulk. The nervous system is simply relearning a skill it had let go slack.

Consider, too, a person recovering from a broken wrist or a surgically repaired shoulder, whose limb has spent weeks in a cast or a sling. Some of the strength they lost during that time is genuine disuse atrophy, actual tissue loss. But a meaningful share of it is neural: the nervous system has simply stopped practicing the skill of driving that limb at high output, and much of the early return of strength during rehabilitation reflects the nervous system relearning that skill quickly, well ahead of any equivalent recovery in tissue size. Recognizing this changes what early rehabilitation progress should be expected to look like, and it is one of the clearest everyday illustrations of exactly the same four contributors described above operating outside a competitive training context entirely.

Applied corollary of the neural, hypertrophic time course

This is also the honest answer to why beginner programs work almost regardless of their finer details, why early progress tends to be fast and then reliably slows, and why layering on hypertrophy-focused protocols too early for a true beginner is often premature. They are still, quite productively, cashing in the cheap and fast neural gains first, and there is no shortcut around that early phase, only an understanding of what it actually is.

The parking brake problem: antagonist co-contraction

Of the four contributors described above, reduced antagonist co-contraction is the easiest one to underestimate and, once you feel it directly, the most satisfying to understand. Net force available at a joint is not simply whatever the agonist produces. It is the agonist's output minus whatever the antagonist happens to be contributing against it at the same moment. A nervous system that co-activates the antagonist out of caution is spending real metabolic effort fighting itself, with nothing useful to show for that effort.

The elegant part of this mechanism is that reducing unnecessary braking costs nothing in terms of additional muscular effort. The agonist works exactly as hard as it did before. The nervous system simply stops opposing it as much. That is why a more skilled, better-coordinated person can express noticeably more strength from the same physiological muscle tissue as a less skilled person, without either of them producing one additional unit of raw muscular output. Two phenomena that prove the point

If strength were purely a matter of muscle tissue, two well-documented phenomena simply could not happen the way they reliably do. They both happen, they have both been measured repeatedly, and each one is a genuinely useful thing to be able to explain clearly.

Cross-education: train one limb, strengthen the other

Train only the right arm for several weeks, and the untrained left arm gets measurably stronger too, with no hypertrophy occurring in that untrained arm at all. The muscle on the untrained side did not change, so the adaptation cannot be muscular. It has to be neural, operating somewhere at the level of the spinal cord and the brain. A meta-analysis of sixteen controlled studies pegged this cross-education effect at roughly 8 percent of initial strength, about half the size of the gain achieved by the trained side, specific to the corresponding muscle on the opposite side of the body, and consistent across a range of ages and both sexes (Carroll et al., 2006). A 2025 study using high-density surface electromyography to track individual motor units over time helped confirm the mechanism directly: eight weeks of one-arm training raised untrained-arm maximal voluntary force by roughly 7 to 10 percent, alongside lowered motor-unit recruitment thresholds and higher discharge rates in that untrained arm that closely mirrored what was happening in the trained arm, which is direct evidence that increased output from the spinal motor neurons themselves carries the effect across to the other side of the body (Bazzucchi et al., 2025). Even the original Moritani and deVries (1979) data hinted at this same phenomenon in an untrained, contralateral arm decades earlier.

The mechanism proposed for cross-education involves the nervous system's control pathways rather than any direct physical connection between the two limbs. Motor commands generated for one side of the body are not perfectly isolated to that side. Some of the neural drive intended for the trained limb appears to spill over through pathways that cross at the spinal cord and through brain regions that participate in planning movement on both sides of the body, even when only one side is actually moving. That spillover is modest by design, which is exactly why the untrained limb gains roughly half as much as the trained one rather than an equal amount, but it is large enough to be reliably measured across dozens of studies and multiple muscle groups (Carroll et al., 2006).

This has a genuinely practical use. When one limb is injured or immobilized, deliberately training the healthy limb helps preserve strength on the injured side. That is not folk wisdom passed around a gym. It is a measurable neural effect that can be put to work, and it is one of the clearest possible demonstrations that the nervous system, not the muscle in isolation, is the thing actually producing the number a person sees when they test their strength.

Specificity: strength is sharpest where it was built

Neural adaptations are, at bottom, learned adaptations, and learning tends to be specific to the conditions under which it happened. Morrissey, Harman, and Johnson (1995) laid out three specificities worth carrying forward from this chapter. Exercise-type specificity means gains are largest when strength is tested using the same movement pattern that was actually trained. Joint-angle specificity means strength improves most at the specific joint angles that were trained, with only partial carryover to angles that were not directly trained. Velocity specificity means strength gains are greatest at or near the speed used in training, again with limited carryover to very different speeds. Train slow and heavy for months and a person becomes genuinely, verifiably strong, slowly. That strength does not automatically transfer to producing force quickly. Sale (1988) framed all of this as evidence that strength itself is best understood as a trained motor pattern, not simply a bigger engine sitting underneath every movement equally.

The uncomfortable, useful implication follows directly: if a particular position or speed matters for a person's goals and it is not represented anywhere in their training, it will not magically transfer in from somewhere else. Specificity is not a limitation to lament. It is a design instruction, and one that only becomes visible once you understand why strength behaves this way in the first place.

It is worth being precise about what specificity does and does not mean, because it is easy to overcorrect in either direction. It does not mean that training only ever helps in the exact position it was performed. There is real carryover to nearby joint angles and nearby speeds, and general qualities such as tendon stiffness, coordination skill, and overall work capacity transfer more broadly than raw force output at one specific angle does. What specificity does mean is that carryover falls off the further a tested position or speed sits from the trained one, and that a person cannot assume a quality trained in one narrow context will show up fully formed in a very different one. Treat specificity as a dial to be respected, not a wall that makes cross-training worthless.

Case: Malachi and the number he did not trust

Case

Malachi is 34 and five weeks into his first structured strength program. In week one he could press a bar loaded to a modest weight for a handful of clean repetitions. By week five that number had climbed by nearly a quarter, his technique looked noticeably more confident on video, and he could feel himself bracing and moving differently under the bar. His forearms and shoulders, when he checked in the mirror, looked exactly like they had on day one. A training partner, meaning well, told him that his new number was not real strength, just nervous-system tricks that would vanish the moment he took a week off, and that only visible muscle growth counted as genuine progress. Malachi left that conversation feeling like a fraud about a number he had, in fact, actually lifted.

Run Malachi's situation through this chapter and the confusion mostly dissolves. Start with the claim that his gain was not real. The force he produced on the bar was measured with a fixed, external load. Physics does not care whether the force that moved that load originated from more muscle tissue or from a better-coordinated signal reaching the same tissue he already had. A 20 percent increase in the weight lifted for the same number of repetitions is a 20 percent increase in force output, full stop. What changed underneath that number, in Malachi's case and in the vast majority of five-week beginners, is almost entirely the four contributors described earlier in this chapter: fuller recruitment of his available motor units, faster firing once those units were recruited, less antagonist braking at the shoulder and elbow, and noticeably better coordination among the muscles doing the pressing (Moritani & deVries, 1979; Gabriel et al., 2006).

Move to the claim that the gain will vanish the moment he stops training. This does not match what the evidence shows either. Neural adaptations are not a fragile illusion sitting on top of unchanged tissue. They are genuine, measurable changes in how the nervous system drives that tissue, and while any adaptation, neural or muscular, can fade with prolonged detraining, there is no evidence that neural strength gains are somehow less durable or less real than gains built through hypertrophy. If anything, a learned skill, once it has been practiced enough, tends to be recovered faster the second time around than it was built the first time, a pattern this course will return to directly in the chapter on motor learning.

Now consider what Malachi's training partner got right by accident. It is true that Malachi's arm looks the same. That observation is accurate. The error is in what that observation is taken to mean. A person can reasonably conclude that the tissue has not changed much yet. It is a mistake to conclude from that same observation that the strength itself is somehow fake, borrowed, or temporary. Those are two entirely different claims, and only the first one is actually supported by what the mirror shows.

What Malachi actually needed was not reassurance that his number was real, though it was, but a working model of why it was real, so that the next uninformed opinion he encountered would not be able to talk him out of trusting his own progress. Once he understood the four contributors described earlier in this chapter, his five weeks stopped looking mysterious. He could point to specific, concrete changes: a bar path that had become noticeably more consistent from set to set, a bracing pattern through his trunk and shoulder that he no longer had to think about, and a subjective sense that lifts which used to feel like a fight now felt merely heavy. None of that required new muscle to be true. All of it is exactly what learning to drive existing tissue more effectively looks like from the inside.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common belief holds that because so much of early strength is neural, any strain, twinge, or joint discomfort that shows up during that same window must also be neural, and therefore safe to simply push through, since real tissue supposedly is not involved yet. But neural adaptation and tissue tolerance are two different systems running on two different timelines. A nervous system that is rapidly getting better at recruiting motor units can still be commanding a tendon, a joint, or a connective tissue structure that has not caught up to the new demand being placed on it. The rise in usable force can genuinely outpace the rise in local tissue capacity, particularly in tendons and connective tissue, which tend to adapt more slowly than the nervous system does. Being told a gain is neural is not a safety certificate for the joint underneath it.

The line we will not cross: understanding versus evaluation

Before moving on to how this chapter's model changes the way a person might approach their own training, it is worth naming the boundary that governs everything in this course. This is education, not diagnosis. We explain mechanisms, and where a specific body, a specific pain, or a specific injury is involved, we point toward a qualified clinician rather than attempting to resolve it here.

That boundary matters directly in this chapter, not as an abstract disclaimer but because of exactly the misunderstanding described above. A learner who has absorbed the idea that strength gains are largely neural in the early weeks might reasonably start wondering whether a nagging ache during that same period is also just the nervous system adapting, and therefore nothing to worry about. Sometimes early discomfort really is ordinary, low-stakes adaptation: mild soreness, unfamiliar sensation in a joint that is simply being asked to do something new, the kind of thing that settles within a session or two. Other times, discomfort is the tissue's own signal that load has outpaced its current capacity, and continuing to push through it risks turning a minor irritation into an actual injury. Nothing in this chapter, and nothing an EMG readout or a strength number on a bar can tell a person, is capable of reliably sorting one of those from the other. That distinction belongs to a clinician who can examine the specific joint, the specific history, and the specific pattern of symptoms involved.

The discipline this course practices is straightforward to state, even if it takes some restraint to actually follow. Understanding that neural drive typically outpaces tendon and connective tissue adaptation is genuinely useful knowledge. It should make a learner appropriately cautious about ramping load too quickly, and it should make persistent joint pain, as opposed to ordinary muscular fatigue, something that gets taken seriously rather than dismissed as just neural. But translating a specific ache in a specific person into a specific verdict about tissue damage is not something this chapter, or any educational material, is equipped to do responsibly. Knowing the mechanism is what makes a good, timely referral possible. It is not a substitute for one.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Putting it to work: strength as skill acquisition

Collapse everything in this chapter into a single operating stance and it changes how a person thinks about their own training, or explains someone else's progress to them. Heavy, near-maximal work in the early weeks of a program is not only building tissue. It is rehearsing full recruitment, high firing rates, reduced antagonist braking, and clean intermuscular coordination, all at a specific position and a specific speed. That makes it, in a very real sense, a skill session dressed up as a strength session.

A few practical implications fall directly out of that reframe.

Match expectations to the phase of training

Early in a program, expect and welcome fast gains that are mostly neural in origin. Loads should be challenging enough to demand genuinely deep recruitment, technique should stay clean because a person is, quite literally, still learning the movement pattern itself, and there is no reason to be discouraged when a scale or a tape measure barely budges during this window. Later in a program, once someone has been training consistently for many months, it becomes reasonable to expect that neural gains are largely banked, and that continued progress will lean more heavily on hypertrophy and on finely targeted, position-specific work.

Treat heavy work as a skill, and dose it like one

Skills degrade under fatigue and improve through quality repetition rather than sheer volume of repetition. That argues for enough near-maximal exposure to keep the underlying access skill sharp, delivered at a quality that never collapses into sloppy, overly braced, heavily co-contracted repetitions. A grinding repetition performed with breakdown in technique is teaching the nervous system the wrong lesson, even if the weight technically moves.

Cover the positions and speeds that actually matter

Because strength is specific, it is worth auditing a training plan against the joint angles and speeds that a person's actual goals demand. If those positions and speeds are missing from the plan, they will not appear on their own. They have to be built in deliberately.

Use cross-education around injury

When a limb is genuinely out of commission, whether from surgery, immobilization, or a clinician's instruction to rest it, training the healthy limb can help defend strength on the injured side in the meantime. This is a low-cost, evidence-backed strategy worth knowing about, not a substitute for whatever rehabilitation a clinician has actually prescribed (Carroll et al., 2006).

Do not mistake "it is neural" for "it is minor"

The single biggest misreading of this chapter would be to conclude that neural strength gains are somehow lesser, temporary, or not worth respecting compared to gains built through visible tissue growth. They are not lesser. They are earlier, and they are exactly as real as anything measured on a scale afterward. The corollary, covered earlier through Malachi's training partner and again through the education boundary section, is just as important to hold onto: neural gains arriving quickly does not mean the joints and connective tissue underneath them have caught up at the same pace. Respecting both halves of that idea at once, the reality of the strength and the caution about the tissue beneath it, is what separates an informed learner from someone simply repeating a slogan they picked up secondhand.

One honest caveat belongs here, so the science gets represented accurately rather than oversold. Researchers can measure that neural drive increases with training, but the precise motor-unit-level changes responsible for that increase are still being worked out in detail. Voluntary activation reliably rises with training, yet the exact discharge adaptations underlying that rise remain incompletely characterized even today (Duchateau et al., 2006). The practical model laid out in this chapter is solid and well supported. The fine-grained neurophysiology underneath it is still being actively written, and that is a normal, healthy state for a field of science to be in, not a reason to distrust the parts that are well established.

Key Takeaways

  • Strength equals force-producing tissue multiplied by how well the nervous system drives it. For roughly the first three to five weeks of a new training stimulus, neural drive is the dominant lever (Moritani & deVries, 1979).
  • Strength rising without measurable muscle growth is the core evidence that early adaptation is neural rather than structural (Sale, 1988; Gabriel et al., 2006).
  • Four trainable neural contributors drive this effect: fuller recruitment of high-threshold motor units, higher firing rates, reduced antagonist co-contraction, and improved intermuscular coordination.
  • Reducing unnecessary antagonist braking increases usable force at the joint at zero additional muscular cost.
  • Cross-education strengthens an untrained limb by roughly 8 percent with no hypertrophy occurring there, direct proof the effect lives in the nervous system (Carroll et al., 2006; Bazzucchi et al., 2025).
  • Strength is specific to the exercise, joint angle, and velocity that were actually trained, so the positions and speeds that matter for a person's goals have to be trained directly (Morrissey et al., 1995).
  • Neural drive typically outpaces tendon and connective tissue adaptation, which is a reason for caution with load, not a reason to assume early discomfort is automatically harmless.
  • This is education, not diagnosis. Understanding the neural model of strength supports better judgment and better referrals. It never replaces a qualified clinician's evaluation of a specific injury.
