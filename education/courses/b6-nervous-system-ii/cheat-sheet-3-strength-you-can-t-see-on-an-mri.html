Chapter 3 : Strength You Can't See on an MRI

Strength equals tissue multiplied by how well the nervous system drives it. Early gains are mostly the second half of that equation.

The Core Model

The equationStrength = force-producing tissue × how well the nervous system drives that tissue.
Weeks 1 to about 5Neural drive is the dominant lever. Muscle cross-sectional area barely moves (Moritani & deVries, 1979).
Weeks 5 and beyondHypertrophy gradually becomes the dominant driver of further strength gains (Rong et al., 2025).
Why it looked backwardHypertrophy is visible in a mirror or a tape measure; neural drive has to be inferred from performance, electrical recordings, or lab comparisons.

The Four Neural Contributors

  • Fuller recruitment : accessing more of the high-threshold motor-unit reserve.
  • Increased firing rate : recruited units fire faster once recruited, building force quicker.
  • Reduced antagonist co-contraction : less braking from the opposing muscle, at zero added muscular cost.
  • Improved intermuscular coordination : prime movers, stabilizers, and synergists sequence and blend more efficiently.

Two Lines of Evidence

  • Surface EMG : electromyography records electrical activity reaching a muscle; amplitude rises track early strength gains.
  • Twitch interpolation : compares voluntary effort against force from direct electrical stimulation; training closes the gap.
  • Two independent methods converge on the same answer: better signal, same tissue.

Cross-Education

  • Training one limb strengthens the untrained limb too, with zero hypertrophy on that side.
  • Roughly 8 percent of initial strength transfers, about half the gain of the trained side (Carroll et al., 2006).
  • Mechanism: motor commands spill over through pathways that cross at the spinal cord and brain.
  • Practical use: training the healthy limb can help defend strength on an injured or immobilized side.

Specificity: Strength as a Trainable Skill

  • Exercise-type : gains are largest in the movement pattern actually trained.
  • Joint-angle : strength improves most at the trained joint angles, with only partial carryover.
  • Velocity : gains are greatest near the trained speed, with limited carryover to very different speeds.
  • Heavy, well-executed early training is rehearsal for recruitment, firing rate, braking, and coordination all at once, a skill session dressed up as a strength session.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how the nervous system contributes to strength, not a license to diagnose or treat. Neural drive typically outpaces tendon and connective tissue adaptation, so persistent joint pain, sudden sharp pain, or discomfort that does not settle with rest are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician, not a shrug of "it's just neural." Work within your role, whatever it is.
