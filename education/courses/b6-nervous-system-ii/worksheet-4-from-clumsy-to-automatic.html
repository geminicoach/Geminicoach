Part B · Feedback and Its Fade

  1. Explain the guidance hypothesis. Why does frequent feedback often make performance look better during a practice session while making long-term learning worse?
  2. Describe how feedback should change as a learner moves from the cognitive stage, to the associative stage, to the autonomous stage. Be specific about frequency and how prescriptive it should be.
  3. What is the "over-coaching trap," and why can correcting a near-autonomous performer's mechanics in the moment actually hurt their execution rather than help it?

Part C · Practice Structure: Order and Spacing

  1. Distinguish blocked practice from random practice. Which one produces smoother performance during the session itself, and which one produces better retention on a test given days later?
  2. Distinguish massed practice from distributed practice, and explain in your own words the likely reason spacing sessions out helps learning stick.
  3. Explain why a true beginner in the cognitive stage might reasonably start with blocked, massed practice, and why that same person should shift toward random, distributed practice once the basic pattern is working.

Part D · Attentional Focus: Internal Versus External

  1. Define internal focus of attention and external focus of attention, and write one example cue of each for a skill of your choosing.
  2. Explain why an external focus tends to produce better performance and better learning than an internal focus does, according to the chapter.
  3. Why does automaticity free up a performer's attention, and what becomes possible for them once a skill no longer demands their full conscious attention? Give a concrete example.

Part E · Trace a Skill Through All Three Stages

Choose a skill you or someone you know is currently learning, or has learned, such as a sport, an instrument, a work task, or a language. Trace it through the framework the way the chapter traces the front squat.

  1. Cognitive stage: describe what early practice looked like, and what kind of feedback would have been appropriate at that point.
  2. Associative stage: describe what changed as the pattern refined, and how feedback should have shifted.
  3. Autonomous stage: describe what the skill looks like once it runs with little conscious attention, and how feedback should shift again.
  4. Practice structure: describe how you would structure practice differently at the beginning of this learning arc versus toward the end, referencing blocked, random, massed, and distributed practice.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds literacy about how skills are learned, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. The stage model explains ordinary skill acquisition; it was never built to explain a neurological event, a sudden tremor, a new asymmetry, or a loss of coordination with a medical cause, and stretching it to cover those situations does real harm. Treat anything that looks medical as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
