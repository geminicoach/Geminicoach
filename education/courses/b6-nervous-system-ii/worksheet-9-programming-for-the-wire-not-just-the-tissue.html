Part B · Training the Wire: Intensity, Intent, and the Ceiling of Recruitment

  1. In full sentences, distinguish what intensity does for recruitment from what intent does for rate coding. Why can a light load moved with genuine maximal intent still be a valuable neural stimulus?
  2. Explain the tradeoff between heavy grinding sets and fast submaximal work in terms of the central fatigue each one costs, and explain why a mature program deliberately uses both at different points rather than leaning on only one.
  3. A learner's program consists entirely of heavy sets taken to a hard grind, with nothing fast or light ever prescribed. Predict what is likely to happen to their rate coding and to their central fatigue over several weeks, and explain your reasoning.

Part C · Specificity, Skill Scheduling, and Exercise Order

  1. Explain the variability-to-specificity funnel: what should a program emphasize early in a cycle, what should it emphasize as the target date approaches, and why is running maximal variety right up to a peak considered a common, avoidable error?
  2. In your own words, explain how the same heavy-load template produces very different outcomes for a learner in the cognitive stage of motor learning versus a learner in the autonomous stage, and why matching practice to a learner's stage matters.
  3. State the exercise-order rule that follows from the finding that starting with smaller, single-joint movements reduced force output on a later, larger multijoint lift. Explain why this counts as specificity expressed as a scheduling decision, not simply a matter of habit or preference.

Part D · Managing Central Fatigue in Programming

  1. Distinguish central fatigue from peripheral fatigue in full sentences, and explain why soreness is an unreliable readout for whether the nervous system is ready for a heavy neural session.
  2. Distinguish a deload from a taper. What job does each one do, what should stay held while a taper cuts volume, and why does cutting the wrong variable undo the very adaptation the cycle was built to produce?
  3. In a full paragraph, explain why placing a technical or skill session directly behind a max-intent strength day predictably degrades that session's quality even when the tissue feels completely fine. Then describe how you would reorder a training week to avoid this problem.

Part E · Velocity as a Fatigue Readout

  1. Explain why the speed at which a fixed load moves is a more direct readout of central fatigue than soreness or general subjective feel.
  2. Describe two practical uses of velocity-based training from the chapter (for example, load-velocity profiling, session-to-session tracking, or velocity-loss thresholds within a set), and explain what decision each one helps a learner or the person guiding their training make.
  3. A learner's velocity on a fixed submaximal load has dropped over three straight sessions, even though the load has not changed and nothing feels sore. Explain what this pattern most likely indicates, and which programming lever from this chapter you would reach for first.

Part F · Honest Limits

  1. Name one thing about neural adaptation that this chapter says remains genuinely unsettled, and explain in your own words why a learner does not need that question resolved in order to program well.
  2. Explain the difference between a population-average number, such as roughly 85 percent of a one-repetition maximum (1RM) or a 60 to 90 percent volume cut for a taper, and one specific learner's actual optimum. How would a learner find their own number instead of simply looking one up?
  3. Explain why programming for the wire is not an argument for chasing maximal intent on every single set, and name what this chapter says still has to be respected regardless of how well the neural programming is done.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds literacy about how the nervous system and the muscular tissue are trained together, not a license to diagnose, treat, or replace a qualified coach or clinician, for yourself or anyone else. The models in this chapter explain ordinary training fatigue and ordinary programming decisions; they were never built to explain a sudden neurological event, an unexplained one-sided loss of function, or a persistent injury, and stretching them to cover those situations does real harm. Treat anything that looks medical as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
