Part A · Coordination as an Intermuscular Skill

Answer each question in full sentences.

  1. In your own words, explain what Nikolai Bernstein's degrees-of-freedom problem is, and why it makes a muscle-by-muscle command model implausible for real-time movement.
  2. Explain what a muscle synergy is, and describe how the frog-kicking research by d'Avella, Saltiel, and Bizzi (2003) supports the idea that the nervous system combines a small number of reusable synergies rather than commanding hundreds of muscles individually.
  3. Two throwers test with nearly identical grip strength, bench press, and jump numbers, yet one throws a ball far faster than the other. Using the idea that "you train patterns, not parts," explain why this is not a contradiction.

Part C · Muscle Synergies and Motor Programs

Reason through the two related but distinct ideas from the chapter.

  1. Distinguish a muscle synergy from Schmidt's generalized motor program (GMP). What question does each idea answer about how coordinated movement is produced and stored?
  2. Schmidt's schema theory separates invariant features of a movement, such as relative timing, from adjustable parameters, such as overall speed or size. Explain this distinction using a pitcher and a javelin thrower as your example.
  3. The chapter argues that coordination is "brutally task-specific." Explain what this means for someone who is beautifully coordinated on a familiar exercise but falls apart on a new movement that uses many of the same muscles.

Part D · The Kinetic Chain: Proximal-to-Distal Sequencing

Use the "whip" logic from the chapter to reason through timing, not just to define it.

  1. Describe proximal-to-distal sequencing in your own words, and explain why a proximal segment decelerating is not wasted motion but part of how energy is handed off to the next segment.
  2. Explain why firing every segment of a throw at once, in one simultaneous, all-out effort, produces less velocity than firing the same segments in sequence with real time between them.
  3. Owens, Coyles, and Khaiyat (2024) compared injured and uninjured overhead throwing athletes and found that most of the chain sequenced identically between the two groups, but one coupling had come apart in the injured group. Describe what that specific breakdown was, and explain what it suggests about the arm's role when the rest of the chain is not doing its job.
  4. Bezodis, Willwacher, and Salo (2019) found the same proximal-to-distal signature in the sprint start. Name the role biarticular muscles, such as the hamstrings and the calf muscle group, play in that chain.

Part E · Anticipatory Postural Adjustments and Movement Economy

Answer each question in full sentences.

  1. Define anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs) using the arm-raise example from the chapter: which muscles fire first, which muscle fires second, and why does the order matter for balance?
  2. Distinguish an anticipatory postural adjustment from a reactive balancing correction. Which one is feedforward and which one is feedback, and why is the feedback version always at least a little late by definition?
  3. Define co-contraction, and explain when it is a genuinely useful strategy versus when it becomes wasteful. Use Hogan's (1984) finding about joint stiffness and Armstrong, Deluzio, and Scott's (2025) finding about corrective responses in your answer.
  4. Brueckner, Kiss, and Muehlbauer (2018) found that raw muscle activity correlates only weakly with skill, while cleaner timing and reduced co-contraction reliably track it. Explain what this implies about what a coach or self-learner should actually be watching for as a marker of improving skill.
