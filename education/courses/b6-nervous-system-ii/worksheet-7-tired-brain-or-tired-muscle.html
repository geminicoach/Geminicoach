Part A · Central Nervous System (CNS) Fatigue vs. Peripheral (Muscular) Fatigue

Define each kind of fatigue in your own words, and explain what actually separates them.

  1. In two or three sentences, define central nervous system (CNS) fatigue. Where in the chain from cortex to muscle fiber does it originate, and what happens to the outgoing command?
  2. In two or three sentences, define peripheral (muscular) fatigue. Where does it originate, and what happens at and inside the muscle fiber itself?
  3. From the inside, a stalled rep caused by central fatigue and a stalled rep caused by peripheral fatigue can feel almost identical. Explain, in your own words, why that is true even though the two are fundamentally different events happening in different places.

Part B · How Each One Is Identified

Explain the reasoning behind the tools and signs the chapter uses to tell the two apart, in the lab and in ordinary training.

  1. Explain, conceptually, how the interpolated twitch technique tests for voluntary activation. What does a large superimposed twitch tell you about where a person's fatigue is located, and what does a small or absent twitch tell you instead?
  2. Perceived effort is not a direct readout of what is happening in the muscle. Explain where perceived effort actually comes from, and why blocking the sensory nerves that carry feedback from the muscle barely changes how hard an effort feels.
  3. Name two field signatures that point toward peripheral fatigue and two that point toward central fatigue, and for each one explain what it is evidence of, not just what it is called.

Part C · The Mechanisms Behind Each

Go one level deeper: explain the actual physiological mechanisms driving each kind of fatigue.

  1. Peripheral fatigue is driven by specific events at or inside the muscle fiber. Name at least one mechanism and explain, briefly, how it reduces the force the fiber can produce.
  2. Central fatigue is, in part, a protective decision made well below conscious control. Explain the role of Group III and IV muscle afferents in this process, and why the chapter describes this system as a governor rather than simply a weakness.
  3. Explain a second wind using the governor model described in the chapter. If the muscle's own chemistry has not meaningfully changed, what actually changed to let force return?

Part D · Why Localization Changes the Recovery Decision

Use the "if it is peripheral, if it is central" logic from the chapter to reason through each situation.

  1. A learner's working muscle is pumped, burning, and visibly fatigued after a high-volume session, and the force loss tracks the local metabolic work. Which kind of fatigue is this, and what does the recovery response actually need to address?
  2. A learner's warm-ups and submaximal sets felt crisp the entire session, but a single attempt at the top of the range stalled, and a short break partially restores the lost performance. Which kind of fatigue is this, and what does the recovery response actually need to address?
  3. Explain, in your own words, why treating central fatigue as if it were peripheral wastes recovery time in one direction, while treating peripheral fatigue as if it were central risks digging a deeper hole in the other.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds understanding of how central and peripheral fatigue work, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain the mechanism, and treat anything that looks like a persistent, unresolving pattern, disrupted sleep, flattening mood, or unexplained tiredness that will not lift, as warranting evaluation by a qualified professional. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
