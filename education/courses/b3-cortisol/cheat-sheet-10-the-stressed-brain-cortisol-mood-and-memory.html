Cheat-Sheet · B3 Cortisol

Chapter 10 : The Stressed Brain

One idea from four angles: cortisol is a signal the brain both reads and is remodeled by. Adaptive in brief pulses, corrosive when chronic.

Two Receptors, Two Regimes

Mineralocorticoid receptor (MR)High affinity; occupied even at low basal cortisol. Maintains ongoing tone and excitability of limbic circuits during the quiet hours.
Glucocorticoid receptor (GR)Roughly tenfold lower affinity; recruited only when cortisol rises, at the peak and above all during stress. Mounts the response, consolidates, then helps restore balance.
MR/GR balanceAn inverted U: too little and too much are both harmful. The balance, not cortisol in the abstract, decides adaptive versus corrosive.
Two clocksSlow genomic actions (minutes to hours, durable) plus fast non-genomic actions (seconds to minutes, priming). Complementary, not competing.

Three Regions, Chronic Stress

  • Hippocampus : declarative and contextual memory, plus a brake on the axis. Chronic stress: dendritic atrophy, suppressed neurogenesis. It retreats.
  • Amygdala : threat detection and fear learning. Chronic stress: dendritic growth, heightened reactivity. It grows.
  • Prefrontal cortex (PFC) : executive control, working memory, top-down regulation of the amygdala. Chronic stress: dendritic retraction. It weakens.

Acute Versus Chronic

  • Acute pulse : selectively enhances memory for emotionally arousing material, a salience filter, not a blanket boost (Buchanan & Lovallo).
  • Mechanism : conditional on arousal; mediated by increased hippocampal subregional connectivity (Sherman et al.).
  • Chronic flood : months of GR occupancy remodel and impair. The load-bearing word is "chronic," not "cortisol."
  • BDNF : brain-derived neurotrophic factor, the growth signal the hippocampus needs, is attenuated by sustained cortisol (Suri & Vaidya).

The Brake That Fails

  • The hippocampus is GR-dense, so it is a prime cortisol sensor and its output restrains corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) drive. A healthy hippocampus pushes the axis toward "off."
  • Chronic cortisol degrades it, so the brake weakens, feedback loosens, and cortisol drifts higher, damaging it further.
  • A sensor that is also a target: negative feedback (Chapter 2) becomes a self-reinforcing loop (Sapolsky; Lei et al.).

Inflammation and Mood

  • Sickness behavior : adaptive short-term withdrawal; prolonged, in vulnerable people, it can shade into depressive symptoms (Dantzer et al.).
  • Glucocorticoid resistance : immune cells stop hearing cortisol, so inflammation persists even while cortisol is high.
  • The hippocampus is the node where feedback, inflammation, and mood intersect (Lei et al.).

What We Can and Cannot Infer

  • Directions of the effects are robust across species and methods. Magnitude, timing, and reversibility vary, and some causal arrows stay open (Sapolsky).
  • These are associations and mechanisms, not a diagnostic algorithm you can run on a person. A plausible mechanism is not a clinical verdict.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how cortisol reshapes the brain, not a license to diagnose or treat. Diagnosing depression or anxiety is a clinical matter for qualified mental-health professionals. Persistent low mood, loss of pleasure, marked cognitive decline, or any request to interpret a hormone result or start treatment warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Explain accurately, recognize when evaluation is warranted, and refer. Work within your role, whatever it is.
