Part A · Exercise as an Adaptive Stressor

A hard training session is, by every biochemical measure, a stressor: it raises cortisol, recruits the sympathoadrenal system, and tilts muscle toward breakdown. Yet nobody calls a squat session bad for you.

  1. Explain why the pulse of cortisol around a genuinely demanding session is better understood as the signal that triggers adaptation than as damage to be minimized.
  2. The chapter says a healthy trained athlete mounts a larger cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) response to a stimulation test than a sedentary person does. Explain what that finding tells you about whether "high cortisol" is automatically harmful.
  3. There is a rough intensity threshold below which the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis barely engages. Why does that threshold matter when someone worries that all exercise "spikes" their cortisol?

Part B · Fitness Is Built in Recovery

During the session you are net catabolic. The rebound to a capacity slightly above where you started, supercompensation, happens afterward.

  1. In your own words, explain the claim that the workout does not deposit fitness but requests it, and that whether the request is granted depends on what happens next.
  2. Muscle protein synthesis stays elevated for roughly 24 to 48 hours after a resistance bout. Explain why "recovered enough to train again" and "returned to hormonal baseline" are not the same claim, and why that gap matters for training frequency.

Part C · The Load-Recovery Balance

Use the fitness-fatigue model and the allostatic-load framework from the chapter.

  1. The fitness-fatigue model says measured performance is roughly fitness minus fatigue. Explain what goes wrong when a new hard session is stacked on top before the previous fatigue has cleared.
  2. The chapter calls training a controlled experiment in allostatic load, with the load-to-recovery ratio as the variable the athlete sets. Name two recovery levers that sit between sessions and explain why they often matter more than the choice of exercise itself.

Part E · DHEA and the Cortisol-to-DHEA Ratio

Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) is the adrenal androgen made on the same steroidogenic pathway as cortisol and is often cast as cortisol's anabolic counterweight.

  1. Cortisol and DHEA share a production line and rise together during hard exercise. Explain why their ratio, read over days to weeks, can carry information that neither hormone carries on its own.
  2. The cortisol-to-DHEA ratio is presented as a promising research marker of anabolic-catabolic balance, not a validated diagnostic test with clean cutoffs. Explain why it earns its keep only as a trend over time and never as a verdict from a single morning reading.
  3. A single resting cortisol value, high or low, is called close to the least informative measurement for judging training state. Explain what would be more informative, and why that more informative measurement sits closer to clinical work than to a mail-order panel.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds understanding of training physiology and monitoring concepts, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe a training program, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works and describe how load and recovery can be tracked, and treat anything that looks clinical (persistent fatigue, mood disturbance, unexplained loss of muscle or performance that does not lift with rest) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: recognize a pattern and refer, rather than diagnosing a syndrome or prescribing a plan.
