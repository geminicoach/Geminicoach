Cortisol After Dark: Sleep and the Circadian Loop

How the nighttime trough and deep sleep hold each other up, and what happens when the loop breaks.

60 min read 8 cited sources

It is 2:14 a.m. You have been horizontal for three hours. Your body is heavy, your eyes ache, and every cell of you wants sleep, yet your mind is racing, your heart feels slightly too fast, and some low electrical hum keeps you just above the threshold of unconsciousness. You are exhausted. You are also, somehow, alert. This paradoxical state, tired but wired, is one of the most common complaints in the modern world, and it is not a psychological quirk. It is a signature. It is what an out-of-place cortisol curve feels like from the inside.

Back in Chapter 3 we drew the diurnal rhythm, the sharp morning peak, the long slide across the day, the deep nighttime trough, and we treated it mostly as a fact of physiology to be described. This chapter closes that loop. We will show that the rhythm is not merely correlated with when you sleep; it is mechanically fused to it. Sleep and cortisol regulate each other, minute by minute, in a reciprocal circuit that can run beautifully or spiral into dysfunction. Once you see the circuit clearly, the 2 a.m. paradox stops being mysterious. It becomes readable, which is a very different thing from being controllable, and keeping those two apart is one of the jobs of this chapter.

The reason this matters beyond the merely intellectual is that sleep sits at the crossing point of nearly everything we have studied so far. In Chapter 5 we built the language of allostatic load, the cumulative wear that accrues when the stress response runs too often or fails to shut off. In Chapter 6 we followed cortisol into metabolism, watching it push glucose into the blood, antagonize insulin, and, when chronic, tilt a body toward central adiposity and insulin resistance. Sleep is the hinge on which both of those chapters turn. A properly low nighttime cortisol is not idle downtime; it is a load-bearing pillar. Pull it out and the systems we studied one at a time begin to fail together.

To keep the physiology anchored to something human, we will follow one learner across the chapter. Beatrice is 38, a hospital pharmacist who rotates onto a week of night shifts every month and spends the rest of her weeks trying to sleep on a normal schedule. She enrolled in this course because she has been living the 2 a.m. state for the better part of a year and wants to understand what is happening inside her before she decides what, if anything, to do about it. Beatrice is not a patient here and she is not a puzzle for us to diagnose. She is a thread, a way to keep the mechanisms honest by asking, at each turn, what they would predict for a real person whose clock and calendar rarely agree. We will be careful, all the way through, about the difference between understanding her situation and presuming to manage it.

Revisiting the diurnal curve, this time as a two-way street

Recall the basic waveform. Circulating cortisol reaches its nadir in the first half of the night, begins climbing in the pre-dawn hours, and peaks 30 to 45 minutes after awakening in what we called the cortisol awakening response. From that morning high it declines across the day, punctuated by ultradian pulses roughly every 60 to 90 minutes, until it bottoms out again the following night. In Chapter 3 the driver of this rhythm was the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the hypothalamic master clock entrained by light, projecting through a multisynaptic pathway to the adrenal cortex.

That account is correct but incomplete. It treats the sleep-wake cycle as a downstream consequence of the clock. The SCN sets the time, cortisol follows, and sleep happens to occur during the trough. The reality is bidirectional. Oster and colleagues (2017), in their comprehensive Endocrine Reviews synthesis, describe the 24-hour glucocorticoid waveform as the product of at least three interacting inputs: the SCN-driven circadian program, the ultradian pulse generator, and the acute modulation imposed by sleep and behavior themselves. Sleep is not a passenger on the cortisol curve. It is one of the hands on the wheel. That single reframing is what turns a descriptive rhythm into a system with feedback, and a system with feedback is a system that can be pushed off its rails and hold itself there.

The evidence for this is elegant and old. Buckley and Schatzberg (2005), reviewing normal HPA physiology in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, catalog the moment-to-moment couplings: the onset of sleep, specifically slow-wave sleep, actively inhibits cortisol secretion, while awakenings and the transition out of sleep stimulate it. Balbo and colleagues (2010) put it plainly: sleep onset is associated with a short-term inhibition of cortisol release, whereas sleep offset and nocturnal awakenings trigger pulses. If you shift a person's sleep period by several hours, the cortisol rhythm partially follows the sleep, not just the clock. The waveform we memorized is a negotiated settlement between the SCN and behavior, and behavior gets a vote.

It is worth being precise about how large that behavioral vote is, because it is easy to hear "behavior gets a vote" and imagine that willpower can simply move the curve. It cannot. The SCN sets the ceiling and floor; the behavioral inputs modulate the shape within those bounds and, over days, can drag the whole pattern. Think of the clock as the composer and sleep as the conductor. The conductor cannot rewrite the symphony, but a conductor who insists on playing the finale during the overture will produce something that sounds badly wrong even though every note is technically in the score. That is what a mistimed sleep schedule does to an intact cortisol rhythm. Nothing is broken at the level of the molecule. The arrangement is simply being played against the clock.

For Beatrice, this two-way street is the whole story in miniature. On her aligned weeks, her SCN and her behavior agree: she sleeps when cortisol is low and wakes into its rise. On her night weeks, her behavior is forcibly moved twelve hours while her SCN, entrained by the daylight she still cannot avoid, stays roughly where it was. The result is not that her cortisol "goes high" in some vague, marketing sense. It is that her cortisol keeps playing the morning movement at what her body is trying to make its bedtime. She is not producing a toxin. She is producing the right signal at the wrong hour, which, as we will see, is its own distinct kind of harm.

The nighttime trough and the architecture of deep sleep

Why does it matter, functionally, that cortisol bottoms out at night? Because the trough is permissive. Low glucocorticoid tone is a precondition for the sleep stage that does the body's heaviest restorative work: slow-wave sleep (SWS), the deep, delta-dominated stage concentrated in the first third of the night, precisely the window in which cortisol is lowest.

The relationship is causal in both directions and can be demonstrated experimentally. Buckley and Schatzberg (2005) detail the mechanisms by which cortisol, and its upstream driver corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), suppress slow-wave sleep. CRH is not merely a hormone-release signal; centrally, it is an alerting peptide. Infusing CRH or cortisol into healthy sleepers shifts sleep architecture away from deep SWS and toward lighter, more fragmented stages and increased wakefulness. Conversely, when the SWS-rich early night proceeds normally, it holds cortisol down. The trough and the deep sleep are not two things that happen to coincide; they are two faces of the same physiological state, a quiescent HPA axis and a quiescent, restorative brain.

This is why the direction of the morning rise makes teleological sense. The pre-waking climb in cortisol is not an accident of the clock; it is, in part, a wake-up signal. Rising CRH and cortisol help lighten sleep, mobilize glucose, and prepare the cardiovascular system for the upright, active day. A well-formed diurnal curve is therefore doing two jobs at once: its low nighttime phase licenses deep sleep, and its rising morning phase ends it. Sleep and cortisol are running the same daily program from opposite ends, one saying "now you may go deep" and the other, hours later, saying "now it is time to surface."

Notice how much this reframes the popular advice to simply "lower your cortisol" for better sleep. The nighttime trough is not low because low is intrinsically good. It is low because a specific, restorative brain state requires it. And the morning peak is not high because high is intrinsically bad. It is high because ending sleep and launching the day requires it. The health of the curve lives in its shape and timing, exactly the lesson of Chapter 1 and Chapter 3, not in some overall smallness of the number. A person whose cortisol were pathologically low around the clock would not sleep like a champion. They would be unable to end sleep, unable to mobilize morning fuel, and, in the extreme of adrenal insufficiency, gravely ill. The trough earns its meaning only against the peak.

It also helps to remember what the deep sleep the trough protects is actually for, because that is what raises the stakes. Slow-wave sleep is not merely rest. It is the stage in which the brain consolidates certain forms of memory, in which the largest nightly pulse of growth hormone is released, and in which the brain's clearance systems are thought to be most active. When elevated evening cortisol shrinks SWS, it is not shaving a luxury off the night. It is cutting into the specific window in which some of the body's most important overnight maintenance is scheduled to happen. This is part of why the downstream consequences of chronically fragmented deep sleep reach so far, touching memory, metabolism, and immune regulation at once. The trough does not merely permit a pleasant depth of sleep; it protects the hour in which a great deal of the body's repair work is booked, and cortisol arriving early is the appointment being cancelled.

What "permissive" means in practice

Consider the worked contrast. In a healthy young sleeper, evening cortisol falls to near-undetectable levels around habitual bedtime; SWS dominates the first two cycles; nocturnal awakenings are brief and cortisol stays suppressed through them. Now hold everything else constant and lift evening cortisol, through stress, a late intense workout, or an infusion in the lab. SWS shrinks. Awakenings lengthen. The sleeper spends more of the night in the shallow stages from which it is easy to surface into full wakefulness.

Nothing about the person's "sleepiness" has changed. The homeostatic drive to sleep, the pressure that builds with every waking hour, is fully intact. What has changed is that the permissive hormonal environment has been withdrawn. The brakes on deep sleep are being held down by a signal that should not be present at that hour. That is the physiology of lying in bed exhausted and unable to drop, and it is worth sitting with, because it explains why the intuitive fixes so often fail. Lying there longer does not help, because the problem is not insufficient sleep pressure. Trying harder does not help, because effort is itself arousing. The only thing that resolves it is the withdrawal of the inappropriate cortisol and CRH tone, and that is a matter of physiology and timing, not of grit.

This is also the point where the boundary of this course starts to matter, so let us name it early rather than late. Recognizing that elevated evening cortisol degrades deep sleep is an educational insight of real value. It lets you understand why a stressful stretch wrecks your nights, and why the wreckage compounds. What it does not do is license anyone to conclude that their own bad nights are caused by "high cortisol," to test for it, or to treat it. Those are clinical acts. We will return to this line explicitly at the end of the chapter, but it is already visible here, running just beneath the mechanism.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common belief holds that if you feel tired, your body must be low on cortisol, so a "boost" should help, or, in the opposite camp, that any sleeplessness means cortisol is "too high" and must be crushed. Both miss the point. The 2 a.m. state is not about the total amount of cortisol at all. It is about a normal morning signal appearing at a nighttime hour, when the trough should be permitting deep sleep. The target is a correctly timed rhythm, never a bigger or smaller number for its own sake.

The other direction: sleep loss raises cortisol

We have established that cortisol shapes sleep. The reverse arrow, that disturbed or insufficient sleep raises cortisol, is what closes the loop into a true circuit, and it is supported by two of the most influential experiments in the field.

The first is Leproult, Copinschi, Buxton, and Van Cauter (1997). They subjected healthy young men to acute partial and total sleep deprivation and tracked 32-hour cortisol profiles. The finding was subtle but mechanistically decisive: sleep loss did not spike cortisol randomly, it delayed the axis's recovery from its early-morning circadian stimulation. The consequence appeared later. Cortisol levels the following evening were elevated, and the rate of decline from the daytime peak was slowed. The authors interpreted this as an impairment of the negative glucocorticoid feedback loop: a night of short sleep leaves the HPA axis less able to shut itself off the next day. In the language of the previous chapters, sleep loss blunts the "off switch."

Now trace the implication, because this is the exact point at which two separate phenomena become a single circuit. Elevated evening cortisol is precisely the perturbation we said suppresses slow-wave sleep. So one bad night raises the next evening's cortisol; that cortisol degrades the next night's deep sleep; that degraded sleep raises the following evening's cortisol again. Leproult's study provides the missing arrow that turns two independent facts, "cortisol harms sleep" and "sleep loss raises cortisol," into a self-reinforcing loop with no natural stopping point. The loop does not need a villain to keep spinning. It only needs one push and the absence of a full recovery night to reset it.

It is also worth appreciating why the field found this result surprising at the time. The intuitive expectation was that sleep deprivation would raise cortisol during the deprivation, as an acute stress response, and then normalize. What Leproult and colleagues showed was subtler and more troubling: the axis takes the hit quietly during the lost night and pays it out the following evening, when the person is trying to wind down again. The damage is time-shifted, and the time it shifts to is the worst possible one for the next night's sleep. That detail, evening rather than morning elevation, is what makes the loop so hard to break by willpower alone.

From acute to chronic: the sleep-debt experiment

Spiegel, Leproult, and Van Cauter (1999) took the acute finding into the domain of chronic restriction, the domain most people actually live in. Eleven healthy young men were restricted to four hours of sleep per night for six consecutive nights, then allowed twelve hours for six recovery nights. The results, published in The Lancet, were striking: during the sleep-debt period, glucose tolerance deteriorated markedly, thyrotropin concentrations fell, sympathovagal balance shifted toward sympathetic dominance, and the evening cortisol pattern was altered in the now-familiar direction, elevated when it should be falling. The authors were the first to frame the metabolic reading explicitly: sleep debt, they argued, could increase allostatic load and facilitate the development of obesity, insulin resistance, and hypertension.

This is the hinge that connects the present chapter to Chapter 6. There we described how chronically elevated cortisol drives gluconeogenesis, promotes central adiposity, and antagonizes insulin. Spiegel's study shows that sleep restriction alone reproduces a slice of that picture, and it does so partly through the cortisol pathway we have been tracing. Poor sleep and elevated cortisol are not two separate roads to metabolic disease. They are the same road, traveled in both directions. A person accumulating sleep debt is, without doing anything else wrong, nudging their glucose handling in the direction we spent an entire chapter warning about.

The scale of the effect in that study deserves emphasis because it is easy to underestimate. These were young, healthy men, and only six nights of restriction pushed their glucose tolerance measurably toward the impaired range. The subjects were not sick, not old, not sedentary, and not eating badly for the experiment. The only variable was sleep. If a week of short nights can do that to the most metabolically forgiving population imaginable, the implication for a person living under chronic restriction, a new parent, a caregiver, a rotating shift worker like Beatrice, is not reassuring. And crucially, the study also showed recovery: with extended sleep, the derangements eased. The loop is reversible in principle, which is exactly why sleep is such a high-leverage node, and exactly why deciding what to do about a given person's sleep belongs to someone qualified to weigh their whole situation.

Insomnia as hyperarousal: which came first?

If the loop is real, then chronic insomnia should look like a state of persistent HPA activation, not just a state of "not sleeping." Vgontzas and colleagues (2001) tested this directly. They compared 24-hour ACTH and cortisol secretion in patients with chronic insomnia against matched normal sleepers. Across the full nyctohemeral cycle, the complete day-and-night period, insomniacs secreted more ACTH and more cortisol. The excess was not evenly distributed: the largest elevations appeared in the evening and the first half of the night, exactly the window in which cortisol should be at its nadir and deep sleep should be at its height.

Two further details make the study pivotal. First, there was a dose-response relationship: the insomniacs with the greatest objective sleep disturbance secreted the most cortisol. That gradient matters, because a dose-response relationship is one of the classic fingerprints of a causal link rather than a coincidence. Second, and most provocatively, the authors argued that this pattern is consistent with a disorder of central nervous system hyperarousal, and that HPA overactivity may be not merely a consequence of poor sleep but a partial cause of it. Buckley and Schatzberg (2005) reach a compatible conclusion, suggesting HPA hyperactivity may be causally upstream of insomnia in at least a subset of patients.

This reframes the clinical picture in a way worth pausing on. The lay model of insomnia is "I can't sleep, so I'm stressed." The physiology suggests the arrow often runs the other way as well: a hyperactive stress axis raises the arousal floor so high that sleep cannot consolidate, which further activates the axis. The tired-but-wired sensation from our opening is the subjective correlate of a brain sitting in an evening cortisol bath it should not be in. It is not that the insomniac is failing to relax. It is that the neuroendocrine setpoint for arousal has drifted upward, and relaxation, in the ordinary sense, cannot pull it back down on its own.

Note carefully what this means for the learner's role, because it is the crux of how this material is meant to be used. Recognizing this pattern is educational insight; it is not a diagnosis. Chronic insomnia is a defined clinical condition, and so is shift-work disorder. The appropriate move, when a real person's sleep looks like this, is to understand the physiology and refer, never to label or to treat. Beatrice, reading this section, might well recognize herself in the description of evening hyperarousal. The right conclusion for her to draw is not "I have hyperarousal insomnia and here is what to take." It is "this is a coherent, well-studied pattern, and it is worth bringing to a clinician who can actually evaluate it." Those are very different sentences, and the whole ethic of this course lives in the gap between them.

When the clock and the calendar disagree: circadian desynchrony

So far the perturbations have been about the amount and quality of sleep. Shift work and jet lag introduce a different and arguably more severe insult: they attack the timing. In these conditions the SCN and its cortisol output remain on their old schedule while behavior, sleeping, eating, working, and light exposure, is forcibly moved. The internal clock and the external calendar fall out of register. This is circadian misalignment, and its consequences have been quantified with unusual precision.

The landmark demonstration is Scheer, Hilton, Mantzoros, and Shea (2009), a forced-desynchrony protocol published in PNAS. Subjects were placed on a schedule in which their sleep and meals occurred roughly twelve hours out of phase from their habitual times, a controlled laboratory model of the disorientation of night-shift rotation or a transmeridian flight halfway around the world. The results were dramatic. Circadian misalignment completely reversed the cortisol rhythm. It raised postprandial glucose by about 6% and insulin by roughly 22%, indicating a sharp drop in insulin sensitivity. It decreased leptin by about 17%, a change that would be expected to increase hunger. Mean arterial pressure rose, and sleep efficiency fell by about 20%. Three of the subjects, the authors noted, exhibited postprandial glucose responses in the range considered pre-diabetic, after only ten days of misalignment.

The crucial methodological point is what the forced-desynchrony design isolated. Because the protocol controlled sleep duration, these metabolic derangements were attributable to misalignment itself, largely independent of sleep loss. In other words: even if a shift worker sleeps a full eight hours, the fact that those hours fall at the biologically wrong time inflicts measurable metabolic and cardiovascular cost. Timing is not a minor variable layered on top of quantity. It is an independent axis of harm. This is the single most important thing for a rotating worker to understand, and it is precisely the thing the culture around shift work tends to miss. "Just get your eight hours" is well meant and incomplete, because eight hours taken at noon are not metabolically equivalent to eight hours taken at night.

For Beatrice, this study is close to a description of her monthly reality. When she rotates onto nights, she does not simply lose sleep, though she often does that too. She eats her main meal at 3 a.m., when her liver's clock has designated the period for fasting. She receives her body's morning cortisol pulse while she is trying to sleep in a darkened room at 9 a.m. Her insulin response to that 3 a.m. meal is measurably blunted, exactly as Scheer predicts. None of this requires that she is doing anything careless. The insult is structural. Her tissues are receiving correct signals delivered on a schedule that no longer matches the world she is living in.

Cortisol as a zeitgeber, and why re-entrainment is slow

Oster and colleagues (2017) add the mechanistic why. The SCN does not directly run every clock in the body; it coordinates a hierarchy of peripheral clocks in the liver, pancreas, adipose tissue, and muscle. And glucocorticoids are one of the master's chief messengers to the periphery. Cortisol itself acts as a zeitgeber, a time-giver, resetting peripheral clocks to match the SCN. When behavior shifts abruptly, the light-entrained SCN moves slowly, roughly an hour per day, and the cortisol signal it sends is now in conflict with the timing signals coming from meals and activity. The periphery receives contradictory instructions. Metabolic tissues that expected the "morning" cortisol pulse to prime them for glucose disposal instead receive it while the person is trying to sleep, and receive their food at a time the liver's clock has designated for fasting. Desynchrony, in this view, is not one clock being wrong. It is many clocks disagreeing.

This is why jet lag lingers and why rotating shift workers rarely fully adapt. Re-entrainment is limited by the slow pace at which the SCN can be dragged by light, and by the fact that peripheral clocks re-entrain at different rates. For several days after a large phase shift, the body operates as an orchestra whose sections are playing the same piece at different bars. And here is the cruel arithmetic for someone on Beatrice's schedule: if the internal clock moves only about an hour a day, a twelve-hour reversal would take on the order of a week and a half to complete, which is longer than her night rotation lasts. By the time her body has made real progress toward the night schedule, she is rotating back to days, and the slow drag begins again in the opposite direction. She spends much of every month in transit between two schedules and fully resident in neither. That is not a failure of effort. It is the predictable output of a system that re-entrains far more slowly than a modern roster demands.

The differing re-entrainment rates of the peripheral clocks add a layer that is easy to overlook and important to name. It is not merely that the whole body lags the calendar by some uniform amount. Different organs catch up at different speeds. The SCN, driven by light, is relatively responsive; the liver, driven heavily by meal timing, can be dragged faster still if feeding shifts, while other tissues lag. So during the days after a large shift, the internal state is not a single clock reading the wrong hour but a spread of clocks reading a range of hours. Glucose handling, alertness, and cortisol timing can each be at a different point in their journey to the new schedule. This internal spread, sometimes called internal desynchrony, is a plausible part of why the misaligned state feels so uniquely bad and why its metabolic costs are so stubborn: there is no single hour the body can be told to adopt, because its parts are not even agreeing with each other, let alone with the world outside.

The tired-but-wired loop, drawn as a whole

We can now assemble the full circuit that opened the chapter. Start anywhere on the ring, because the loop has no privileged entry point, which is exactly what makes it self-sustaining.

A stressful day leaves evening cortisol elevated. Elevated evening cortisol and central CRH tone raise the arousal floor and suppress slow-wave sleep, so the night is shallow and fragmented (Buckley & Schatzberg, 2005; Balbo et al., 2010). Fragmented, insufficient sleep impairs the next day's negative feedback, so cortisol recovers slowly and is again elevated the following evening (Leproult et al., 1997). That evening's elevation degrades the next night, and around it goes. Layer chronic insomnia's baseline hyperarousal on top (Vgontzas et al., 2001), or a shift-work schedule that keeps the cortisol rhythm reversed (Scheer et al., 2009), and the loop acquires a floor it cannot easily fall below.

The subjective experience of standing inside this loop is precisely "tired but wired." The homeostatic sleep drive, the pressure to sleep that builds with hours awake, is high, hence tired. But the HPA and arousal system is running an evening program that belongs to the morning, hence wired. Two systems that should agree about the time of day are giving opposite reports, and the person lies in bed as the disputed territory. This is why the sensation is so distinctive and so miserable. It is not ordinary tiredness, which resolves into sleep, and it is not ordinary alertness, which feels like energy. It is the collision of the two, and the collision is legible on a hormone assay as an evening value where a trough should be.

Worked example: three nights of Beatrice's rotation

Run Beatrice's night week through the machinery we have built. Night 1: she works through the biological night; her SCN-driven cortisol trough occurs while she is awake and active, and her attempt to sleep the next morning coincides with the pre-programmed cortisol rise, so sleep is short and light and efficiency is low (Scheer et al., 2009). Day 2 evening: the previous morning's truncated sleep has blunted her feedback, so as she arrives at work her cortisol, instead of being suppressed for a coming sleep window, is running high (Leproult et al., 1997). Night 3: her glucose tolerance during her 3 a.m. meal is measurably worse, the misaligned insulin response predicted by the desynchrony literature (Spiegel et al., 1999; Scheer et al., 2009), and her daytime sleep remains fragmented.

Nothing in that sequence requires that Beatrice is "not trying" to sleep or "eating badly." The insult is structural: her clocks disagree, and cortisol is the loudest voice in the argument. This is the payoff of treating the relationship as a loop rather than a one-way arrow. If you believed sleep loss were the only problem, you would tell her to sleep more, and she would fail, because the timing is wrong regardless of duration. If you believed cortisol were the only problem, you would tell her to lower it, and she would fail, because the amount is not the issue and, in any case, blunting a needed signal carries its own risks. Seeing the whole ring is what keeps the understanding honest, and it is also what makes clear that the genuinely useful interventions for someone like her are matters for a qualified clinician who can weigh her roster, her history, and her risks together.

Sleep as allostatic load, cause and symptom at once

The final integrative move belongs to Bruce McEwen, the originator of the allostatic-load framework we met in earlier chapters. In his 2006 Metabolism review, McEwen argues that chronic sleep deprivation should be classified not as a lifestyle inconvenience but as a genuine neurobiologic and physiologic stressor, a source of allostatic load in its own right. The evidence he assembles is the by-now-familiar constellation: chronic sleep deprivation raises evening cortisol, elevates proinflammatory cytokines, increases blood glucose and insulin, and shifts autonomic balance toward sympathetic dominance. These are not transient adjustments. They are wear-and-tear that accumulates across the brain, immune system, and metabolic organs.

The conceptual payoff is that sleep disruption sits on both sides of the allostatic ledger. It is a cause of load: poor sleep pushes cortisol, glucose, and inflammation in the damaging direction. And it is a symptom of load: a stress-activated HPA axis fragments sleep in the first place. This dual position is exactly what we should expect of a node embedded in a reciprocal loop. It also explains why sleep is such a high-leverage target. Intervening on it can, in principle, damp the entire circuit, whereas ignoring it lets every other stressor compound. A person who fixes nothing but their sleep timing may find that their glucose, their mood, and their stress reactivity all ease, not because sleep is magic, but because it sits at the junction where several of the systems we studied separately actually meet.

This connects directly back to the allostatic-load framework of Chapter 5. There we described load as the price the body pays for repeated or inefficient stress responses, and we distinguished a healthy response that switches cleanly on and off from a dysregulated one that does not. Chronic sleep disruption is a near-perfect example of the dysregulated case. The nightly recovery that should reset the system is precisely what is being lost, so the load never gets discharged. Each night that fails to restore becomes a small deposit in an account that compounds. Over months and years, that is how a rotating schedule or a chronic insomnia turns from a nightly nuisance into a genuine, measurable risk to metabolic and cardiovascular health.

There is a reason to state this plainly rather than gently. The cultural framing of poor sleep as a matter of discipline or productivity, something to be optimized with a better morning routine or apologized for as a personal shortcoming, badly understates what the evidence actually shows. McEwen's argument is that sleep loss belongs in the same category as other genuine physiologic stressors, with measurable endocrine, inflammatory, autonomic, and metabolic signatures. That is not a motivational point about feeling your best. It is a claim about wear on the body's regulatory systems, the same wear we tracked through the metabolic and immune chapters. Reframing chronic sleep disruption as allostatic load rather than lifestyle failing does two useful things at once. It removes the moralizing that makes people hide the problem, and it correctly locates the seriousness of it, which is exactly the combination that makes a person more likely to bring it to someone qualified to help rather than to a supplement aisle.

Which is the real lesson of this chapter, and the reason it is placed where it is in the course. In Chapters 1 through 8 we studied the HPA axis, glucocorticoid feedback, metabolic downstream effects, and allostatic load as if they were separable topics. They are not. Sleep is the tissue that binds them into one network. A properly low nighttime cortisol is not passive downtime waiting for the day to resume. It is a load-bearing pillar of the entire regulatory architecture. Remove it, and the systems we studied one at a time begin to fail together, which is both the alarming reading and, inverted, the hopeful one: support that one pillar and you support a great deal at once.

Beatrice and the borrowed conclusion

Case

Beatrice finished the chapter, recognized her own nights in nearly every section, and did what a careful person does: she started to draw conclusions. She read that evening hyperarousal drives fragmented sleep, that shift work reverses the cortisol rhythm, that misalignment worsens glucose independent of sleep duration. She ordered an at-home saliva cortisol kit, took a sample one evening after a night shift, saw a value her kit flagged as high, and arrived at the next session ready to announce that she had "hyperarousal insomnia from cortisol dysregulation" and to ask which supplement would lower it.

Run Beatrice's situation through everything the chapter has established, and most of her conclusion is built from real bricks laid in the wrong order. The physiology she absorbed is sound. Evening hyperarousal is a documented pattern (Vgontzas et al., 2001). Shift work does reverse the cortisol rhythm and worsen metabolism (Scheer et al., 2009). Her nights genuinely do look like the loop we drew. What she got wrong was not the mechanism. It was the leap from "this mechanism exists and I seem to fit it" to "therefore this is my diagnosis and here is my treatment." Those last two steps are not education. They are clinical acts, and they require training, appropriate testing, and the full context of her history that neither a chapter nor a kit can supply.

Start with the evening saliva value she found alarming. A single reading, taken after a night shift, on a schedule that is by definition misaligned, cannot support a diagnosis. It cannot even be interpreted cleanly, because the whole point of the desynchrony literature is that her cortisol timing is scrambled relative to the clock the reference range assumes. Her "high evening value" might reflect exactly the reversed rhythm the chapter predicts, or the acute stress of the shift, or the timing of her waking, or ordinary variability. Distinguishing among those is not something a course, a kit, or a forum can do. It is the kind of judgment that a clinician with her roster, her symptoms, and proper repeated testing might begin to make. One undated evening number, drawn during a misaligned week, is close to the least interpretable measurement she could have chosen.

So what did the chapter actually give her, if not a diagnosis? Something sturdier. She now understands why her night weeks feel the way they do, that the misery is structural rather than a personal failing, that duration and timing are separate problems, and that the loop is real and reversible in principle. That understanding lets her walk into a clinician's office with good questions instead of a borrowed verdict, and it inoculates her against the sellers who would happily confirm her self-diagnosis and take her money for a product aimed at a number. The deliverable is not a smaller cortisol value. It is a person who is much harder to frighten and much harder to sell to, precisely because she knows where her own understanding ends.

That last phrase is the one to hold onto, because it is the whole ethic of the course compressed into a sentence. Beatrice's error was not stupidity; it was the natural overreach of a sharp learner who has just acquired a powerful model. The model is genuinely powerful. It explains her nights. It just does not authorize her to diagnose or treat them, and knowing that boundary is not a limitation on what she learned. It is the most professional thing she carries out of the chapter.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Think About It

A colleague shows you an evening cortisol result from a home kit and asks whether it means their shift work has "broken" their hormones. Using only this chapter, what can you honestly explain to them about timing and the loop, and where exactly does your answer have to stop? The place it stops is the boundary this section is about.

Sleep loss is a chronic stressor to which the brain and body adapt in ways that carry a hidden cost, the wear and tear of allostatic load.

Adapted from McEwen (2006), Metabolism

Key Takeaways

  • The cortisol–sleep relationship is reciprocal, not one-directional: the SCN clock, ultradian pulses, and sleep/behavior all shape the 24-hour cortisol waveform (Oster et al., 2017).
  • The nighttime cortisol trough is permissive for slow-wave sleep; centrally, CRH and cortisol act as alerting signals that suppress deep sleep, so low nighttime cortisol and deep sleep are two faces of the same quiescent state (Buckley & Schatzberg, 2005; Balbo et al., 2010).
  • Sleep loss impairs the HPA axis's negative feedback, raising cortisol the following evening, the arrow that closes two phenomena into a self-reinforcing loop (Leproult et al., 1997).
  • Chronic insomnia is associated with 24-hour HPA activation with peak excess in the evening and early night, consistent with CNS hyperarousal that may be partly causal, not merely consequential (Vgontzas et al., 2001).
  • Circadian misalignment reverses the cortisol rhythm and worsens glucose, insulin, leptin, and blood pressure independent of sleep loss, so timing is its own axis of harm (Scheer et al., 2009).
  • Sleep restriction reproduces cortisol-driven metabolic derangements (impaired glucose tolerance), linking this loop directly to the metabolic disease risk of Chapter 6 (Spiegel et al., 1999).
  • Chronic sleep disruption is both a cause and a symptom of HPA dysregulation, a load-bearing pillar of allostatic regulation, not passive downtime (McEwen, 2006).
  • This is educational physiology: recognize the pattern, educate, and refer. Insomnia and shift-work disorder are clinical conditions to be identified and handed off, not diagnosed or treated.
Looking Ahead

We have now seen the network operating against itself. In the next chapter we turn the question around: if sleep, feeding, light, and stress can push the cortisol system out of balance, can the same levers be used deliberately to support it? We move from mechanism to modulation, examining the evidence for behavioral inputs that stabilize the diurnal curve, and drawing the sharp line between educating on those inputs and the clinical work that lies beyond our scope.
