Part C · Presence Versus Dysregulation

  1. Define presence and dysregulation in one sentence each, using cortisol as the example. Make clear why one is healthy and the other is not.
  2. Two people share an identical 24-hour total cortisol. One has a strong morning peak and a deep nighttime trough; the other runs flat all day. Explain why they may be in very different states of health despite the same total.
  3. Name one form of dysregulation that involves the receiving tissue rather than the amount released, and explain in a sentence why a blood level alone would miss it.

Part D · Acute Versus Chronic

Use the zebra-and-the-inbox contrast from the chapter.

  1. In one or two sentences, describe what the cortisol response does for the zebra that sprints from a lion, and identify the single step that makes that response protective rather than harmful.
  2. Explain why a symbolic, never-ending human stressor (an inbox, a rehearsed worry) runs the same machinery to a different outcome. Use the idea of allostatic load in your answer.
  3. A hard training session produces a sharp cortisol rise. Explain why treating that acute spike as equivalent to chronic, unresolved stress is a mistake, and what would be lost by cutting the training to avoid it.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds cortisol literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the signal works, and treat anything that looks clinical (profound fatigue with weight loss, fainting or near-fainting, dizziness on standing, or other alarm signs) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Understanding the biology deeply is what makes a good referral possible; it never replaces one. Work within your role, whatever it is: a self-learner does not self-diagnose, and a symptom in a real person belongs with a clinician, not a saliva kit and a forum.
