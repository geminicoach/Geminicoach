Why Stress Makes You Sick: Cortisol and the Immune System

How one hormone can both suppress inflammation and, over time, permit it.

60 min read 8 cited sources

You have felt this timeline in your own body. The semester tightens. Deadlines stack, sleep thins, and you run for two weeks on adrenaline and coffee. You submit the final paper, exhale — and within seventy-two hours your throat is raw and your head is full of concrete. You made it through the crisis and then got sick, as if your body had been holding illness at arm's length until it was safe to collapse. The folk wisdom is almost right, but the mechanism it implies — "stress lowers your immune system" — is too blunt to be true. The real story is stranger and more elegant.

Cortisol, the hormone this course has followed through metabolism, the brain, and the daily rhythm of your blood, is prescribed in synthetic form precisely to shut inflammation down. And yet chronic stress, which floods the body with cortisol, is associated with more inflammation, not less. Same hormone, opposite outcomes. Resolving that paradox is the work of this chapter — and it will hand you the clearest illustration in the entire course of the acute-versus-chronic principle.

The prescription-pad paradox

Walk into any pharmacy and you will find cortisol's chemical relatives everywhere: hydrocortisone cream for a rash, prednisone for an asthma flare, dexamethasone for severe inflammation. These are glucocorticoids — synthetic analogues of the very hormone your adrenal glands secrete under stress. Their job is to calm immune activity. So the naïve syllogism writes itself: cortisol suppresses immunity; stress raises cortisol; therefore stress suppresses immunity; therefore stressed people get sick.

Every step of that argument is partly true, and the conclusion is directionally correct — stressed people do get sick more often. But the syllogism hides the most important fact in immunology's relationship with cortisol: the effect of the hormone depends almost entirely on how long it acts and whether the target cells are still listening. A brief pulse of cortisol does something categorically different to the immune system than a months-long elevation does. Treating "cortisol" as a single lever with a single direction is like describing a thermostat by saying it "makes the room hot." It does — in one mode. In another it does the opposite.

Acute cortisol as a battlefield strategist

Start with the version of stress your body was actually built for: the sprint, the fight, the near-miss on the highway. This is acute stress — a surge lasting minutes to perhaps an hour, followed by recovery. From an evolutionary standpoint, the moments most likely to threaten your ancestors' survival were also the moments most likely to break skin and admit pathogens. A stress response that anticipated injury and pre-positioned immune defenses would be enormously adaptive.

This is exactly what the acute response does, and the elegant experiments demonstrating it come from Firdaus Dhabhar and Bruce McEwen. Working in rodents, they measured delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) — a cell-mediated skin reaction that is a clean readout of immune cells doing surveillance work in peripheral tissue. Acute stress, applied just before antigen challenge, enhanced the DTH response. Chronic stress suppressed it. The same stressor category, opposite signs, distinguished only by duration (Dhabhar & McEwen, 1997).

The mechanism they identified is the concept that reframes this entire field: leukocyte trafficking, or immune-cell redistribution. Acute stress hormones do not simply turn immunity "up." They relocate it. Within minutes of a stressor, leukocytes exit the bloodstream and marginate into tissues — the skin, the lymph nodes, the mucosal surfaces — the very places a wound is likely to breach. A blood draw taken mid-stressor might show fewer circulating lymphocytes, which a naïve reading would call immunosuppression. But the cells have not vanished; they have deployed. Dhabhar and McEwen showed that low doses of corticosterone (the rodent's cortisol) mimicked the enhancing effect, while chronic or high-dose exposure suppressed it. Acute glucocorticoid, in their memorable framing, acts as a warning signal — a hormonal bugle call that sends immune cells to the ramparts.

The human evidence tracks this animal work with satisfying precision. Suzanne Segerstrom and Gregory Miller's meta-analysis — more than 300 studies spanning thirty years of psychoneuroimmunology — sorted stressors by duration and found a coherent gradient (Segerstrom & Miller, 2004). Acute stressors lasting minutes were associated with upregulation of natural immunity: the fast, non-specific arm (natural killer cells, granulocytes) that mounts a first response and is exactly what you would want primed at the moment of injury. This is not damage. This is design.

So the first correction to the naïve syllogism is this: acute cortisol does not suppress immunity wholesale — it modulates and redistributes it, often adaptively. Any account that starts with "stress hormones weaken you" has already lost the plot in the first act.

Why the modulation matters: restraint as a feature

There is a second, subtler role for acute cortisol that the "warning signal" framing sets up. The inflammatory response is a controlled fire. Its purpose is to destroy pathogens and clear damaged tissue, and it does so with weapons — reactive oxygen species, proteases, pro-inflammatory cytokines — that do not distinguish cleanly between invader and host. An unchecked inflammatory cascade can kill; septic shock is inflammation spiraling into self-damage.

Cortisol is one of the body's principal brakes on that fire. As the acute response resolves, rising cortisol restrains the pro-inflammatory cytokines it helped mobilize, keeping the emergency from consuming the tissue it was meant to protect. This is why the anti-inflammatory action of cortisol is not a bug or a contradiction — it is the off-switch built into the same system as the on-switch. A well-functioning acute response looks like this: mobilize fast (redistribute leukocytes, upregulate natural immunity), then resolve cleanly (cortisol reins in the cytokines before they overshoot). Deploy, then stand down.

Chronic stress: when the bugle never stops

The stress response is a system optimized for the sprint and priced for the sprint. Extend it into a marathon — the caregiving that lasts years, the job that never lets up, the exam period that bleeds into the next — and the same machinery that protected you begins to cost you. This is the allostatic load framework from earlier chapters, now applied to immunity: the "wear and tear" of chronic activation includes a mis-tuned immune system.

Segerstrom and Miller's gradient tells the story cleanly. Move from acute stressors (minutes) to brief naturalistic stressors — the exam week is their canonical example — and the picture shifts. Exams tended to suppress cellular immunity (the arm that fights viruses and intracellular pathogens) while largely preserving humoral immunity. Move further, to genuinely chronic stressors — unemployment, caregiving for a spouse with dementia, tending a child through cancer — and both cellular and humoral measures were suppressed. The longer and less controllable the stressor, the broader and deeper the immune cost (Segerstrom & Miller, 2004). Physical vulnerability — age, existing disease — amplified every effect.

So far this looks like straightforward immunosuppression, and for the viral-defense arm it largely is. But suppression cannot be the whole story, because chronically stressed people also show elevated inflammation. Both cannot be caused by "more cortisol suppressing everything." Something has to give the same hormone two faces. That something is the mechanism we turn to next — and it is the concrete instance of the blunted-feedback theme this course has been building toward.

Glucocorticoid receptor resistance: the deaf cell

A hormone is only as powerful as its receptor's willingness to listen. Cortisol delivers its anti-inflammatory message by binding the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) inside immune cells; the activated receptor then travels to the nucleus and switches off pro-inflammatory genes. Remove or blunt that receptor's response and cortisol can shout all it likes — the cell no longer hears the "stand down" order.

This is glucocorticoid receptor resistance (GCR), and it is the hinge of the entire paradox. Under prolonged high cortisol, immune cells downregulate their sensitivity to the hormone — a plausible adaptation to constant signaling, and a direct parallel to the receptor downregulation you have seen in insulin resistance and in the blunted HPA feedback of Chapter 5. The consequence is precise and devastating: circulating cortisol stays high, but its restraining grip on inflammation slips. The on-switch (inflammation) keeps firing while the off-switch (cortisol's brake) is disconnected. Inflammation rises not despite high cortisol but because the cells stopped answering it.

Gregory Miller and colleagues built the first clean human demonstration of this model. They compared parents of children being treated for cancer — a paradigm of unrelenting chronic stress — with parents of healthy children. The chronically stressed parents showed flatter diurnal cortisol slopes (the dysregulated rhythm of Chapter 6) and, critically, a reduced capacity of a synthetic glucocorticoid to suppress IL-6 production in their immune cells tested in vitro (Miller, Cohen, & Ritchey, 2002). In plain terms: hand these cells a potent anti-inflammatory drug in a dish and they shrugged. Their receptors had gone partly deaf. Miller and colleagues proposed this glucocorticoid-resistance model as the pathway linking chronic stress to inflammatory disease.

The systematic review by Lasselin and colleagues confirms the robustness of this picture across species. Pooling 15 mouse, 7 primate, and 19 human studies, they found chronic stress reliably associated with downregulated GR sensitivity, altered β-adrenergic signaling, and upregulated pro-inflammatory biomarkers in peripheral blood (Lasselin et al., 2021). Intriguingly, they surface a mechanism deeper than receptor tuning: chronic stress may alter myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow, releasing immune cells that are inherently less glucocorticoid-sensitive from birth. Resistance, on this account, is not only cells going deaf — it is the factory shipping cells that were never fully wired to listen.

Closing the loop: from resistance to illness

A model is only as good as its prospective test, and Sheldon Cohen's team supplied one of the most convincing in the field. In two viral-challenge studies, healthy volunteers were assessed for stress and glucocorticoid sensitivity, then deliberately exposed to a common-cold virus under quarantine (Cohen et al., 2012). In the first study (n = 276), stressful life events lasting a month or more were associated with GCR, and GCR statistically mediated the increased risk of developing a cold — meaning the resistance was not a bystander correlate but a step on the causal path. In the second (n = 79), volunteers with greater GCR produced more local pro-inflammatory cytokines after rhinovirus infection. This is the whole argument assembled in one design: chronic stress → glucocorticoid resistance → failure to restrain inflammation → worse illness.

And here is the beautiful subtlety worth pausing on. The cold symptoms you experience are largely produced by your own inflammatory response, not by the virus directly. So GCR does not just leave you more vulnerable to catching the virus (through suppressed antiviral cellular immunity); it also makes the illness you do get feel worse, because the inflammatory response runs without its brake. Suppression and excess inflammation, the two faces of the paradox, turn out to be two consequences of the very same receptor failure.

Four places you can watch it happen

Mechanisms convince a physiologist; consequences convince everyone else. The glucocorticoid-resistance model earns its keep because it predicts four concrete, measurable phenomena, each documented in humans.

1. You catch the cold after finals

The founding study of modern psychoneuroimmunology settled the direction of causation. Sheldon Cohen's team recruited 394 healthy adults, measured their psychological stress, then deliberately exposed them to one of five respiratory viruses and quarantined them (Cohen et al., 1991). Higher stress predicted higher rates of clinical cold in a clean dose-response pattern. Crucially, the effect ran through increased rates of infection — stress lowered host resistance, letting the virus take hold — rather than merely making the already-infected feel worse. Because the exposure was controlled and randomized, this study did what observational work cannot: it established that stress precedes and raises susceptibility, not the reverse.

2. Your wounds heal more slowly

Wound healing is inflammation's constructive face — the early phase depends on pro-inflammatory cytokines recruiting cells to the injury. Glaser and Kiecolt-Glaser's authoritative review synthesizes evidence that stress slows healing, in part through a stress-induced reduction in exactly those pro-inflammatory cytokines at the wound site (Glaser & Kiecolt-Glaser, 2005). Note the apparent tension with the GCR story: here stress produces too little local inflammation where it is needed, while systemically producing too much. This is not a contradiction — it is dysregulation. A mis-tuned system does not fail in one direction; it loses the ability to put the right amount of inflammation in the right place at the right time.

3. Your vaccine takes less well

Kiecolt-Glaser and colleagues compared 32 elderly caregivers of spouses with dementia — a model chronic stressor — with 32 matched controls, following their response to influenza vaccination (Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 1996). Caregivers mounted significantly poorer antibody responses by both ELISA and hemagglutination inhibition, and showed lower virus-specific IL-2 and IL-1β. A vaccine is a controlled rehearsal of immune defense; a blunted rehearsal means weaker protection. This is one of the cleanest demonstrations that a real-world chronic stressor degrades a clinically meaningful immune outcome.

4. Dormant viruses wake up

Many people carry latent herpesviruses — Epstein–Barr (EBV), herpes simplex (HSV-1) — held in check by ongoing cell-mediated surveillance. When chronic stress suppresses that surveillance, the leash loosens and the virus reactivates, measurable as rising antibody titres against the latent virus (Glaser & Kiecolt-Glaser, 2005). Reactivation is a particularly elegant readout because the rising titre is an indirect meter of exactly how much the controlling arm of immunity has been suppressed.

Methodological honesty: what the evidence can and cannot say

An advanced reader should know where this literature is firm and where it is still settling. The viral-challenge studies (Cohen et al., 1991, 2012) are the field's strongest work precisely because the exposure is controlled and randomized — they can speak to causation. Much of the rest is observational: caregiver studies and exam cohorts show robust associations, but disentangling stress itself from the behaviors that travel with it (poorer sleep, worse diet, less exercise, more smoking) is genuinely hard, and those behaviors independently affect immunity. The strongest studies adjust for them; none can eliminate the concern entirely.

The mechanistic in-vitro work — measuring GR sensitivity in cultured cells (Miller et al., 2002) — is powerful for establishing plausibility but is one step removed from whole-organism outcomes. And effect sizes, while consistent, are moderate: chronic stress shifts probabilities, it does not flip a switch. The cross-species systematic review (Lasselin et al., 2021) is reassuring precisely because the same GR-resistance signature appears in mice, primates, and humans — convergent evidence across designs is worth more than any single study. This is why we speak of raised susceptibility and altered regulation, never of stress as a toxin that inevitably makes you ill.

Putting it together: the same hormone, two faces

Return one last time to the student who gets sick after finals. We can now narrate that timeline mechanistically. During the exam crunch, sustained cortisol suppressed her cellular (antiviral) immunity — the Segerstrom–Miller exam-stressor signature — while the same prolonged exposure began nudging her immune cells toward glucocorithoid resistance. A rhinovirus she encounters finds her antiviral surveillance blunted and takes hold. As symptoms emerge, her cortisol brake is partly disconnected, so the inflammatory response that produces her misery runs less restrained than it should. High cortisol, suppressed defense, unrestrained inflammation — all at once, all from one hormone acting too long on cells that have stopped listening.

That is the resolution of the prescription-pad paradox. A pharmacological pulse of glucocorticoid, delivered to responsive receptors, suppresses inflammation — which is why it treats a flare. Chronic endogenous cortisol, delivered to receptors that have gone resistant, permits inflammation, because the message no longer lands. The difference is not the molecule. The difference is duration and receptor sensitivity — the acute-versus-chronic principle, written in the language of immunity.

Key Takeaways

  • Acute cortisol does not simply suppress immunity — it redistributes leukocytes into peripheral tissue and upregulates fast natural immunity, an adaptive "warning signal" ahead of likely injury (Dhabhar & McEwen, 1997; Segerstrom & Miller, 2004).
  • Cortisol's anti-inflammatory action is a built-in brake preventing an emergency response from spiraling into self-damage — the off-switch and on-switch share one system.
  • Stressor duration sets the sign of the effect: minutes-long stressors enhance natural immunity; exam-length stressors suppress cellular immunity; chronic stressors suppress both cellular and humoral immunity (Segerstrom & Miller, 2004).
  • Glucocorticoid receptor resistance resolves the central paradox: under chronic high cortisol, immune cells stop "hearing" the anti-inflammatory signal, so inflammation rises even while cortisol stays high (Miller et al., 2002; Cohen et al., 2012; Lasselin et al., 2021).
  • The model predicts four documented outcomes: raised infection susceptibility (Cohen et al., 1991), slowed wound healing, blunted vaccine responses (Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 1996), and latent-virus reactivation (Glaser & Kiecolt-Glaser, 2005).
  • This is allostatic load applied to immunity: "wear and tear" includes a mis-tuned immune system that loses the ability to put the right amount of inflammation in the right place. Stress shifts susceptibility — it is not a toxin, and we educate and refer rather than diagnose or treat.
