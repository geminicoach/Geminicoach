Chapter 11 : Adapt or Break Down

One molecule, one axis: the same cortisol that signals adaptation is the cortisol that, sustained without recovery, grinds an athlete down. Balance decides which face it shows.

The Core Argument

  • Exercise is a stressor you choose: a hard session raises cortisol, fires the sympathoadrenal system, and tilts muscle toward breakdown, above a rough intensity threshold.
  • The acute cortisol pulse is not damage to minimize; it is part of the signal that triggers adaptation. Fitness is built afterward, in recovery, not during the session.
  • Whether training builds or breaks an athlete turns on one variable: the load-to-recovery ratio, which the athlete sets. Nothing about the cortisol molecule changes between the two outcomes.

Stressor In, Adaptation Out

  • Adaptive stress : the acute rise is dose-dependent and trainable; regular recovered training tunes the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.
  • Healthy trained athletes show enhanced cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) responses to a stimulus, not quieter ones.
  • A well-trained stress axis is responsive, not silent: quick to rise for a real demand, quick to stand down.

Recovery Builds Fitness

  • Supercompensation : the rebound above baseline that happens after the stressor is removed.
  • Muscle protein synthesis stays elevated roughly 24 to 48 hours post-session, only if fuel and rest are supplied.
  • Fitness-fatigue model : performance is roughly fitness minus fatigue; stack sessions before fatigue clears and the rebound never surfaces.
  • Training is allostatic load with a dial: recovery levers (sleep, fuel, time) between sessions often matter most.

The Overtraining Continuum

Functional overreaching (FOR)Planned short-term dip; a genuine unloading period follows, and performance rebounds above baseline. The engine of periodized training.
Non-functional overreaching (NFOR)Same overload, no adequate recovery. The dip persists for weeks with mounting fatigue and mood disturbance; the rebound never arrives.
Overtraining syndrome (OTS)Prolonged multi-system maladaptation. Recovery takes months. A clinical diagnosis of exclusion, because treatable conditions mimic it.

DHEA & the Ratio

  • Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) : the adrenal androgen made on the same steroidogenic pathway as cortisol; cortisol's anabolic counterweight.
  • Cortisol-to-DHEA ratio : indexes anabolic-catabolic balance; a rising ratio means catabolism is winning.
  • A ratio survives the problem that sinks a single value, but it is a research marker and a trend, not a diagnostic verdict.
  • After hard exercise, DHEA rises with cortisol, then a bigger cortisol spike predicts more anabolic suppression a day later.

What Actually Marks Training State

  • Resting hormone levels in overtrained athletes are often normal; a standard resting panel can look unremarkable.
  • The functional marker is HPA axis responsiveness under stimulation: enhanced in health, blunted in overtraining.
  • A single resting cortisol value is close to the least informative measurement for this purpose.

Monitoring Without a Laboratory

  • Session rating of perceived exertion (RPE) : rated hardness times duration; a cheap, validated index of internal load, summed weekly.
  • Heart rate variability (HRV) : a noisy autonomic window on recovery; read as a rolling weekly trend, never as a single-day verdict.
  • Mood : rising fatigue and falling vigor often move earlier and more sensitively than any hormone assay; a genuine early-warning channel.
  • Cortisol awakening response (CAR) : a promising but composite monitor that encodes both prior load and anticipated stress, so it resists simple reading.
  • Load rises, felt recovery lags, mood dips, performance stalls: those four moving together over weeks beat any resting cortisol number.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of training physiology and monitoring, not a license to diagnose, treat, or prescribe a training program. Functional overreaching is a normal state you can recognize and reason about; overtraining syndrome is a clinical condition you recognize the warning signs of and refer, because many treatable conditions mimic it. Work within your role: recognize a pattern and refer, rather than diagnosing or prescribing.
