Fuel, Fat, and Muscle: Cortisol and Metabolism

How the body's master fuel-mobilizing hormone becomes a metabolic liability when the emergency never ends.

60 min read 8 cited sources

Imagine two glucose molecules leaving a liver cell within the same hour, headed for the same bloodstream, driven by the same hormone. The first belongs to a hiker who has just startled a bear — her cortisol is spiking, her liver is dumping fuel, and within minutes that glucose will power a sprint that keeps her alive. The second belongs to a shift worker on his fourth consecutive night of broken sleep, sitting perfectly still at a desk. His cortisol is also elevated, his liver is also dumping glucose, but there is no sprint. No muscle is asking for the fuel. The glucose simply accumulates in a bloodstream that is becoming, week by week, less able to clear it.

Same molecule. Same mechanism. Opposite meaning. This is the central puzzle of cortisol and metabolism, and it is the puzzle this chapter exists to solve: an action that is unambiguously adaptive in an acute emergency becomes unambiguously corrosive when it runs day after day. To understand why chronic stress and modern metabolic disease keep showing up together, we have to follow the fuel.

Cortisol's One Job, Restated

In Chapter 4 we established cortisol's defining physiological purpose: to make fuel available. In Chapter 3 we traced its daily rhythm — the pre-waking surge that lifts blood glucose before your feet hit the floor. This chapter cashes out both ideas into their metabolic consequences over time. If you retain nothing else, retain this framing: cortisol is a fuel-mobilization hormone, and nearly everything metabolic it does is a variation on the theme of getting energy substrate into the blood and keeping it there.

It accomplishes this through a coordinated set of actions across three tissues — liver, muscle, and adipose — that we will examine in turn. But the mechanism underneath them all is worth naming first, because it explains why cortisol's effects are broad, slow, and durable. Cortisol works primarily as a transcriptional regulator. When cortisol crosses a cell membrane and binds the intracellular glucocorticoid receptor (GR), the hormone-receptor complex translocates to the nucleus and alters the expression of hundreds of genes (Lockett et al., 2024). This is why the fuller metabolic picture unfolds over hours to days rather than seconds — cortisol doesn't flip a switch so much as retune an entire orchestra of enzymes.

Because GR is expressed almost everywhere, this single hormone can coordinate a whole-body reallocation of fuel. That coordination is beautiful in a crisis and dangerous as a chronic default. Let us follow it, organ by organ.

The Liver: Manufacturing Glucose From Scratch

Start with the liver, because it is where cortisol makes its most direct metabolic mark. The liver's job during stress is to guarantee a glucose supply for tissues that depend on it — most urgently the brain, which under ordinary conditions runs almost exclusively on glucose. Cortisol ensures that supply in two ways: by promoting glycogenolysis (the rapid breakdown of stored glycogen) in the short term, and by ramping up gluconeogenesis — the synthesis of brand-new glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors — over the longer term.

What gluconeogenesis actually means

The word is less intimidating once you break it apart: gluco- (glucose), -neo- (new), -genesis (creation). New glucose creation. When glycogen stores run low or demand stays high, the liver builds glucose molecules from smaller carbon skeletons — chiefly amino acids liberated from muscle protein, glycerol released from fat, and lactate returning from working tissue. Think of gluconeogenesis as the body's ability to run its fuel factory in reverse: instead of only storing and releasing pre-made glucose, it can manufacture glucose out of raw materials scavenged from elsewhere in the body.

Cortisol is a principal driver of this factory. Acting through the hepatic glucocorticoid receptor, it upregulates the two rate-limiting gluconeogenic enzymes: phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) (Luo et al., 2026). PEPCK commits carbon skeletons to the glucose-building pathway; G6Pase performs the final step that frees glucose to leave the liver cell and enter the blood. When cortisol turns up the transcription of these two genes, hepatic glucose output climbs. Liver-specific GR-knockout models make the point cleanly: remove the glucocorticoid receptor from hepatocytes and the gluconeogenic response to glucocorticoids collapses (Luo et al., 2026).

Think About It

If gluconeogenesis builds glucose from amino acids, and amino acids come largely from muscle protein, what does that imply about the trade-off the body is making during prolonged cortisol elevation? Hold that question — we return to it when we reach muscle.

Cortisol rarely acts alone here. It potentiates the actions of glucagon and the catecholamines (adrenaline and noradrenaline), meaning cortisol makes the liver more responsive to those faster-acting signals (Lockett et al., 2024). This is a recurring theme in glucocorticoid biology: cortisol is often less the trigger and more the amplifier — the hormone that sets the metabolic stage so that other signals hit harder. It is a slow permission slip that says, in effect, release fuel, and keep releasing it.

The Bloodstream: Cortisol Versus Insulin

Manufacturing glucose accomplishes nothing if that glucose is immediately swept out of the blood and into storage. So cortisol does something clever and, over time, costly: it antagonizes insulin. Insulin is the body's primary storage-and-uptake signal — it tells muscle and fat cells to pull glucose out of the blood. Cortisol works against this, keeping glucose in circulation where an emergency-mobilized brain or a sprinting muscle can grab it directly.

Two fronts of insulin antagonism

Cortisol opposes insulin on two fronts simultaneously. Centrally, in the liver, it drives glucose production upward (as we just saw). Peripherally, in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, it inhibits insulin-stimulated glucose uptake (Beaupere et al., 2021). The molecular details are still being mapped, but the pattern is consistent: glucocorticoids interfere with insulin's signaling cascade downstream of the insulin receptor, blunting the translocation of GLUT4 transporters to the cell surface and reducing how much glucose muscle and fat can absorb (Geer et al., 2014). Less glucose goes in; more stays out. The net effect is higher blood glucose.

In an acute stressor this is exactly right. A threatened body wants glucose to stay available in the blood rather than get locked into storage. But now run the same program continuously. Sustained cortisol elevation produces sustained insulin antagonism, and the pancreas responds the only way it can — by secreting more insulin to force glucose out of an increasingly resistant bloodstream. This is the physiological definition of insulin resistance: the same amount of insulin accomplishing less, requiring the system to compensate with more. Beaupere and colleagues (2021) trace this insulin-resistant state across liver, muscle, adipose, pancreas, and even gut and brain, framing it as a whole-body phenomenon rather than a single-tissue defect.

Think About It

Cushing's syndrome — pathological cortisol excess — reliably produces hyperglycemia and, in many patients, frank diabetes (Geer et al., 2014). Why do endocrinologists treat Cushing's as a "natural experiment" for understanding what chronic cortisol does to metabolism? What are the limits of generalizing from a disease of extreme excess to the milder dysregulation of everyday chronic stress?

The clinical logic runs in a straight line, and it is worth stating plainly because it anchors everything that follows: cortisol raises blood glucose by making more of it and by preventing tissues from clearing it. In a crisis, adaptive. Chronically, a recipe for the exact metabolic environment that precedes type 2 diabetes.

Adipose Tissue: The Lipolysis Paradox

Now to the tissue everyone actually asks about: fat. Here cortisol produces one of the most misunderstood effects in all of endocrinology, and getting it right is central to this chapter's purpose. The confusion stems from a genuine paradox in the science.

Acutely, cortisol is lipolytic — it breaks fat down. It promotes the release of free fatty acids and glycerol from adipose stores to serve as alternative fuel and as raw material for gluconeogenesis (Macfarlane et al., 2008). If cortisol only did this, the popular story would be exactly backwards: cortisol would be a fat-burning hormone. So why does chronic cortisol excess — as in Cushing's syndrome — reliably produce fat accumulation, and specifically a redistribution toward the trunk and abdomen?

Resolving the paradox: it's about depot and about location

The resolution has two parts. First, chronic cortisol drives depot-specific fat redistribution — it doesn't simply add or remove fat everywhere but shifts where fat is stored, favoring visceral adipose tissue (the metabolically active fat packed around the abdominal organs) over subcutaneous depots (Geer et al., 2014). This is why chronic glucocorticoid excess produces the characteristic central pattern rather than uniform weight gain. Visceral fat happens to be richly supplied with glucocorticoid receptors and highly responsive to cortisol's storage signals in the presence of insulin.

And that phrase — in the presence of insulin — is the second part of the resolution. Cortisol's lipolytic and lipogenic effects depend on the hormonal environment. When cortisol is chronically elevated and insulin is chronically elevated (because of the insulin resistance we just described), the combination favors net fat storage in visceral depots, even as cortisol keeps free fatty acids cycling. Macfarlane and colleagues (2008) frame this precisely as the paradox that must be explained: acute lipolysis coexisting with chronic fat redistribution, "fuelling fat redistribution in the metabolic syndrome."

The 11β-HSD1 twist: local cortisol, not just blood cortisol

There is one more layer, and it is the layer that quietly reshaped how the field thinks about the whole problem. For years, researchers were puzzled that many people with classic metabolic syndrome do not have elevated blood cortisol. If cortisol drives the syndrome, why do the numbers so often look normal?

The answer is that tissues can manufacture their own active cortisol locally. The enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (11β-HSD1) converts inactive cortisone into active cortisol within adipocytes and hepatocytes, amplifying glucocorticoid action at the tissue level regardless of what circulating levels show (Seckl, 2004). Seckl's synthesis reframed metabolic syndrome as a kind of "Cushing's-like state" driven by local tissue cortisol excess. The evidence is striking: transgenic mice engineered to overexpress 11β-HSD1 specifically in adipose tissue develop the full metabolic syndrome — visceral obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia — even with normal blood cortisol (Seckl, 2004). This tells us something conceptually important for the entire course: "the cortisol level" is not a single number in the blood. It is a tissue-by-tissue phenomenon, regenerated and amplified locally. Keep this in your pocket the next time someone tries to sell a saliva test as the whole story.

Think About It

The 11β-HSD1 finding means two people with identical blood cortisol could have very different tissue glucocorticoid exposure. What does this do to the marketing claim that you can measure and "fix" your cortisol with a simple test-and-supplement protocol?

Muscle: The Catabolic Cost

Return now to the question we parked earlier. Gluconeogenesis needs carbon skeletons, and a major source is amino acids drawn from muscle protein. This is not a side effect — it is a deliberate, evolved priority. In a true survival emergency, the body will sacrifice muscle protein to keep the brain fueled, and that is the correct trade-off when the alternative is dying. The problem, again, is chronicity.

Cortisol is catabolic toward skeletal muscle, and it achieves muscle breakdown through a well-characterized pathway. The classic review by Schakman and colleagues (2008) lays out the mechanism with unusual clarity. Chronic glucocorticoid exposure activates the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) — the cell's protein-degradation machinery — by upregulating two muscle-specific "atrogenes": atrogin-1 and MuRF-1. These enzymes tag muscle proteins for destruction, and the proteasome dismantles them. The liberated amino acids then flow to the liver as gluconeogenic substrate.

Not just more breakdown — less building

Cortisol attacks muscle mass from both directions. Beyond driving breakdown, it is anti-anabolic: it suppresses insulin-like growth factor I (IGF-I) signaling and upregulates myostatin, a hormone whose job is to limit muscle growth (Schakman et al., 2008). Both effects converge on the PI3K/Akt/FoxO signaling pathway — the same hub that insulin uses to build tissue. When cortisol suppresses Akt activity, the FoxO transcription factors are released to drive the atrogenes even harder. So cortisol simultaneously accelerates the demolition crew and lays off the construction crew.

One clinically important detail: glucocorticoid-induced muscle wasting preferentially affects fast-twitch (type II) fibers — the fibers responsible for powerful, rapid contraction (Schakman et al., 2008). This selectivity matters to anyone thinking about athletic performance, healthy aging, and recovery. Type II fibers are also the fibers most vulnerable to age-related sarcopenia, which raises a genuinely important question for anyone interested in body composition across the lifespan: what happens when the muscle-eroding pressure of chronic cortisol is layered on top of the muscle loss that aging brings anyway?

Think About It

An athlete under high chronic psychological stress, sleeping poorly, and training hard may find recovery and muscle gain frustratingly slow. Using the mechanisms above — UPS activation, IGF-I suppression, myostatin upregulation, type II fiber vulnerability — construct a plausible physiological explanation. Then note the honest limit: can you diagnose why a given individual isn't recovering? (You cannot, and this course is about educating and referring, not diagnosing.)

Putting It Together: Why Chronic Stress and Metabolic Disease Travel Together

Step back and assemble the pieces. Chronic cortisol elevation, whatever its source, pushes the metabolic system in a consistent direction: more hepatic glucose production, less peripheral glucose uptake, eroding insulin sensitivity, redistribution of fat toward the visceral compartment, and net loss of muscle protein. Every one of these actions is adaptive in an acute emergency and maladaptive as a chronic default. This is allostatic load — the concept from Chapter 5 — cashed out in its first concrete metabolic arena.

The epidemiology supports the mechanistic story. Hackett and Steptoe (2017), reviewing the link between psychological stress and type 2 diabetes in Nature Reviews Endocrinology, report that dysregulation of the diurnal cortisol profile — a flatter, less dynamic daily curve rather than simply "high cortisol" — predicts incident type 2 diabetes. This is a crucial nuance: it is often the loss of healthy rhythm, not merely elevated levels, that carries metabolic risk. Recall the morning surge from Chapter 3; a robust, dynamic diurnal curve appears to be a feature of metabolic health, and its flattening a warning sign.

The honest complication: cortisol never acts alone

Here we must be scrupulously careful, because this is exactly where popular messaging goes off the rails. Hackett and Steptoe (2017) emphasize that stress does not corrode metabolism only through direct hormonal pathways. It also operates through behavior: chronic stress reliably shifts food choice toward energy-dense palatable foods, reduces physical activity, and degrades sleep. These behavioral pathways interact with, and often amplify, the direct endocrine effects. Poor sleep alone impairs glucose tolerance and flattens the cortisol rhythm; the two feed each other.

So when we say cortisol and metabolic disease "travel together," we mean something specific and defensible, and we explicitly do not mean "cortisol makes you fat." Cortisol does not, by itself, cause obesity in a healthy person with adequate sleep, sensible energy intake, and regular movement. What chronic cortisol dysregulation does is shift the metabolic environment — tilting the odds toward glucose intolerance, visceral storage, and muscle loss — in ways that interact with diet, sleep, and activity. It is a modifier of risk operating inside a web of causes, not a solitary villain.

Fatty acid metabolism in relation to glucocorticoids is considerably less well understood than the effects on glucose, yet mounting evidence suggests that subtle abnormalities of glucocorticoid concentration or action underlie the metabolic syndrome.

Refusing the "Cortisol Detox" Narrative

You have almost certainly seen the advertisements: supplements to "block cortisol" and "melt belly fat," cleanses to "reset" your stress hormone, protocols promising that visceral fat will vanish once cortisol is "detoxed." Now that you hold the mechanisms, you can see precisely why these claims are, at best, a distortion.

First, cortisol is not a toxin to be flushed; it is an essential hormone you would die without. Second, as the 11β-HSD1 story shows, the biologically relevant cortisol exposure is generated locally in tissues and cannot be captured by a single blood or saliva number, let alone "blocked" by a capsule. Third, and most importantly, the physiology we have traced does not describe a lever you pull in isolation. Visceral fat and insulin resistance emerge from the interaction of cortisol dynamics with sleep, energy balance, and activity — which is exactly why the Scenario Explorer refuses to let you attribute all change to a single variable.

But — and this is the constructive flip side — the same mechanisms explain why sleep, stress management, and meal timing genuinely do belong in a serious conversation about metabolic health. Not because they "detox cortisol," but because restoring a healthy diurnal rhythm, protecting sleep, and moving regularly all shift the very variables the physiology identifies as load-bearing (Hackett & Steptoe, 2017). The legitimate version of the message is unglamorous and evidence-based: chronic stress physiology is real, it interacts with metabolism through well-characterized pathways, and addressing it is part of — never a substitute for — the fundamentals of diet, movement, and sleep.

For this audience, the professional posture follows directly. You educate clients about why stress, sleep, and rhythm matter metabolically. You do not diagnose insulin resistance, you do not treat metabolic disease, and you do not sell a hormone "reset." When the physiology suggests something clinical may be going on, you refer.

Key Takeaways

  • Cortisol's core metabolic job is fuel mobilization: it drives hepatic gluconeogenesis by upregulating PEPCK and G6Pase through the glucocorticoid receptor, manufacturing new glucose from amino acids, glycerol, and lactate.
  • Cortisol antagonizes insulin on two fronts — increasing hepatic glucose output and inhibiting insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in muscle and fat — keeping blood glucose available. Chronically, this produces whole-body insulin resistance.
  • The lipolysis paradox: cortisol is acutely fat-mobilizing, yet chronic excess drives fat redistribution toward visceral depots, especially in the presence of the elevated insulin that accompanies insulin resistance.
  • 11β-HSD1 regenerates active cortisol locally in adipose and liver tissue, meaning tissue glucocorticoid exposure can be high even when blood cortisol is normal — a decisive argument against simplistic "measure and block your cortisol" claims.
  • Cortisol is catabolic and anti-anabolic to muscle: it activates the ubiquitin-proteasome system via atrogin-1 and MuRF-1, suppresses IGF-I, and upregulates myostatin, preferentially wasting fast-twitch type II fibers.
  • Chronic stress and type 2 diabetes travel together partly through direct endocrine pathways (notably a flattened diurnal cortisol curve) and partly through behavior (food, activity, sleep). Cortisol does not "make you fat" alone — it shifts a metabolic environment shaped by many interacting variables.
Looking Ahead

We have followed cortisol into its first concrete arena and watched a survival mechanism curdle into metabolic liability. Next we turn to the immune system, where cortisol plays an equally double-edged role — an anti-inflammatory signal so powerful we prescribe synthetic versions of it, yet one whose chronic presence reshapes immune defense in ways that echo the metabolic story you now understand.
