Part C · Acute Enhancement Versus Chronic Impairment

The word doing the heavy lifting in this chapter is not "cortisol." It is "chronic." Use that distinction here.

  1. Buchanan and Lovallo found that stress-level cortisol at encoding selectively enhanced later memory for emotionally arousing images but not neutral ones. Explain why the selectivity matters, that is, why cortisol acting as a salience filter is a different and more biologically sensible claim than "cortisol improves memory" across the board.
  2. Sherman and colleagues found the acute enhancement was conditional on subjective arousal and was mediated by increased connectivity between hippocampal subregions. Explain how this resolves the apparent paradox that cortisol can both help and harm hippocampus-dependent memory.
  3. Someone reads that cortisol is "damaging the brain" and concludes that all cortisol is bad for memory. Using the difference between an acute pulse during an arousing event and months of elevated exposure, write the correction you would give them.

Part D · The Hippocampus as an HPA Brake

This is where the two halves of the course fuse. Trace the mechanism, do not just name it.

  1. The hippocampus is not only a memory structure. Explain its second job as a brake on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, including the direction of its output on corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) drive.
  2. Assemble the vicious cycle in order: chronic cortisol, then the hippocampus, then feedback, then cortisol again. Identify the single link in the chain that flips the sign from the self-correcting negative feedback of Chapter 2 to a self-reinforcing loop.
  3. The chapter compares this to a thermostat whose temperature probe melts a little more each time the furnace runs. Explain what that image captures that the ordinary picture of negative feedback leaves out, namely that one of the sensors is also a target of the signal it senses.

Part E · The Inflammation-Mood Link

Cortisol dynamics do not operate in isolation from the gut and immune systems studied earlier in the course.

  1. Dantzer and colleagues describe sickness behavior as adaptive in the short term but able to shade into depressive symptoms when immune activation is prolonged in vulnerable individuals. Restate the shared structural argument, a short-term response that is protective becoming harmful only when sustained, and give one earlier example from the course that fits the same shape.
  2. Cortisol normally restrains inflammation, yet inflammation can persist even while cortisol is high. Explain glucocorticoid resistance at immune cells, and why a signal can matter as much for whether it is heard as for how loudly it is sent.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds understanding of how cortisol reshapes the brain, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, and treat anything that looks clinical (persistent low mood, loss of pleasure, marked memory or cognitive decline, or any request to interpret a hormone result or start a treatment) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Diagnosing depression or anxiety belongs to a qualified mental-health professional. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
