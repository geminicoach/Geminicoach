The Gut Feeling: The Brain–Gut–HPA Axis

Why stress is felt below the diaphragm, and how the gut talks back.

60 min read 9 cited sources

Before a job interview, an exam, or a hard conversation, the body announces itself in a familiar place: the stomach clenches, the bowels grow restless, appetite vanishes or turns urgent. We call it a "gut feeling," and the phrase is not merely poetic. Long before the cerebral cortex has finished narrating the threat, a dense meshwork of neurons lining the intestine has already changed how the gut moves, secretes, and defends itself — and it is preparing to send its own report back up to the brain.

In Chapter 4 we saw acute stress divert resources away from digestion: blood shunted to muscle, motility suppressed, the whole enterprise of breaking down lunch put on hold in favor of running or fighting. That was one direction of a two-way street. This chapter completes the loop. We will map the brain–gut axis as a genuinely bidirectional system, show how it couples to the HPA axis you now know well, and ask what happens to a digestive tract that is chronically told "not now."

A second brain, and why it matters

The intestine is not a passive tube waiting for commands. Embedded in its wall is the enteric nervous system (ENS), an intrinsic network of an estimated 200–600 million neurons — comparable in number to the spinal cord — arranged in two interconnected plexuses that run the length of the gut (Sharkey & Mawe, 2023). The ENS can generate coordinated motility and secretion largely on its own, which is why an isolated segment of intestine, disconnected from the brain, will still produce peristaltic reflexes in a dish. It is this remarkable autonomy that earned the ENS its nickname, the "second brain."

But autonomy is not isolation. The ENS is continuously informed and modulated by the central nervous system through the autonomic nervous system — parasympathetic fibers traveling mostly in the vagus nerve, and sympathetic fibers from the prevertebral and paravertebral ganglia. Mayer (2011) described the resulting arrangement as a layered control hierarchy: local ENS circuits handle moment-to-moment digestion, while the brain exerts top-down modulation that biases the whole system according to context — safety or threat, feeding or fleeing.

Crucially, most of the traffic on this line runs the "wrong" way. Roughly 80–90% of the fibers in the vagus nerve are afferent — they carry information from the gut to the brain, not the other way around (Mayer, 2011). The gut is, in informational terms, mostly a sensor. It monitors stretch, chemistry, osmolarity, the presence of nutrients and toxins, immune activity, and the metabolic output of trillions of resident microbes, and it reports upward. This asymmetry is the anatomical heart of why the axis is bidirectional and why "gut feelings" are more than metaphor.

What acute stress does to a working gut

Let us reconstruct the acute stress response from Chapter 4, but this time keep our eyes on the intestine. When a stressor is perceived, the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), initiating the HPA cascade that ends in cortisol. Simultaneously, the sympathetic branch fires and the adrenal medulla releases catecholamines. Both arms of this response reach into the gut.

Konturek and colleagues (2011) organize the gastrointestinal consequences of stress into a useful inventory of six effects: altered motility, increased visceral perception, changed secretion, increased intestinal permeability, impaired mucosal regeneration and blood flow, and negative effects on the microbiota. In the acute phase, the first several dominate.

Motility: hurry up top, slow down below

Stress does not simply "slow the gut" — it reorganizes motility along the tract. A characteristic pattern is accelerated upper-gut transit and delayed gastric emptying, together with stimulated motor activity in the colon (Konturek et al., 2011). This is why acute stress can produce the seemingly contradictory pair of a stomach that feels leaden and heavy while the lower bowel becomes urgent. Leigh and colleagues (2023) note that acute stress, acting through glucocorticoids and catecholamines, generally reduces the contractility that drives orderly digestion in the small intestine while the colon becomes more active — a redistribution that makes sense if the organism's priority has shifted from extracting nutrients to unloading and mobilizing.

Secretion and blood flow: rationing the mucosa

Secretion shifts as well. Acid, mucus, and bicarbonate outputs change under stress, and — the point that ties this to Chapter 4 — splanchnic blood flow is reduced as circulation is redirected toward skeletal muscle and heart. The mucosa is a metabolically expensive, rapidly renewing tissue; it depends on generous perfusion to maintain its protective mucus layer and to regenerate its lining. When blood flow is throttled, mucosal defense and regeneration are compromised (Konturek et al., 2011). A brief episode is easily tolerated. The problem, as we will see, is repetition.

Visceral sensitivity: the volume knob turns up

Stress also changes how the gut feels. Increased visceral perception — heightened sensitivity of the afferent pathways that report gut state to the brain — means that normal distension or contraction can be perceived as discomfort or pain (Leigh et al., 2023). This sensitization is central to understanding disorders of gut–brain interaction, and it illustrates the axis working in the gut-to-brain direction: the same physical event registers differently depending on the sensitivity of the reporting channel.

The signal is a loop, not an arrow

The widget above makes a point worth stating plainly. Whether a signal begins in the brain or the gut, it does not travel one way and stop. A stressor descending from the hypothalamus alters gut function; the altered gut sends changed afferent signals upward; those signals feed back into the very circuits that govern the stress response. This is the essence of a bidirectional axis: cause and effect chase each other around the loop.

A striking demonstration of the brain-to-gut arm comes from a 2023 study identifying a specific CRH-expressing neuronal circuit projecting from the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVH) to the colon. In animals subjected to chronic social defeat stress, this circuit drove colonic inflammation, increased intestinal permeability, and elevated circulating bacterial products — and, remarkably, produced social avoidance behavior. Using chemogenetics, the authors showed that activating the PVH CRH⁺ neurons alone was sufficient to reproduce gut inflammation and barrier disruption, while inhibiting them prevented these effects (Stress-activated brain-gut circuits, 2023). This is causal evidence, not mere correlation: a defined brain circuit reaching down to break the gut barrier, with behavioral consequences that then loop back to the brain's state.

The barrier: one cell layer between two worlds

Here is a fact that reorients most people's intuition about the gut. The lining of the intestine — the surface separating the outside world (the lumen, teeming with food, microbes, and their toxins) from the inside of the body (the bloodstream) — is a single layer of epithelial cells. One cell thick. The integrity of that layer is maintained by protein complexes called tight junctions that seal the spaces between adjacent cells, allowing selective passage while keeping the microbial population and its debris on the luminal side where they belong.

Under chronic stress, this seal loosens. de Punder and Pruimboom (2015) synthesized the evidence that stress-system mediators — glucocorticoids and catecholamines chief among them — increase intestinal permeability, and they proposed a specific, consequential chain of events. When tight junctions loosen, bacterial components, especially lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, translocate across the barrier into circulation. The result is metabolic endotoxemia: a low but persistent burden of LPS in the blood that the innate immune system treats as an infection, mounting a chronic low-grade inflammatory response.

You should recognize where this leads. In Chapter 7 we built the case that chronic stress sustains a smoldering inflammatory state, and we watched the inflammation meter climb. The gut barrier is one of the most important mechanistic reasons that meter climbs. Stress compromises the barrier; the barrier leaks endotoxin; endotoxin drives inflammation; and inflammation, as we will see, feeds back onto the brain and the HPA axis. de Punder and Pruimboom (2015) explicitly tie this mechanism to the broad family of non-communicable diseases associated with chronic low-grade inflammation.

Mast cells: the translators at the barrier

How does a psychological signal become a physical breach in a cell layer? A key answer, emphasized by Konturek and colleagues (2011), is the mast cell. Mast cells sit in the gut mucosa in close apposition to enteric nerve endings, and they are exquisitely responsive to stress mediators — CRH acting locally, and neuropeptides released by activated enteric neurons. When triggered, mast cells degranulate, releasing histamine, proteases, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. These mediators loosen tight junctions, sensitize afferent nerves (raising visceral perception), and recruit further immune activity. The mast cell is thus a literal translator: it converts a neural stress signal into the biochemical language of inflammation and barrier change. This is why the same stressor can, within one tissue, simultaneously increase permeability, increase pain sensitivity, and increase local inflammation — the effects share an effector.

The microbiota enters the story

So far the cast has been neurons, hormones, immune cells, and epithelium. But the gut houses another participant with its own metabolic and signalling repertoire: the gut microbiota, the community of trillions of microorganisms resident in the intestine. Over the past fifteen years, a substantial body of work has recast this community not as passive lodgers but as active contributors to the brain–gut axis — and, most relevant to us, to the regulation of the HPA stress system itself.

Cryan and Dinan (2012), in a landmark review, established that gut microbiota communicate with the central nervous system through at least three channels: neural (largely via the vagus), endocrine, and immune pathways. They coined the framing of the microbiota–gut–brain axis and reviewed evidence from germ-free animals, probiotics, and antibiotic manipulations linking the microbiota to anxiety, mood, cognition, and pain. Rusch and colleagues (2023) later expanded the list of mechanistic routes to include microbial metabolites — short-chain fatty acids produced by bacterial fermentation, and bacterial influence over tryptophan metabolism (the precursor of serotonin) — along with regulation of the blood–brain barrier and modulation of hormone metabolism.

The germ-free experiment

The most persuasive early evidence came from germ-free animals — raised in sterile conditions with no microbiota at all. Dinan and Cryan (2012) reviewed the finding that germ-free mice mount an exaggerated HPA response to psychological stress: they release more ACTH and corticosterone than conventionally colonized animals. The response could be normalized by colonizing the animals with commensal bacteria — and notably, this normalization was effective only if colonization occurred within a critical early developmental window, implicating the microbiota in the developmental programming of the stress axis. Colonization with a specific organism, Bifidobacterium infantis, was among the interventions that could blunt the exaggerated response.

This is a genuinely bidirectional relationship. Stress alters the microbiota — recall that increased permeability lets bacterial antigens activate mucosal immunity, and that stress changes gut chemistry and transit in ways that reshape the microbial community. In turn, the microbiota shapes HPA reactivity. Dinan and Cryan (2012) further noted that in some models, probiotic administration blunted HPA reactivity, hinting at a route from the microbial community back to the calm or excitability of the whole stress system.

Think About It

Germ-free mice show an exaggerated cortisol-equivalent response to stress, and colonizing them early normalizes it while later colonization does not. What does the existence of a critical window suggest about how — and when — the microbiota exerts its influence on the stress axis? Is it acting like a moment-to-moment dial, or like a developmental architect?

Scientific humility: promising is not proven

It would be easy to over-read the microbiota literature, and much popular coverage does exactly that. So let us be precise about what the evidence supports and what it does not. The strongest, most mechanistic findings — the germ-free HPA data, the PVH-to-colon CRH circuit — come predominantly from rodent models. Animal work of this kind is indispensable for establishing causation, because it permits manipulations (sterile rearing, chemogenetic circuit control, defined colonization) that are impossible in humans. But rodent-to-human translation in the microbiome field is notoriously incomplete: microbial communities differ across species, diets differ, and the psychological stressors used in animal models are only analogies for human experience.

The authors themselves model the appropriate caution. Cryan and Dinan (2012) explicitly advise that microbiota-modulation strategies, however promising, are not yet established therapies. In human work, much of the evidence is associational — chronic stress is associated with altered bowel habit, with constipation or diarrhea (Leigh et al., 2023), and with shifts in microbial composition — and association does not settle the direction of causation or license clinical claims. The correct posture for an educated reader is to hold these findings as real and important signals about mechanism, while resisting the leap to "take this probiotic to fix your stress axis." Promising associations are not prescriptions.

The concept that specific probiotics may have therapeutic benefit… is intriguing but requires substantial further validation before clinical recommendations can be made.

Where your role ends

This chapter is a good place to be explicit about a professional boundary that runs through the whole course. Everything above is physiology: how stress and digestion are mechanistically coupled. That is squarely within what an educator, coach, or nutrition professional may teach and discuss. What lies outside that role is diagnosis. The symptoms we have described — altered bowel habit, abdominal pain, heightened visceral sensitivity — overlap with the presentation of genuine gastrointestinal disease. Irritable bowel syndrome, which Konturek and colleagues (2011) describe as the prototypical stress-related GI disorder, is itself a clinical diagnosis, and its symptoms can mimic or coexist with inflammatory bowel disease, infection, celiac disease, and other conditions that require medical evaluation.

The rule is simple and non-negotiable: we explain the physiology and we refer. Explaining why stress can disrupt digestion is education. Telling someone that their abdominal pain is stress-related, or that it is not something more serious, is diagnosis — and it belongs to clinicians. Persistent, severe, or alarming gut symptoms warrant a referral to a physician, full stop. Understanding the brain–gut–HPA axis makes you a better educator precisely because it lets you explain mechanism honestly while knowing exactly where your competence ends.

A worked example: closing the loop

Let us assemble the pieces into a single trajectory. Imagine a person under sustained work stress over several months — the chronic condition, not a single bad afternoon.

Step 1 — descending drive. Repeated activation of the HPA axis and sympathetic system, including CRH signalling from the PVH, reaches the colon (Stress-activated brain-gut circuits, 2023). Colonic motility patterns shift; the person notices irregular bowel habit (Leigh et al., 2023).

Step 2 — barrier compromise. Sustained glucocorticoid and catecholamine exposure, amplified locally by mast-cell degranulation, loosens tight junctions (Konturek et al., 2011; de Punder & Pruimboom, 2015). Splanchnic blood flow, chronically rationed, leaves the mucosa less able to regenerate.

Step 3 — leak and inflammation. LPS translocates into circulation, producing low-grade endotoxemia and driving systemic low-grade inflammation (de Punder & Pruimboom, 2015) — the Chapter 7 meter rising, with a concrete mechanism behind it.

Step 4 — the loop back up. Inflammatory cytokines and altered afferent signalling feed back onto the brain, and the microbial community — reshaped by the whole process — feeds into HPA reactivity (Dinan & Cryan, 2012; Rusch et al., 2023). Visceral sensitivity rises, so ordinary gut sensations register as discomfort, which is itself a stressor. The loop is now self-sustaining: stress degrades the gut, the degraded gut sustains the stress.

Notice that no step in this chain requires disease. It is ordinary physiology pushed by an ordinary modern stressor — which is exactly why gut symptoms and stress travel together so reliably, and why the honest educational message is about mechanism and referral, not diagnosis and cure.

Key Takeaways

  • The brain–gut axis is genuinely bidirectional: the ENS runs digestion locally, the CNS modulates top-down via the autonomic nervous system, and the majority of vagal traffic runs up from gut to brain.
  • Acute stress reorganizes gut function — delaying gastric emptying while stimulating the colon, altering secretion, cutting splanchnic blood flow, and turning up visceral sensitivity (Konturek et al., 2011; Leigh et al., 2023).
  • The intestinal barrier is a single cell layer sealed by tight junctions; chronic stress loosens it, letting LPS translocate and driving the low-grade inflammation of Chapter 7 (de Punder & Pruimboom, 2015). Mast cells translate neural stress signals into barrier and immune changes.
  • A defined PVH-to-colon CRH circuit provides causal evidence that the brain can break the gut barrier and alter behavior (Stress-activated brain-gut circuits, 2023).
  • The microbiota participates in HPA regulation — germ-free animals show exaggerated stress responses normalizable by early colonization — but much evidence is animal-based or associational. Promising is not proven (Cryan & Dinan, 2012; Dinan & Cryan, 2012; Rusch et al., 2023).
  • The educator's role is to explain physiology and refer. Diagnosing GI disease — including IBS — belongs to clinicians.
