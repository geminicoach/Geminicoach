Part A · The Two-Way Street

Chapter 3 treated the diurnal curve as something the clock produces and sleep merely sits inside. This chapter reframes it as reciprocal. Answer in two to three sentences each.

Part C · The Tired-but-Wired Loop

This is the self-reinforcing circuit at the heart of the chapter. Answer each item in two to three sentences.

  1. Leproult and colleagues (1997) found that sleep loss raised cortisol the following evening, not immediately on waking, by impairing the axis's negative feedback. Walk the loop: how does one bad night raise the next evening's cortisol, and how does that cortisol degrade the next night's sleep?
  2. Describe the "tired but wired" state in physiological terms. Which system reports tired, which reports wired, and why does lying in bed longer or trying harder fail to resolve it?
  3. Vgontzas and colleagues (2001) found a dose-response relationship in chronic insomnia and argued for central nervous system hyperarousal. Explain why a dose-response pattern points toward a causal link rather than a coincidence.

Part D · When the Clock and the Calendar Disagree

Shift work and jet lag attack timing rather than amount. Use the desynchrony logic from the chapter.

  1. Scheer and colleagues (2009) held sleep duration constant and still found worsened glucose, insulin, leptin, and blood pressure under misalignment. Explain why "just get your eight hours" is well meant but incomplete for a rotating night worker.
  2. Cortisol acts as a zeitgeber (time-giver) to peripheral clocks in the liver, pancreas, and other tissues. Explain what "many clocks disagreeing" means, and why re-entrainment after a large phase shift is slow (roughly one hour per day).

Part E · Tie It to Metabolism

This chapter is the hinge back to the metabolic chapter. Reason from mechanism.

  1. Spiegel and colleagues (1999) restricted healthy young men to four hours of sleep for six nights and saw glucose tolerance deteriorate. Explain how this links poor sleep and elevated cortisol as "the same road, traveled in both directions" toward metabolic disease.
  2. McEwen (2006) places chronic sleep disruption on both sides of the allostatic-load ledger. Explain how sleep disruption is at once a cause of load and a symptom of it, and why that makes sleep timing a high-leverage node.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds understanding of how the sleep-cortisol loop works, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain the mechanism, and treat anything that looks clinical (chronic insomnia, non-restorative sleep with persistent low mood, suspected shift-work disorder, or a home hormone result someone wants interpreted) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: understand the physiology, educate, and refer; never label or treat.
