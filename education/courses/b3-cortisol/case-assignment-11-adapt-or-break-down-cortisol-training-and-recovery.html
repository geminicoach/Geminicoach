Chapter 11: Reason Through a Stalled Athlete

Take the training chapter and use it to explain a real complaint, in plain language, within the bounds of general education and monitoring.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Petra, 36
Consider Petra, who runs and lifts several days a week and spent two years getting steadily fitter on a plan of harder weeks followed by easier ones. Chasing a personal best, she added volume, cut her one rest day, and started sleeping badly through a demanding stretch at work. Instead of getting faster she got slower, flatter, and short-tempered. Alarmed, she bought a mail-order saliva panel; it flagged a morning cortisol value as "elevated," and she concluded that cortisol was the problem and that the fix was to drive it as low as possible. She asks: "Is my cortisol wrecking my training, and how do I crush it?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, what is most likely happening to Petra using the load-recovery framework from the chapter. Make clear where fitness is actually built and why cutting a rest day and losing sleep would flatten performance rather than sharpen it.
  2. Explain why her single "elevated" saliva cortisol reading tells her almost nothing on its own, and what kind of information (responsiveness over time, trends, the cortisol-to-DHEA ratio read as a trend) would actually be informative.
  3. Place her picture on the continuum from functional overreaching to non-functional overreaching to overtraining syndrome (OTS), say how you know, and name the monitoring concepts a learner may reason about, without prescribing a training program.
  4. State clearly where general education and monitoring end and clinical care begins, including when Petra's situation would warrant a clinician rather than more self-managed recovery.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing or prescribing. A responsible, role-neutral response explains the mechanism, describes monitoring concepts within scope, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and neither writes a training program nor diagnoses. Diagnosis and treatment, including the clinical call on overtraining syndrome, belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains the load-recovery imbalance and that fitness is built during recovery, not during the session.
  • Explains why a single elevated resting cortisol value is uninformative, and that responsiveness and trends over time, including the cortisol-to-DHEA ratio, are what matter.
  • Places the picture on the overreaching-to-overtraining continuum with reasoning, and notes that overtraining syndrome is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion because treatable conditions mimic it.
  • Describes in-scope monitoring concepts (session rating of perceived exertion, mood, heart rate variability as trends) without prescribing a training plan.
  • Draws the education-versus-clinical boundary, refers rather than diagnoses, and does not prescribe.
