Part C · From Cholesterol to Cortisol

Cortisol is a steroid: it cannot be stockpiled and must be manufactured on demand from cholesterol. Use the pathway from the chapter.

  1. Name the rate-limiting first step (the molecule that must ferry cholesterol across the mitochondrial membrane, and the enzyme that then acts). Explain why this step, rather than a later one, is the main lever adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) pulls.
  2. Because cortisol is built to order rather than pulled from storage, there is a delay of minutes between a stressor and peak cortisol. Contrast this with adrenaline from the adrenal medulla, and explain what this timing difference tells you about the popular claim that adrenal glands can be "depleted."

Part D · The Loop That Ends the Response

Negative feedback is the heart of the course: cortisol circles back and shuts down its own production.

  1. Describe the two arms of the brake: the fast, non-genomic arm (seconds) and the slow, genomic arm (hours). Name the mechanism or receptor each one uses, and explain why the axis needs both rather than one.
  2. Cortisol at rest is released in hourly pulses, an ultradian rhythm. Explain how a feedback loop with a built-in time lag produces oscillating pulses rather than a flat line.
  3. Suppose the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), the sensor the slow brake depends on, were weakened. Predict what happens to the peak cortisol reached and to the baseline the system settles at, and say why.

Part E · Free Versus Bound Cortisol

Once built, the hormone has to travel, and most of it travels packed away. Reason about transport.

  1. Under normal conditions, roughly what fraction of blood cortisol circulates as free cortisol, and what carries the rest? Explain why only the free fraction can actually exert an effect.
  2. Cortisol-binding globulin (CBG) is not just packaging. Give one condition (for example fever or local inflammation) that loosens its grip, and explain how that can raise active cortisol at a tissue without the brain issuing a single new order.
  3. A blood assay usually measures total cortisol; saliva reflects free cortisol. Explain why an estrogen-containing medication can raise total blood cortisol while the active free fraction barely moves, and why this matters when reading a test.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds physiological literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the axis works, and treat anything that looks clinical (unexplained weight loss, darkening skin, dizziness on standing, persistent fatigue, or any result someone is genuinely worried about) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is: explain the mechanism, refer when a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, and do not self-diagnose.
