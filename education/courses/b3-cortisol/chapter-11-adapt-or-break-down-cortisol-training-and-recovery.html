Adapt or Break Down: Cortisol, Training, and Recovery

Exercise is a stressor you choose on purpose, and the same physiology that heals you can also grind you down.

60 min read 11 cited sources

A barbell does not know it is medicine. When a lifter loads the bar and drives it overhead, the body reads the event the way it reads any threat: metabolic demand spikes, the sympathetic nervous system fires, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the machinery you have followed for ten chapters, releases a pulse of cortisol into the blood. Heart rate climbs. Glucose is mobilized. Muscle protein is, for a while, broken down faster than it is built. By every biochemical measure we have studied, a hard training session is a stressor. If you had never seen the aftermath, and someone described only the acute event, higher cortisol, higher catecholamines, suppressed digestion, accelerated protein breakdown, you would file it next to a near-miss with a car or a bad night of work.

And yet nobody would call a squat session "bad for you." That is the paradox this chapter resolves. The cortisol rise around training is not damage to be avoided; it is the opening move of adaptation. But the same framework, allostatic load, that explained why chronic stress erodes health tells us something uncomfortable about training too: fitness is not built during the workout. It is built afterward, during recovery. Get the balance wrong for long enough, and the athlete meets the exact wear-and-tear physiology we have spent this course dissecting, only now it is self-inflicted and dressed up as discipline.

The resolution turns on a single word this chapter will keep returning to: balance. A stressor that is imposed and then relieved builds capacity; the same stressor imposed without relief erodes it. Nothing about the cortisol molecule changes between those two cases. What changes is the ratio of load to recovery, and with it the direction the physiology travels. That is why the popular project of driving cortisol as low as possible is not just unnecessary but incoherent. It targets the messenger while ignoring the only variable that actually decides whether training makes an athlete or breaks one. Watch, in particular, what happens to muscle, because muscle is where the ledger of this balance is kept most honestly: protein torn down under load, rebuilt during recovery, and quietly lost when the recovery never comes.

To keep this concrete, we will follow one learner through the chapter. Freya is 38 and enrolled in this course after a training block went sideways. She runs and lifts several days a week, and for two years the plan she followed made her steadily fitter. Then, chasing a personal-best half marathon, she added volume, cut a rest day, and started sleeping worse under the pressure of a busy quarter at work. Instead of getting faster she got slower, flatter, and irritable, and a saliva panel she bought online showed a cortisol value she was told was "elevated." Her first instinct, shaped by the same marketing this course keeps dismantling, was that cortisol was the problem and the fix was to crush it. Freya is not a patient here and she is not a case to be solved. She is a stand-in for the honest confusion that surrounds training and stress, and by the end of this chapter you will read her situation more clearly than the panel that alarmed her did. Her story is a thread, not a diagnosis, and we will be careful about that difference the whole way through.

Exercise as a deliberate stressor

Recall the core logic of the stress response from earlier chapters. A challenge is detected, the HPA axis and the sympathoadrenal system are activated, and cortisol is secreted to redirect fuel and modulate immune and inflammatory signaling. Exercise fits this template exactly. A single bout of endurance work produces an acute, dose-dependent rise in plasma cortisol; the size of that rise tracks intensity and duration, with a well-documented threshold below which the HPA axis barely stirs (Athanasiou et al., 2022). Below roughly moderate intensity, the body treats the effort as ordinary metabolic housekeeping and the stress axis stays quiet. Cross that threshold, into hard, sustained, or high-volume work, and the axis engages in proportion to the demand. Resistance exercise does the same, its cortisol response scaling with the combination of load and volume. Short, heavy, low-volume sets with long rests do relatively little, while high-volume, short-rest protocols that accumulate metabolic stress drive cortisol sharply upward.

It is worth being precise about what "the stress system" means here, because cortisol never acts alone. A demanding session recruits two coupled arms. The fast arm is the sympathoadrenal system, which within seconds floods the circulation with the catecholamines adrenaline and noradrenaline, sharpening the heart, dilating the airways, and mobilizing fuel. The slower arm is the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which over minutes layers cortisol on top, sustaining the fuel supply and restraining the inflammatory signaling that the mechanical work sets off. The two arms are why the acute physiology of a hard interval and the acute physiology of a fright look so similar under the hood: both run the same emergency program. The difference that matters for an athlete is entirely downstream, in whether the program is allowed to switch off and be paid for, which is the theme of every section that follows.

This is adaptive stress in its clearest form. The acute cortisol pulse is part of the signal that tells tissue something demanding just happened and that the organism should prepare to handle it better next time. It is not a side effect of training that we tolerate; it is part of the message that drives the response. Crucially, the response is not fixed, it is trainable. Athanasiou and colleagues (2022) summarize a large literature showing that regular training reshapes the HPA axis itself: basal cortisol rhythms are modified, and the axis becomes more responsive to acute challenge. The trained body does not just have bigger muscles or a larger stroke volume; it has a differently tuned stress system. This is the same lesson from the very first chapter of the course, restated in an athletic register: cortisol's meaning lives in its pattern and its regulation, not in a single number on a chart.

The trainability of the axis deserves a moment on its own, because it overturns the intuition that repeated stress must simply wear a system down. A muscle exposed to progressive load grows stronger, not weaker, provided the load is recovered from. Athanasiou and colleagues (2022) describe the HPA axis behaving in a broadly similar way: regular, recovered training does not exhaust the stress response but tunes it, so that the trained athlete mounts a cleaner, better-regulated cortisol answer to a genuine challenge and returns to baseline more efficiently afterward. The stress system, in short, adapts to being stressed, exactly as tissues do. This is the strongest possible rebuttal to the idea that the goal of training is to minimize the cortisol it produces. A well-trained stress axis is not a quiet one. It is a responsive one, quick to rise when a real demand arrives and quick to stand down when the demand passes.

Freya had been treating her training almost as if the cortisol it produced were an unfortunate cost, something to minimize by keeping every session gentle. That instinct gets the physiology backward. The pulse of cortisol around a genuinely demanding session is not the tax on adaptation. It is closer to the invoice that triggers it. Remove the stimulus entirely and you remove the signal to adapt, which is precisely why detraining, weeks of doing nothing, shrinks the very capacities she was trying to protect.

The Endocrine and Metabolic Responses on Overtraining Syndrome study, known by its acronym EROS, makes this concrete and gives us one of the most important findings in the chapter. Cadegiani and Kater (2017b), using a cosyntropin stimulation test, an insulin tolerance test, and salivary cortisol rhythms, compared HPA axis function across three groups: athletes with overtraining syndrome, healthy athletes, and sedentary controls. The healthy athletes did not merely tolerate the stress of training, they showed enhanced cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) responses to challenge compared with sedentary people. Adrenocorticotropic hormone is the pituitary signal that tells the adrenal cortex to release cortisol, so a larger ACTH response to a standardized stimulus means the whole axis is primed to answer a real demand vigorously. Their stress axis had been conditioned, like a muscle, into a more robust responder. This is the physiological fingerprint of successful adaptation, and it reframes everything: a vigorous cortisol response to a genuine challenge is a sign the system is working, not failing.

Hold that finding against Freya's saliva panel. A number flagged as "elevated" told her almost nothing on its own, because a trained, adapting body is supposed to mount a strong cortisol response, and a robust morning value can be a marker of a healthy rhythm rather than a broken one. Whether her result meant adaptation or the beginning of trouble depended on context the panel did not capture: her training history, her sleep, her mood, her performance trend, and the shape of the rhythm across the day rather than a single reading. This is the recurring trap of reducing a system to one measurement, and it is worth pausing on before we go further.

If a healthy trained athlete shows a larger cortisol response to a stimulation test than a sedentary person, why would it be a mistake to interpret "high cortisol" as automatically harmful? What context would you need before drawing any conclusion about Freya's single saliva value?

Recovery is where adaptation happens

Here is the pivot that separates people who understand training physiology from people who merely accumulate fatigue. During the session, you are net catabolic: cortisol is elevated, protein breakdown outpaces synthesis, glycogen is depleted, micro-damage accumulates in the working tissue. Nothing is being built. The supercompensation, the rebound to a level of capacity slightly above where you started, occurs afterward, when the stressor is removed and anabolic signaling takes over. Adaptation is the body's response to the stimulus, not the stimulus itself. The workout writes the order; recovery fills it.

This is worth stating carefully because it inverts a common intuition. Many people believe, implicitly, that the workout is the improvement, that each session deposits a little fitness directly, the way each dollar deposited raises a bank balance. The physiology is closer to the opposite. Each session first makes you temporarily worse, more fatigued, more damaged, less capable, and the improvement only appears during the hours and days afterward when the body repairs the damage and, if given the chance, overshoots slightly to prepare for a repeat. The training does not deposit fitness. It requests it. Whether the request is granted depends on what happens next.

At the tissue level this is not a metaphor but a measurable sequence. A bout of resistance exercise elevates the rate of muscle protein synthesis for roughly 24 to 48 hours afterward, and for that window synthesis can outpace the breakdown that dominated during the session itself, tipping the muscle into a net positive balance only if amino acids and rest are supplied. The building, in other words, is scheduled for the recovery days, and it has a clock. Cortisol is part of what governs the other side of that ledger: it is a permissive brake on synthesis and a promoter of proteolysis, the controlled breakdown of muscle protein into amino acids the body can burn or repurpose. In an acute, self-limited pulse that brake is harmless and even useful, clearing damaged protein so fresh protein can replace it. Sustained, it becomes the mechanism by which chronic under-recovery visibly costs an athlete muscle, the physical signature of a balance tilted too long toward breakdown.

This maps directly onto the fitness-fatigue model, a two-factor framework long used in sport science. Every training session deposits two things simultaneously: a durable, slowly decaying "fitness" component and a large, quickly decaying "fatigue" component. Measured performance at any moment is roughly fitness minus fatigue. Immediately after a hard session, fatigue dominates and performance dips. As fatigue clears faster than fitness, performance rises above baseline, if you gave it the time. Stack another session on top before fatigue clears and you never see the rebound; you only see the accumulating deficit. This is exactly the trap Freya fell into. By cutting a rest day, she did not add fitness, she removed the window in which the previous sessions' fitness could surface from underneath the fatigue that was masking it.

The overtraining continuum is fundamentally the story of an imbalance between training stress and recovery, not of training being intrinsically dangerous.

Paraphrasing Meeusen et al. (2013), ECSS/ACSM Consensus Statement

Notice that this is the allostatic-load framework wearing athletic clothing. In Chapter 5 we described allostatic load as the cumulative cost of repeated activation without adequate recovery, the price of the stress response staying "on." Training is precisely a program of repeated activation. Whether it produces fitness or damage depends entirely on whether the recovery cost is being paid down between activations. Training, in other words, is a controlled experiment in allostatic load. The independent variable is the load-to-recovery ratio, and the athlete gets to set it. That last point is what makes exercise such a clean teaching case for this whole course: unlike a demanding job or a strained relationship, the stressor here is deliberately titrated, scheduled, and measured, which means the recovery side can be titrated, scheduled, and measured too.

It also reframes the levers that matter. The popular conversation about training obsesses over the session: which exercises, which intensity, which fashionable protocol. The physiology says the more decisive variables are frequently the ones that sit between sessions, sleep, fuel, and time, because those are what determine whether the fitness component ever gets to surface. Freya had spent months optimizing her workouts and almost no attention on the gaps between them. The overtraining continuum: overreaching versus overtraining

When recovery lags load, the athlete does not fall off a cliff, they slide down a graded continuum. The consensus statement issued jointly by the European College of Sport Science (ECSS) and the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) gave the field its standardized vocabulary (Meeusen et al., 2013), and it is worth learning precisely because casual usage blurs these categories badly. In everyday gym talk, "overtraining" gets applied to a single tired week, which is a bit like calling every low mood clinical depression. The consensus vocabulary exists to keep three genuinely different states distinct.

Functional overreaching (FOR)

Functional overreaching, abbreviated FOR, is a planned, short-term performance decrement caused by a deliberate spike in training load. The athlete feels flat, tests slightly worse, and knows it. This is not a mistake; it is the setup for a rebound. After a period of reduced load, a taper or a lighter week, performance rebounds to a level above where it started. Functional overreaching followed by adequate recovery is the engine of periodized training. The dip is the point. Coaches deliberately induce it, then unload to harvest the supercompensation. When Freya first started making gains two years ago, she was almost certainly cycling through mild functional overreaching without a name for it: harder weeks followed by easier ones, each easier week revealing a new best.

Non-functional overreaching (NFOR)

Non-functional overreaching, abbreviated NFOR, is what functional overreaching becomes when recovery is inadequate. The performance decrement persists for weeks to months, and the expected supercompensation never arrives. Now the dip is no longer a setup for anything, it is stagnation with mounting fatigue, disturbed mood, and hormonal perturbation. The athlete is working hard and getting worse. The critical difference between functional and non-functional overreaching is not the training itself but what follows it: the same overload week produces functional overreaching when a genuine unloading period follows and non-functional overreaching when it does not. Freya's failed block, added volume, a cut rest day, worse sleep, and a slide from faster to slower, is the textbook picture of overreaching that stopped being functional because nothing was allowed to recover.

Overtraining syndrome (OTS)

Overtraining syndrome, abbreviated OTS, sits at the severe end: a prolonged, sometimes months-long maladaptation across biological, neurochemical, and hormonal regulatory systems. Kreher and Schwartz (2012) frame overtraining syndrome deliberately as a multi-system syndrome, neurologic, endocrinologic, and immunologic disturbances coupled with persistent mood changes, not a mere performance problem. The keyword in the consensus definition is "prolonged maladaptation" (Meeusen et al., 2013). Recovery from true overtraining syndrome is measured in months, and it is a diagnosis of exclusion, made by clinicians only after other explanations, such as illness, iron deficiency, thyroid dysfunction, or depression, have been ruled out. That detail matters: the same fatigue, flatness, and performance loss that mark overtraining syndrome are produced by many treatable medical conditions, which is exactly why the call belongs to a clinician and not a coach with a spreadsheet.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

That boundary is not a legal footnote; it is where this course draws the line between education and medicine. As a learner you can explain the physiology and describe monitoring concepts. Functional overreaching is a normal, plannable state you can recognize and reason about. Overtraining syndrome is a clinical condition you recognize the warning signs of and refer, precisely because distinguishing it from the medical conditions that mimic it requires tests and judgment that sit outside this course. Keeping those two acts separate, recognizing a pattern versus diagnosing a syndrome, is a habit worth building deliberately, and it is one Freya's story keeps testing.

The practical stakes of that boundary are easy to see in Freya's position. Suppose her flatness had a cause that had nothing to do with training at all, an underactive thyroid, low iron stores, an unrecognized mood disorder, or a simmering infection. Each of those can produce the same fatigue and performance loss that mark the far end of the overtraining continuum, and each has a specific treatment that no amount of unloading or extra sleep would supply. A coach or a self-directed learner working from a spreadsheet cannot tell these apart from genuine overtraining syndrome, because telling them apart requires the very tests and clinical judgment that sit outside this course. That is not a limitation to apologize for. It is the reason the boundary exists. Recognizing that a pattern has persisted past the point where self-managed recovery should have resolved it is exactly the moment to hand the question to someone equipped to diagnose it.

What actually distinguishes these states physiologically? Here the endocrine data are subtle and, at first, disappointing. Cadegiani and Kater (2017a), in a systematic review conducted under the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, abbreviated PRISMA, and covering 38 studies, reported that basal hormone levels in overtrained athletes are frequently normal. A resting blood draw often looks unremarkable. This is a genuinely important and counterintuitive result: the athlete can be deep in trouble while a standard resting panel reads within range. The signal appears under provocation. Stimulation tests reveal blunted growth-hormone and ACTH responses in overtrained athletes. Combined with the EROS finding that healthy athletes show enhanced HPA responsiveness (Cadegiani & Kater, 2017b), a coherent picture emerges: HPA axis responsiveness, not resting cortisol, is the functional marker of training state. Adaptation looks like a system that responds vigorously and then recovers; maladaptation looks like a system that has gone quiet and flat under challenge, as though the axis itself has been worn down.

The search for a single hormonal marker of this slide has a long history, and it is instructive precisely because it has repeatedly disappointed. In an influential early review, Urhausen, Gabriel, and Kindermann (1995) surveyed the blood markers proposed for training stress and concluded that no resting hormone reliably separates the overtrained athlete from the merely well-trained one. The most durable idea to survive from that literature is a ratio rather than a level: the testosterone-to-cortisol ratio, offered as an index of the balance between anabolic and catabolic drive. That ratio falls with the intensity and duration of hard training and can be restored by rest, which makes it a plausible readout of whether an athlete is building or breaking down. Crucially, though, Urhausen and colleagues framed it as a marker of the strain of training rather than a diagnostic test for overtraining syndrome. The ratio tracks the load an athlete is carrying; it does not, on its own, announce that the athlete has crossed into pathology. That distinction, between a marker of strain and a diagnosis of disease, is one this chapter will not let go of.

This is why Freya's single saliva value could never settle the question of where on the continuum she sat. A resting number, high or low, is close to the least informative measurement available for this purpose. What would actually be informative, the shape of her response to a standardized challenge, is not something a mail-order panel measures, and interpreting it is clinical work. The educational point stands on its own, though: the difference between adapting and breaking down is written in responsiveness, and responsiveness is a property of a system over time, not a value you can read off a Tuesday-morning tube of saliva.

DHEA: cortisol's counterweight on the same production line

Until now this course has treated the adrenal cortex almost as if cortisol were its only product. It is time to formally introduce the other major adrenal steroid: dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and its sulfated storage form, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S). Recall the layered steroidogenic factory from Chapter 2, the adrenal cortex's zonated pathway that starts from cholesterol and branches into mineralocorticoids, glucocorticoids, and androgens. DHEA is the principal adrenal androgen precursor, produced in the zona reticularis from the same pregnenolone-derived backbone that yields cortisol. Two hormones, one production line, sharing precursors and regulatory input. When the pituitary sends its ACTH signal, it is not addressing cortisol alone; it is turning up a factory whose outputs include DHEA as well, which is why the two so often move together in the short term.

Functionally, DHEA is often cast as cortisol's anabolic counterweight. Where cortisol is catabolic, mobilizing fuel, breaking down protein, restraining growth signaling, DHEA is associated with anabolic, tissue-building, restorative processes and serves as a precursor to downstream androgens and estrogens. This opposition is why the cortisol-to-DHEA ratio has attracted so much interest as a candidate marker of the body's overall anabolic-catabolic balance. The idea is intuitive: it is not the absolute level of either hormone that captures your state, but the tug-of-war between them. A rising cortisol-to-DHEA ratio suggests the catabolic side is winning; a falling ratio suggests recovery and building are ascendant. The ratio is attractive precisely because it survives the problem that sank the single resting value: even if both hormones drift together, their balance can still shift in a way that carries information.

The cortisol-to-DHEA ratio is best understood as a close cousin of the older testosterone-to-cortisol ratio we just met. Both set a catabolic hormone against an anabolic one and ask which way the balance tips. DHEA has a particular appeal in this role because it shares not just a general anabolic character with testosterone but an actual production line with cortisol, arising in the adrenal cortex from the same precursors under the same ACTH signal. When the ratio between two products of one factory shifts, that shift is unlikely to be an artifact of the factory simply running faster or slower overall; it points to a change in how the outputs are being partitioned. That is the conceptual payoff of a ratio, and it is why researchers keep returning to these paired measures despite their messiness. Still, the same humility applies. A ratio is a more informative window than a single value, not a diagnostic verdict, and it earns its keep only when read as a trend across weeks rather than a snapshot from one morning.

A word of caution belongs here, because the ratio is easy to oversell. It is a promising research marker and a useful conceptual lens, not a validated diagnostic test with clean cutoffs you can act on. Assay methods vary, values swing across the day and the menstrual cycle, and no single ratio reading tells you where an individual sits. Treat it the way this course treats every biomarker: as one imperfect window onto a system, most meaningful as a trend over time and never as a verdict on its own. That caveat is itself part of the education. Learning what a marker cannot tell you is as important as learning what it can.

The dynamics around a single hard session illustrate how tightly these two hormones are coupled. Hackney, Wilber, and colleagues (2024) tracked DHEA, DHEA-S, cortisol, and testosterone across exercise and recovery in female endurance athletes. At the point of volitional fatigue, DHEA and DHEA-S rose along with cortisol, the anabolic and catabolic steroids surged together, consistent with their shared adrenal origin and shared drive. On its own, that co-movement might look reassuring, as if the building hormones were keeping pace with the breaking-down ones. But the recovery phase revealed the more important story: the magnitude of the cortisol rise at fatigue predicted the suppression of DHEA, DHEA-S, and testosterone twenty-four hours later. The bigger the catabolic spike during the effort, the more the anabolic hormones were still knocked down a full day afterward, meaning resting endocrine equilibrium had not been re-established within 24 hours.

That last point deserves emphasis for anyone thinking about training frequency. "Recovered enough to train again" and "returned to hormonal baseline" are not the same claim. The Hackney data suggest that after a genuinely fatiguing session, the anabolic-catabolic balance can remain tilted toward the catabolic side beyond a day. Stack the next hard session on top of that residual tilt, session after session, and you have a physiological mechanism for how the overtraining continuum actually accumulates. This is Freya's failed block described at the hormonal level: each hard day likely landed while the previous day's anabolic suppression had not yet lifted, so the tilt never reset and instead compounded. Nothing about any single session was pathological. The damage was in the spacing, which is exactly what the load-recovery framing predicts.

Under-recovery is a systems problem: sleep, muscle, and mood

The overtraining literature insists on a multi-system view for a reason: under-recovery does not confine itself to a performance chart. It reaches into exactly the domains this course has already mapped, metabolism, sleep, and mood, and it does so through cortisol-mediated mechanisms you now recognize. This is why the syndrome at the end of the continuum is defined across systems rather than by a single failing organ, and it is why the earliest warning signs so rarely show up first as a slower time on the track.

Muscle as the ledger of catabolic tone

Of all the tissues that record the load-recovery balance, skeletal muscle keeps the plainest account. Cortisol is, among its many roles, a catabolic signal for muscle: it promotes the breakdown of muscle protein into amino acids and restrains the machinery that builds new protein, part of its ancient job of keeping fuel available when demand is high. During a single hard session this is appropriate and reversible, a brief tilt toward breakdown that the recovery period more than repays. The problem is chronic exposure. When cortisol tone stays elevated because recovery never arrives, the brief tilt becomes a standing one, and synthesis fails to catch up with breakdown day after day. The visible result, in the extreme, is the loss of the very muscle an athlete trained to build, along with the strength and power that depend on it. This is why unexplained loss of muscle or a stalling of strength gains is treated in the monitoring literature as a warning sign rather than a plateau. It is the ledger showing, in the most tangible currency available, that the catabolic side has been winning for too long. Read this way, muscle is not just the thing training builds. It is one of the honest gauges of whether the training is being recovered from at all.

Sleep and the catabolic environment

The link between recovery and sleep is not metaphorical. Lamon and colleagues (2021), in a controlled crossover experiment, subjected healthy young adults to a single night of total sleep deprivation and measured the consequences directly. Muscle protein fractional synthesis rate fell by 18 percent. Plasma cortisol rose by 21 percent. Testosterone dropped by roughly a quarter. In one night, sleep loss produced a measurably catabolic hormonal environment, higher cortisol, lower anabolic drive, slower muscle building. This is the same cortisol-mediated protein catabolism from the metabolism chapter, now triggered by a training input coaches routinely underestimate: the athlete's sleep.

Sit with the direction of those numbers, because they line up precisely with everything else in this chapter. A single bad night pushes cortisol up and the muscle-building machinery down, which is the same anabolic-catabolic tilt the Hackney data traced after a hard session. Now imagine the two stacked, a hard session followed by a short night, repeated across a busy training block. That was Freya's block exactly. The added volume raised the demand for recovery at the same time the work pressure and lost sleep cut the supply of it. If recovery is where adaptation lives, and sleep loss both raises cortisol and directly suppresses muscle protein synthesis, then poor sleep is not a lifestyle footnote to a training plan, it is a load on the same scale as the training itself. A learner who tracks every set and rep but ignores sleep is measuring one side of a two-sided ledger.

The mechanism runs deeper than a single night's hormone panel. Much of the body's daily pulse of growth hormone, a powerfully anabolic signal, is released during the slow-wave stages of early-night sleep, the very stages that shorten first when sleep is cut or fragmented. Sleep is therefore not a passive gap in which nothing happens but an active anabolic window with its own hormonal schedule, one that overlaps precisely with when the muscle repair requested by training is meant to be carried out. Curtail it and you do two things at once: you raise the catabolic signal, cortisol, and you clip the anabolic one, growth hormone, pushing the same balance the whole chapter tracks in the wrong direction from both ends. For an athlete adding training load during a stressful stretch of life, this is the quiet trap. The load rises exactly when the sleep that would pay for it is most likely to be sacrificed to the same busy schedule that raised the load.

Mood as a monitoring signal

Persistent mood disturbance is one of the most consistent features across the overtraining continuum (Kreher & Schwartz, 2012; Meeusen et al., 2013). Elevated fatigue, irritability, loss of motivation, and depressed affect track the slide from functional overreaching into non-functional overreaching and overtraining syndrome, often before performance decrements are unambiguous. This connects back to the mood chapter's account of how chronic HPA activation and disrupted cortisol rhythms interact with the brain's affective circuitry. The practical implication is striking: the irritability and flatness Freya first dismissed as "just being stressed about work" may have been among the earliest and most sensitive signals that her training had crossed from functional into non-functional overreaching. In athlete monitoring, subjective mood and perceived fatigue frequently move earlier and more sensitively than any single hormone assay, which is one reason validated questionnaires remain central despite the marketing appeal of biomarkers. The cheapest, fastest instrument in the whole monitoring toolkit is often a short, honest self-report answered consistently.

This is not soft science standing in for hard measurement. Validated mood instruments such as the Profile of Mood States have a long track record in sport monitoring, and the pattern they detect, rising fatigue and falling vigor as load accumulates, has been observed reliably enough that mood is treated as a genuine early-warning channel rather than a courtesy question. The physiology makes sense of this. The same circuits that cortisol and chronic HPA activation act on, the ones this course mapped in the mood chapter, are the circuits that govern motivation, irritability, and the sense of effort. It should not surprise us that they register a training imbalance before a stopwatch does. For Freya, the flatness and short temper she attributed to work were arguably her most sensitive instruments, reporting a problem her performance times would only confirm weeks later. The mistake was not that she lacked data. It was that she discounted the data her own mood was handing her.

Monitoring load without a laboratory

If resting hormones are uninformative and stimulation tests are clinical, what can a learner actually reason about? A great deal, as it turns out, and most of it requires no laboratory. The most widely validated field method for tracking training stress is the session rating of perceived exertion, or session RPE, introduced by Foster and colleagues (2001). The rating of perceived exertion (RPE) is simply the athlete's rated hardness of a session on a standardized scale; multiply that rating by the session's duration in minutes and you get a single internal-load number in arbitrary units. Summed across a week, and tracked against how that week actually felt and performed, session RPE captures the accumulation the fitness-fatigue model describes, and it does so cheaply, consistently, and without the interpretive traps of a single hormone value. Haddad and colleagues (2017), reviewing the method, found it a valid and ecologically useful index of internal training load across many sports.

The point is not to prescribe a monitoring protocol, that would cross the line this chapter keeps drawing, but to show that the concepts in this course translate into observable, reasoned tracking rather than a fixation on a lab number. Load rises, felt recovery lags, mood dips, performance stalls: those four moving together over weeks is a far richer and more trustworthy picture than any resting cortisol reading, and every element of it is available without a blood draw.

Heart rate variability as an autonomic window

Session rating of perceived exertion measures the load going in. A complementary family of tools tries to read the recovery coming out, and the most studied of these is heart rate variability (HRV), the beat-to-beat variation in the timing of the heartbeat. Because that variation is shaped largely by the parasympathetic, or vagal, branch of the autonomic nervous system, the branch associated with rest and recovery, HRV offers an indirect window onto whether the body has returned to a recovered, parasympathetically dominant state or is still running in a sympathetically driven one. In broad terms, a well-recovered athlete tends to show higher vagally mediated HRV, and a slide toward under-recovery is often accompanied by a fall in it. Plews and colleagues (2013), reviewing HRV in elite endurance athletes, endorse the basic logic while adding a sharp caution that echoes everything else in this chapter: the relationship is neither simple nor uniform, single-day readings are noisy, and in elite athletes the picture is genuinely equivocal, with both increases and decreases in HRV reported alongside negative adaptation. Their practical recommendation is to track a rolling weekly average rather than react to any one morning's number, and to interpret HRV alongside training load and how the athlete actually feels rather than in isolation. The lesson generalizes past HRV to every monitoring tool in this chapter. No single autonomic or hormonal signal is a verdict; each is one noisy channel, most trustworthy as a trend and most meaningful when several channels are read together.

The cortisol awakening response as a composite monitor

Can we monitor training state from the cortisol rhythm itself? The cortisol awakening response (CAR), the sharp rise in cortisol in the first 30 to 45 minutes after waking that you first met in Chapter 1, has been proposed as exactly such a biomarker. Anderson and Wideman (2017), reviewing 32 studies, reached a carefully hedged conclusion: a threshold of exercise intensity may be required before the HPA axis and the cortisol awakening response shift at all, and the response appears to encode a composite of both prior training load and anticipated upcoming stress. That dual encoding is a double-edged property. It makes the cortisol awakening response a rich integrative signal, but it also means a single morning measurement cannot be cleanly attributed to yesterday's session versus tomorrow's dread. As a monitoring tool it is promising and genuinely complex, a caution against the temptation to reduce training state to any one number. It is also a neat closing of the loop: the same morning surge Chapter 1 defended as a healthy feature is, in the training context, a signal so information-dense that it resists simple reading, which is the whole lesson of this course in a single measurement.

There is a tidy symmetry worth naming before we close. Everything that builds an athlete and everything that breaks one runs through the same molecule and the same axis. The cortisol pulse that signals adaptation is the cortisol tone that, sustained, drives catabolism. The HPA responsiveness that marks a healthy trained system is the responsiveness that goes blunted and flat when the system is overwhelmed. The morning surge that Chapter 1 defended as essential is the morning surge that, pressed into service as a monitor, proves too information-dense to read simply. There is no second, villainous hormone hiding behind the useful one. There is one hormone doing one job across a spectrum of contexts, and the context, set by the balance of load and recovery, is what decides which face it shows. That is the whole argument of the course, and training is simply the cleanest place to watch it play out.

Step back and the picture is unified. A hard session raises cortisol (adaptive) and transiently suppresses anabolic hormones (Hackney et al., 2024). Recovery, especially sleep, is when the anabolic side recaptures ground and the actual building happens (Lamon et al., 2021). Chronic imbalance blunts the HPA axis's responsiveness (Cadegiani & Kater, 2017b), disturbs mood (Kreher & Schwartz, 2012), and tilts the cortisol-DHEA balance toward catabolism. Every thread of this course converges here, because training is the one stressor the athlete titrates by choice. Freya's way out was never to crush a hormone. It was to restore the balance the framework describes: unload, sleep, refuel, let the fitness that months of work had requested finally surface, and, if the flatness and fatigue did not lift, to treat that persistence as a reason to see a clinician rather than a number to fight on her own.

Key Takeaways

  • Exercise is a genuine stressor that triggers an acute, dose-dependent cortisol response, and that response is adaptive, not harmful. Regular training conditions the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis toward enhanced responsiveness in healthy athletes.
  • Fitness is built during recovery, not during the session. The fitness-fatigue model and the allostatic-load framework describe the same truth: adaptation depends on the balance between load and recovery, and the levers between sessions often matter most.
  • The overtraining continuum runs from functional overreaching (FOR), which is planned, recoverable, and leads to supercompensation, through non-functional overreaching (NFOR) to overtraining syndrome (OTS), a prolonged multi-system maladaptation.
  • Resting hormone levels in overtrained athletes are often normal; the functional marker is HPA axis responsiveness under stimulation, which is blunted in overtraining syndrome and enhanced in healthy athletes.
  • Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) is the adrenal androgen produced on the same steroidogenic pathway as cortisol and acts as an anabolic counterweight; the cortisol-to-DHEA ratio indexes anabolic-catabolic balance over time, best read as a trend rather than a verdict.
  • Under-recovery is a systems problem: sleep loss directly raises cortisol and suppresses muscle protein synthesis, mood disturbance is an early warning signal, session rating of perceived exertion (RPE) tracks internal load cheaply, heart rate variability (HRV) offers a noisy autonomic window best read as a trend, and the cortisol awakening response (CAR) is a promising but composite monitor.
  • Learners explain physiology and describe monitoring; they recognize warning signs and refer. Diagnosis of overtraining syndrome is clinical, in part because many treatable conditions mimic it.
Looking Ahead

We have watched cortisol build and, when mismanaged, break the athlete. Next we turn to the levers people actually reach for to shift their stress physiology, nutrition, so-called "adaptogens," and the crowded marketplace of interventions that promise to "lower cortisol." Armed with everything you now know about what cortisol is for, we will ask which of these claims survive contact with the evidence.
