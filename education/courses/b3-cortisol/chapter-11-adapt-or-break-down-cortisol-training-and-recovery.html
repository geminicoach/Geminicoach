Adapt or Break Down: Cortisol, Training, and Recovery

Exercise is a stressor you choose on purpose — and the same physiology that heals you can also grind you down.

60 min read 8 cited sources

A barbell does not know it is medicine. When a lifter loads the bar and drives it overhead, the body reads the event the way it reads any threat: metabolic demand spikes, the sympathetic nervous system fires, and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis — the machinery you have followed for ten chapters — releases a pulse of cortisol into the blood. Heart rate climbs. Glucose is mobilized. Muscle protein is, for a while, broken down faster than it is built. By every biochemical measure we have studied, a hard training session is a stressor.

And yet nobody would call a squat session "bad for you." That is the paradox this chapter resolves. The cortisol rise around training is not damage to be avoided; it is the opening move of adaptation. But the same framework — allostatic load — that explained why chronic stress erodes health tells us something uncomfortable about training too: fitness is not built during the workout. It is built afterward, during recovery. Get the balance wrong for long enough, and the athlete meets the exact wear-and-tear physiology we have spent this course dissecting, only now it is self-inflicted and dressed up as discipline.

Exercise as a deliberate stressor

Recall the core logic of the stress response from earlier chapters. A challenge is detected, the HPA axis and sympathoadrenal system are activated, and cortisol is secreted to redirect fuel and modulate immune and inflammatory signaling. Exercise fits this template exactly. A single bout of endurance work produces an acute, dose-dependent rise in plasma cortisol; the size of that rise tracks intensity and duration, with a well-documented threshold below which the HPA axis barely stirs (Athanasiou et al., 2022). Resistance exercise does the same, its cortisol response scaling with the combination of load and volume — short, heavy, low-volume sets do relatively little, while high-volume, short-rest protocols that accumulate metabolic stress drive cortisol sharply upward.

This is adaptive stress in its clearest form. The acute cortisol pulse is part of the signal that tells tissue something demanding just happened and that the organism should prepare to handle it better next time. Crucially, the response is not fixed — it is trainable. Athanasiou and colleagues (2022) summarize a large literature showing that regular training reshapes the HPA axis itself: basal cortisol rhythms are modified, and the axis becomes more responsive to acute challenge. The trained body does not just have bigger muscles; it has a differently tuned stress system.

The EROS study makes this concrete and gives us one of the most important findings in the chapter. Cadegiani and Kater (2017b), using a cosyntropin stimulation test, an insulin tolerance test, and salivary cortisol rhythms, compared HPA axis function across three groups: athletes with overtraining syndrome, healthy athletes, and sedentary controls. The healthy athletes did not merely tolerate the stress of training — they showed enhanced cortisol and ACTH responses to challenge compared with sedentary people. Their stress axis had been conditioned, like a muscle, into a more robust responder. This is the physiological fingerprint of successful adaptation, and it reframes everything: a vigorous cortisol response to a genuine challenge is a sign the system is working, not failing.

If a healthy trained athlete shows a larger cortisol response to a stimulation test than a sedentary person, why would it be a mistake to interpret "high cortisol" as automatically harmful? What context would you need before drawing any conclusion?

Recovery is where adaptation happens

Here is the pivot that separates people who understand training physiology from people who merely accumulate fatigue. During the session, you are net catabolic: cortisol is elevated, protein breakdown outpaces synthesis, glycogen is depleted, micro-damage accumulates. Nothing is being built. The supercompensation — the rebound to a level of capacity slightly above where you started — occurs afterward, when the stressor is removed and anabolic signaling takes over. Adaptation is the body's response to the stimulus, not the stimulus itself.

This maps directly onto the fitness–fatigue model, a two-factor framework long used in sport science. Every training session deposits two things simultaneously: a durable, slowly decaying "fitness" component and a large, quickly decaying "fatigue" component. Measured performance at any moment is roughly fitness minus fatigue. Immediately after a hard session, fatigue dominates and performance dips. As fatigue clears faster than fitness, performance rises above baseline — if you gave it the time. Stack another session on top before fatigue clears and you never see the rebound; you only see the accumulating deficit.

The overtraining continuum is fundamentally the story of an imbalance between training stress and recovery — not of training being intrinsically dangerous.

Paraphrasing Meeusen et al. (2013), ECSS/ACSM Consensus Statement

Notice that this is the allostatic-load framework wearing athletic clothing. In Chapter 5 we described allostatic load as the cumulative cost of repeated activation without adequate recovery — the price of the stress response staying "on." Training is precisely a program of repeated activation. Whether it produces fitness or damage depends entirely on whether the recovery cost is being paid down between activations. Training, in other words, is a controlled experiment in allostatic load. The independent variable is the load–recovery ratio, and the athlete gets to set it.

The overtraining continuum: overreaching versus overtraining

When recovery lags load, the athlete does not fall off a cliff — they slide down a graded continuum. The ECSS/ACSM consensus statement (Meeusen et al., 2013) gave the field its standardized vocabulary, and it is worth learning precisely because casual usage blurs these categories badly.

Functional overreaching (FOR)

Functional overreaching is a planned, short-term performance decrement caused by a deliberate spike in training load. The athlete feels flat, tests slightly worse, and knows it. This is not a mistake; it is the setup for a rebound. After a period of reduced load — a taper or a lighter week — performance rebounds to a level above where it started. FOR followed by adequate recovery is the engine of periodized training. The dip is the point.

Non-functional overreaching (NFOR)

Non-functional overreaching is what FOR becomes when recovery is inadequate. The performance decrement persists for weeks to months, and the expected supercompensation never arrives. Now the dip is no longer a setup for anything — it is stagnation with mounting fatigue, disturbed mood, and hormonal perturbation. The athlete is working hard and getting worse.

Overtraining syndrome (OTS)

Overtraining syndrome sits at the severe end: a prolonged, sometimes months-long maladaptation across biological, neurochemical, and hormonal regulatory systems. Kreher and Schwartz (2012) frame OTS deliberately as a multi-system syndrome — neurologic, endocrinologic, and immunologic disturbances coupled with persistent mood changes — not a mere performance problem. The keyword in the consensus definition is "prolonged maladaptation" (Meeusen et al., 2013). Recovery from true OTS is measured in months, and it is a diagnosis of exclusion made by clinicians, not coaches.

Scope note

This distinction matters for your practice. FOR is a normal, plannable state; OTS is a clinical condition. As an educator you can explain the physiology and describe monitoring concepts, but you do not diagnose overtraining syndrome — you recognize warning signs, and you refer. That boundary is not a legal footnote; it is where this course draws the line between education and medicine.

What actually distinguishes these states physiologically? Here the endocrine data are subtle and, at first, disappointing. Cadegiani and Kater (2017a), in a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 38 studies, reported that basal hormone levels in overtrained athletes are frequently normal — a resting blood draw often looks unremarkable. The signal appears under provocation. Stimulation tests reveal blunted growth-hormone and ACTH responses in overtrained athletes. Combined with the EROS finding that healthy athletes show enhanced HPA responsiveness (Cadegiani & Kater, 2017b), a coherent picture emerges: HPA axis responsiveness — not resting cortisol — is the functional marker of training state. Adaptation looks like a system that responds vigorously and recovers; maladaptation looks like a system that has gone quiet and flat under challenge.

DHEA: cortisol's counterweight on the same production line

Until now this course has treated the adrenal cortex almost as if cortisol were its only product. It is time to formally introduce the other major adrenal steroid: DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone) and its sulfated storage form, DHEA-S. Recall the layered steroidogenic factory from Chapter 2 — the adrenal cortex's zonated pathway that starts from cholesterol and branches into mineralocorticoids, glucocorticoids, and androgens. DHEA is the principal adrenal androgen precursor, produced in the zona reticularis from the same pregnenolone-derived backbone that yields cortisol. Two hormones, one production line, sharing precursors and regulatory input.

Functionally, DHEA is often cast as cortisol's anabolic counterweight. Where cortisol is catabolic — mobilizing fuel, breaking down protein, restraining growth signaling — DHEA is associated with anabolic, tissue-building, restorative processes. This opposition is why the cortisol-to-DHEA ratio has attracted so much interest as a candidate marker of the body's overall anabolic–catabolic balance. The idea is intuitive: it is not the absolute level of either hormone that captures your state, but the tug-of-war between them. A rising cortisol:DHEA ratio suggests the catabolic side is winning; a falling ratio suggests recovery and building are ascendant.

The dynamics around a single hard session illustrate how tightly these two hormones are coupled. Hackney, Wilber, and colleagues (2024) tracked DHEA, DHEA-S, cortisol, and testosterone across exercise and recovery in female endurance athletes. At the point of volitional fatigue, DHEA and DHEA-S rose along with cortisol — the anabolic and catabolic steroids surged together, consistent with their shared adrenal origin and shared drive. But the recovery phase revealed the more important story: the magnitude of the cortisol rise at fatigue predicted the suppression of DHEA, DHEA-S, and testosterone twenty-four hours later. The bigger the catabolic spike during the effort, the more the anabolic hormones were still knocked down a full day afterward — meaning resting endocrine equilibrium had not been re-established within 24 hours.

That last point deserves emphasis for anyone thinking about training frequency. "Recovered enough to train again" and "returned to hormonal baseline" are not the same claim. The Hackney data suggest that after a genuinely fatiguing session, the anabolic–catabolic balance can remain tilted toward the catabolic side beyond a day. Stack the next hard session on top of that residual tilt, session after session, and you have a physiological mechanism for how the overtraining continuum actually accumulates.

Think About It

DHEA and cortisol both come from the same adrenal pathway and rise together during hard exercise. Why, then, might their ratio over days-to-weeks tell you something that neither hormone tells you on its own? What is the ratio capturing that a single value misses?

Under-recovery is a systems problem: sleep, muscle, and mood

The overtraining literature insists on a multi-system view for a reason: under-recovery does not confine itself to a performance chart. It reaches into exactly the domains this course has already mapped — metabolism, sleep, and mood — and it does so through cortisol-mediated mechanisms you now recognize.

Sleep and the catabolic environment

The link between recovery and sleep is not metaphorical. Lamon and colleagues (2021), in a controlled crossover experiment, subjected healthy young adults to a single night of total sleep deprivation and measured the consequences directly. Muscle protein fractional synthesis rate fell by 18%. Plasma cortisol rose by 21%. Testosterone dropped. In one night, sleep loss produced a measurably catabolic hormonal environment — higher cortisol, lower anabolic drive, slower muscle building. This is the same cortisol-mediated protein catabolism from the metabolism chapter, now triggered by a training input coaches routinely underestimate: the athlete's sleep. If recovery is where adaptation lives, and sleep loss both raises cortisol and directly suppresses muscle protein synthesis, then poor sleep is not a lifestyle footnote to a training plan — it is a load on the same scale.

Mood as a monitoring signal

Persistent mood disturbance is one of the most consistent features across the overtraining continuum (Kreher & Schwartz, 2012; Meeusen et al., 2013). Elevated fatigue, irritability, loss of motivation, and depressed affect track the slide from functional overreaching into non-functional overreaching and OTS — often before performance decrements are unambiguous. This connects back to the mood chapter's account of how chronic HPA activation and disrupted cortisol rhythms interact with the brain's affective circuitry. In practical monitoring, subjective mood and perceived fatigue frequently move earlier and more sensitively than any single hormone assay, which is one reason validated questionnaires remain central to athlete monitoring despite the appeal of biomarkers.

The cortisol awakening response as a composite monitor

Can we monitor training state from the cortisol rhythm itself? The cortisol awakening response (CAR) — the sharp rise in cortisol in the first 30–45 minutes after waking — has been proposed as exactly such a biomarker. Anderson and Wideman (2017), reviewing 32 studies, reached a carefully hedged conclusion: a threshold of exercise intensity may be required before the HPA axis and CAR shift at all, and the CAR appears to encode a composite of both prior training load and anticipated upcoming stress. That dual encoding is a double-edged property. It makes the CAR a rich integrative signal, but it also means a single morning measurement cannot be cleanly attributed to yesterday's session versus tomorrow's dread. As a monitoring tool it is promising and genuinely complex — a caution against the temptation to reduce training state to any one number.

Step back and the picture is unified. A hard session raises cortisol (adaptive) and transiently suppresses anabolic hormones (Hackney et al., 2024). Recovery — especially sleep — is when the anabolic side recaptures ground and the actual building happens (Lamon et al., 2021). Chronic imbalance blunts the HPA axis's responsiveness (Cadegiani & Kater, 2017b), disturbs mood (Kreher & Schwartz, 2012), and tilts the cortisol–DHEA balance toward catabolism. Every thread of this course converges here, because training is the one stressor the athlete titrates by choice.

Key Takeaways

  • Exercise is a genuine stressor that triggers an acute, dose-dependent cortisol response — and that response is adaptive, not harmful. Regular training conditions the HPA axis toward enhanced responsiveness in healthy athletes.
  • Fitness is built during recovery, not during the session. The fitness–fatigue model and the allostatic-load framework describe the same truth: adaptation depends on the balance between load and recovery.
  • The overtraining continuum runs from functional overreaching (planned, recoverable, leads to supercompensation) through non-functional overreaching to overtraining syndrome (prolonged multi-system maladaptation).
  • Resting hormone levels in overtrained athletes are often normal; the functional marker is HPA axis responsiveness under stimulation, which is blunted in OTS and enhanced in healthy athletes.
  • DHEA is the adrenal androgen produced on the same steroidogenic pathway as cortisol and acts as an anabolic counterweight; the cortisol:DHEA ratio indexes anabolic–catabolic balance over time.
  • Under-recovery is a systems problem: sleep loss directly raises cortisol and suppresses muscle protein synthesis, mood disturbance is an early warning signal, and the CAR is a promising but composite monitor.
  • Educators explain physiology and describe monitoring — they recognize warning signs and refer. Diagnosis of overtraining syndrome is clinical.
Looking Ahead

We have watched cortisol build and, when mismanaged, break the athlete. Next we turn to the levers people actually reach for to shift their stress physiology — nutrition, so-called "adaptogens," and the crowded marketplace of interventions that promise to "lower cortisol." Armed with everything you now know about what cortisol is for, we will ask which of these claims survive contact with the evidence.
