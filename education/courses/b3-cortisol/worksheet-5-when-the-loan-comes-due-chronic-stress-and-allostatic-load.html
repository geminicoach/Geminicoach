Part D · The Deformed Rhythm

Chronic stress leaves a signature in the diurnal cortisol curve you mapped earlier in the course.

  1. Describe the three characteristic changes chronic stress makes to the diurnal curve (what happens to the peak, the trough, and the amplitude), and name the single term the literature uses for the flattened result.
  2. Two mechanisms make the amplitude collapse. One is blunted feedback sensitivity. Explain what that means at the level of receptors and why it makes the axis harder to switch off the longer chronic stress runs.

Part E · Where the Wear Shows First

The chapter treats the cardiovascular system as the clearest ledger of allostatic load. Reason from the evidence, not from memory of headlines.

  1. A single episode of ordinary mental stress impaired the vessel lining (the endothelium) for hours in healthy volunteers. Explain how that one finding, combined with Mode 1 (repeated hits) and Mode 3 (failure to shut off), builds into lasting cardiovascular disease (CVD).
  2. Distinguish the popular belief that the danger of stress "is really just the momentary spike" from what the evidence actually shows. What is the injury, if not the spike itself?
  3. A flattened diurnal slope also removes the vessels' nightly recovery window. Explain the link between an elevated evening cortisol, the nighttime blood-pressure dip, and cumulative cardiovascular wear.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds physiological literacy about chronic stress and allostatic load, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, and treat anything that looks clinical (a blood pressure that will not come down, chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, or any acute or worsening symptom) as warranting evaluation by a qualified clinician. Computing or interpreting an allostatic-load score in a real person is clinical work. Work within your role, whatever it is: refer when a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, and do not self-diagnose.
