The Number Is Not the Verdict

Where the lines on your lab report actually come from — and why "in range" was never a promise of health.

60 min read 8 cited sources

Two people receive identical lab reports on the same morning. Same marker, same value, same little flag in the margin: HIGH. The first person is a 24-year-old with crushing fatigue. The second is a distance runner who has never felt better in her life. The report treats them as the same case. They are not. And the machinery that produced that flag has no idea which one is standing in front of it.

This is the uncomfortable truth at the center of this entire course: the flag on your report is not a diagnosis, not a verdict, and often not even about you. It is the output of a statistical convention decided decades ago by committees of laboratory scientists. Before we open a single panel — before we talk about iron, or thyroid, or cholesterol — you need to understand what those flags are actually made of. Because once you see how the sausage is made, you can never un-see it, and every result you read for the rest of your life gets more honest.

Start here: the three sentences that change everything

If you remember nothing else from this chapter, hold onto these:

1. A reference range is a description of a population, not a target for you. It answers the question "where do most healthy people land?" — not "where should this specific person land to thrive?"

2. "In range" and "optimal" are different claims. A value can sit comfortably inside the range and still be a poor position for your age, your sex, your training status, or your trajectory over time.

3. "Out of range" is a prompt, not a diagnosis. It means "look closer, and bring a professional." It does not mean "you have a disease," and it does not license you to self-diagnose from a spreadsheet.

Everything else in this chapter is the reasoning that makes those three sentences true. We'll build the reference range from scratch, watch it move when we change the population, and then separate it cleanly from a second kind of line — the decision limit — that looks identical on your report but means something entirely different.

Think About It

Before reading on: if a lab told you that "95% of healthy people fall inside this range," what would you predict happens to the other 5%? Hold your answer — the number turns out to matter more than it first appears.

How a reference range is actually manufactured

Let's build one together, the way a laboratory does. The recipe is startlingly simple, and its simplicity is exactly the point.

Step 1 — Gather a "healthy" reference population

You recruit a group of people you have decided are healthy for the marker in question. The international guideline that governs this — the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute's EP28-A3c — recommends a minimum of 120 reference individuals to establish a reliable interval (CLSI, 2008). Notice the two soft words already doing enormous work: "healthy" and "decided." Who counts as healthy? Which people were even available to recruit? Those choices are made by humans, and they get baked permanently into the number that later appears on your report.

Step 2 — Measure the marker in everyone

You run the test on all 120-plus people and plot the results. For most markers, the values pile up in the middle and thin out toward the edges — the familiar bell-ish shape. Some markers are skewed and need mathematical transformation first, but the principle holds: you now have a distribution of what healthy people actually produce.

Step 3 — Trim off the tails and call the middle "normal"

Here is the move that defines everything. By convention, you draw the lower limit at the 2.5th percentile and the upper limit at the 97.5th percentile (CLSI, 2008). The middle 95% becomes "the reference range." The bottom 2.5% and top 2.5% — healthy people, remember, that's who we recruited — get labeled abnormal.

Read that again. The reference range is not a line between sick and well. It is a line drawn around the middle 95% of a group we already agreed were well. The 5% we exclude were never unhealthy. We simply decided the boundary would live there.

The "normal range" is not normal, and it is not a range. It is a statistical artifact, and clinical risk is continuous rather than binary.

Whyte & Kelly (2018)

This is why Whyte and Kelly (2018) argue the everyday phrase "normal range" is doubly misleading. It isn't "normal" in any biological or moral sense — it's a percentile cut. And it isn't a "range" in the sense of a wall between health and disease — because risk for most markers rises smoothly, not in a step at the boundary. The word on your report imports a certainty the statistics never contained.

Think About It

Your earlier prediction: what happens to the 5%? By construction, roughly 1 in 20 perfectly healthy people will land "out of range" on any given marker — not because anything is wrong, but because that's where the scissors were placed. Sit with that before we make it worse.

The one-in-twenty problem gets dramatically worse

One marker, one in twenty. Fine. But you rarely get one marker. A comprehensive metabolic panel bundles a dozen or more. And here's the arithmetic that catches almost everyone off guard: if each test independently has a 95% chance of landing "in range" for a healthy person, the chance that all of them do drops fast with every test you add.

Run ten independent tests and the probability that a healthy person gets a clean sweep — every single value in range — falls to roughly 60% (Acutecaretesting.org, 2016). In other words, order a full panel on a perfectly healthy person and it is more likely than not that at least one value comes back flagged. Timbrell (2024) makes the same point structurally: the probability of at least one abnormal result climbs with every additional test ordered, purely as a matter of statistics.

This has a direct, practical consequence for how you read your own report. A single isolated flag on a broad panel is the statistically expected outcome, not an alarm. It earns a second look — a repeat test, a look at the trend, a conversation with a clinician — but a lone flag amid a dozen normal values is often just the reference-range convention doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Why the population you choose is the range

The widget above hides a lesson worth naming out loud. The reference range is only as meaningful as the population it was built from. Change the people and you change the number — no new biology required.

This is not a hypothetical fragility. Timbrell (2024) catalogues how method bias, population differences, and individual characteristics — age, sex, ethnicity — can all prevent a reference interval from being validly transferred from one laboratory or group to another. The range printed on your report from Lab A may not even be the range Lab B would have used for the identical sample. Doyle and Bunch (2023) put it bluntly: a laboratory result has no meaning without a reference point — and yet practitioners are frequently unaware of how shaky and context-bound that reference point is.

The two cases that will follow us through the whole course

Two archetypes make the "population, not person" problem concrete. Meet them now; you'll see them again in almost every panel we study.

The endurance athlete with low-normal hemoglobin. Trained endurance athletes routinely expand their plasma volume — the liquid part of blood grows, diluting the concentration of red cells. Their hemoglobin can read at the very bottom of the range, or even nominally "low," while their oxygen-carrying capacity and performance are excellent. The reference range was built mostly from sedentary people. It describes them, not her. Reading her flag as "anemia" would be reading the population's story onto an individual it doesn't fit.

The older adult with age-typical testosterone. Many markers drift predictably with age. A testosterone value that is perfectly ordinary for a 70-year-old man might look alarming against the expectations for a 25-year-old — and vice versa. If a laboratory reports a single adult range that lumps every age together, the same number carries a totally different meaning depending on whose body produced it. Coskun (2024) argues this is precisely why population-based intervals raise reliability concerns at the individual level, and why the field is moving toward references derived from a person's own longitudinal data — your past self as your best comparison.

Think About It

Both cases share one flaw. Name it in a sentence before reading on. — The flaw is applying a population's central tendency to an individual whose relevant context (training, age) was never represented in that population. The number is fine; the comparison is wrong.

The second kind of line: decision limits

So far everything we've drawn has been statistical — the middle 95% of some group. But some of the most important lines on your report are not made this way at all. They look identical: same tidy cutoff, same flag if you cross it. They mean something completely different.

A clinical decision limit (also called a decision limit or, in some framings, an action limit) is not derived from where healthy people happen to cluster. It is set by outcome research — studies linking a marker value to the actual risk of a bad clinical event or to the diagnosis of a specific disease (Ozarda et al., 2018). The question shifts from "where do most people land?" to "above what value does risk climb enough that we should act?"

The contrast, in one table's worth of prose

A reference interval answers: Is this value typical for a healthy population? Its boundary is the 2.5th or 97.5th percentile. Cross it and the honest interpretation is "unusual — worth a look."

A decision limit answers: Does this value carry enough risk, or enough diagnostic weight, that we should intervene? Its boundary comes from mortality data, disease definitions, and clinical trials — not percentiles. Cross it and the interpretation is "risk threshold reached — this is where action is indicated."

Ozarda and colleagues (2018), writing for the IFCC committee that governs both concepts, are explicit: reference intervals describe the typical distribution of a healthy population, while decision limits are tied to significantly elevated risk of adverse outcomes or are diagnostic for specific diseases. And crucially — falling outside a reference interval does not by itself indicate disease; it warrants further evaluation.

The everyday examples you already meet

Several of the most familiar cutoffs in all of medicine are decision limits wearing the same clothes as reference ranges:

Fasting glucose and HbA1c. The thresholds for prediabetes and diabetes are not "the top 2.5% of healthy people." They are diagnostic cutoffs chosen because they predict the future development of complications. They are decision limits by design (Ozarda et al., 2018).

Cholesterol / lipids. The target values for LDL and related lipid parameters come from cardiovascular outcome research — the level above which heart-disease risk rises meaningfully — not from where the population happens to sit. In fact, because so much of the population carries elevated cardiovascular risk, the "typical" value and the "desirable" value diverge sharply. Here, "in range for the population" and "at your goal" are almost opposites.

This is the deepest practical payoff of the chapter. A single value can be simultaneously inside the population reference interval and above the decision limit — perfectly ordinary and genuinely worth acting on, at the same time. The report shows you one line. Knowing which kind it is changes what you do next.

Putting it to work: how to actually read a flag

Theory earns its keep only when it changes behavior. Here is the decision frame this chapter is built to install. Run any flagged value through it before you feel anything.

The four-question read

1. Which kind of line is this? Is the boundary a statistical reference interval or an outcome-based decision limit? For glucose, HbA1c, and lipids, assume decision limit and weight it accordingly. For most other markers, assume statistical range — and remember the one-in-twenty math.

2. Is it one flag or a pattern? A single flag on a broad panel is statistically expected and often benign. A cluster, a large deviation, or a value that keeps drifting the same direction over repeated tests is a different animal.

3. Does the range fit the person? Age, sex, training status, pregnancy, ethnicity, and medications can all mean the population range doesn't describe you well. The athlete and the older adult are your reminders.

4. What's the trend? Your own previous results are frequently a better comparison than any population interval — the principle Coskun (2024) and Doyle & Bunch (2023) describe as personalized, individual-based reference. Position within the range, and the direction of travel across the ranges, both matter.

Think About It

Where does self-diagnosis fit in this frame? — Nowhere. Every one of the four questions ends in "so investigate further" or "so discuss with a professional." The frame tells you how seriously to take a flag and what to ask. It never hands you a diagnosis. That line is the ethical spine of the entire course.

The mindset, stated plainly

"Out of range" is a signal that warrants medical evaluation — not a diagnosis you can issue yourself, and not, on its own, evidence of disease (Ozarda et al., 2018; Whyte & Kelly, 2018). "In range" is not a certificate of health, and it is certainly not a synonym for "optimal for someone like you." The number tells you a position. Your job — the skill this course builds — is to read that position, respect the context, and know when the value is telling you to see a professional.

A clinical laboratory test result has no value without a reference point — yet practitioners are often unaware of the inherent limitations in how that reference point is defined and derived.

Doyle & Bunch (2023)

Key Takeaways

  • A reference range is the central 95% of values from a chosen "healthy" population — lower limit at the 2.5th percentile, upper at the 97.5th (CLSI, 2008). It describes a group, not your personal ideal.
  • By construction, about 1 in 20 healthy people falls "out of range" on any single marker — and across ten tests, a healthy person is more likely than not to get at least one flag (Acutecaretesting.org, 2016; Timbrell, 2024).
  • Change the population and you change the range. Age, sex, ethnicity, training status, and lab method can all make a printed range a poor fit for the individual holding the report.
  • A decision limit is a different kind of line: set by outcome and diagnostic research, not percentiles. Glucose, HbA1c, and lipid cutoffs are decision limits (Ozarda et al., 2018).
  • One value can be simultaneously inside the reference range and over the decision limit — ordinary for the population yet worth acting on.
  • "In range" ≠ "optimal." "Out of range" ≠ "diseased." A flag is a prompt for evaluation with a professional, never a self-issued diagnosis.
