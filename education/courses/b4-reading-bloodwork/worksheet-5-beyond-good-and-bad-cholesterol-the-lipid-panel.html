Part A · The Particles, Not the Scoreboard

In two or three sentences each, explain what low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and triglycerides actually represent, using the delivery-truck model from the chapter rather than the "good versus bad" shorthand.

  1. What does low-density lipoprotein (LDL) carry, which direction does it travel, and why does that direction matter for artery health?
  2. What does high-density lipoprotein (HDL) do, and why has the field become more cautious about treating a high HDL number as a protective charm on its own?
  3. What are triglycerides, which particles mainly carry them, and why does the chapter call them the most behaviorally responsive of the three markers?

Part B · A Cleaner Story Than One Number

Explain each of the following in your own words, in complete sentences.

  1. What is non-HDL cholesterol, how is it calculated, and why does it often read cardiovascular risk more consistently than LDL cholesterol alone?
  2. What does apolipoprotein B (apoB) measure that LDL cholesterol cannot, and why does that difference matter when two people share the same LDL cholesterol reading?
  3. Describe "discordance" in your own words, using an example: two learners with identical LDL cholesterol but different particle counts. What would explain the gap, and which measurement would catch it?
  4. What do the total cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein (HDL) ratio and the triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein (HDL) ratio suggest when they run high, and why are they described as pattern-recognition tools rather than diagnostic tests?

Part C · Fasting Versus Non-Fasting

  1. Which lipid markers barely change between a fasting and a non-fasting blood draw, and which one changes the most? Explain why, in terms of what is physically happening in the bloodstream after a meal.
  2. Name two specific situations described in the chapter where a fasting recheck genuinely earns its inconvenience, and explain what each situation is trying to rule out.
  3. Why does the chapter recommend comparing a fasting result to a fasting result, or a non-fasting result to a non-fasting result, rather than comparing either one to a general population cutoff calculated from a mix of both?

Part E · Cutoffs as Decisions, Not Grades

  1. Explain what it means to call cardiovascular cutoffs "outcome-based decision limits" rather than population percentiles. Why does that distinction matter for how a learner should read the labels desirable, borderline, and high on a lab report?

  3. Name two places where the chapter says researchers still honestly disagree, for example around older adults or around which marker should anchor treatment targets. Why is naming that disagreement more useful to a learner than pretending the science is fully settled?

Where education ends
This worksheet builds lipid-panel literacy, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the particles, the calculations, and the cutoffs work, and treat anything that looks clinical, such as triglycerides near or above 500 milligrams per deciliter or a markedly elevated LDL cholesterol alongside a family history of early heart disease, as signs that warrant medical evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
