  1. Explain why a rising thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) means the thyroid is falling behind, not speeding up. Name the gland that makes TSH and the gland it is instructing.

  3. Two people both have a TSH of 6.5. One has a normal free T4; the other has a low free T4. Explain, in your own words, why these are not the same situation.

Part B · The Population Range and the Contested Functional Target

This part works through Owen's report from the chapter: a TSH of 0.7, inside a lab's printed range of roughly 0.4 to 4.5.

  1. Explain what it means to say Owen's 0.7 clears the population floor but sits well below the roughly 1 to 2 mid-range a healthy young adult commonly shows. Why does a low TSH, specifically, prompt a further look rather than either panic or reassurance?
  2. Name the two tests that a low, borderline TSH like Owen's should prompt a look at next, and explain what each one would add to the picture.
  3. The roughly 1 to 2 functional target is described in the chapter as evidence-graded and still contested, not a diagnostic line. Explain what that phrase means in practice, and why a learner should never chase that number on their own with supplements or an unsupervised regimen.

Part C · Age Changes the Thermostat's Setting

A 75-year-old's TSH comes back at 5.8, with a normal free T4 and no symptoms.

  1. Explain why this result is generally read as an unremarkable, even expected, finding for this age, rather than as a signal of disease. What is thought to change in the thyroid axis as people age?
  2. Explain why pasting a young-adult functional target of roughly 1 to 2 onto an older adult's chart is a misreading, and describe what risk that misreading can lead to.

Part D · Pregnancy Resets the Range

  1. Explain why a single, generic adult TSH range is the wrong yardstick during pregnancy. Name the hormone responsible and describe, briefly, how it affects the thyroid axis.
  2. Describe how the recommended TSH ceiling changes across the three trimesters of pregnancy, and explain why a TSH that looks low against the standard non-pregnant range might simply be an expected feature of early pregnancy.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds literacy in reading thyroid and testosterone panels, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the feedback systems work, and treat anything that looks clinical (a discordant panel, a new neck swelling, a racing or unsettled heart rate, unexplained weight change, or persistent symptoms) as a sign that warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose or self-treat with supplements, iodine, or hormones.
