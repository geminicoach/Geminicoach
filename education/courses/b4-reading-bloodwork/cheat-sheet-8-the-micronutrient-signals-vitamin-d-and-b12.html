Chapter 8 : The Micronutrient Signals

Vitamin D moves with the calendar. B12 can look normal while running short. Neither reads honestly as one number alone.

25-Hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D): A Hormone Built by Sunlight

What is measured25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), the stable, slow-turnover form made by the liver from sunlight-driven cholecalciferol. The kidney converts it to the active hormone calcitriol only on demand, which is why calcitriol itself is not the useful test.
What moves itLatitude and season, skin pigmentation, body fat (a fat-soluble hormone sequesters in adipose tissue), age, sun exposure habits, and diet or supplementation. Most of these track the calendar and the map, not underlying health.
The seasonal curveRises through spring and summer, peaks in late summer to early autumn, falls through winter to a late-winter trough. Two draws with the same number, taken in different seasons, can sit on opposite slopes of that curve.

Sufficient vs. Optimal: An Honest Debate

  • Institute of Medicine: population floor, roughly 20 ng/mL, meant to cover 97.5% of a healthy population.
  • Endocrine Society: clinical target, 30 ng/mL or higher, with a preferred range some clinicians favor near 40 to 60 ng/mL.
  • Different bodies, different questions: a population nutrition floor versus individual clinical guidance.
  • The VITAL trial found no reduction in cancer or major cardiovascular events from pushing already-adequate levels higher.
  • Correcting a true deficiency has strong support. Chasing "optimal" beyond sufficiency remains genuinely contested.

Supplementation: The Bathtub Model

  • Dose-response is logarithmic, not straight-line: rises quickly at first, then levels off toward a new plateau over several weeks.
  • Circulating 25(OH)D has a half-life of weeks, so one large dose gives a temporary spike, not a lasting change.
  • Consistent, moderate dosing tends to produce steadier, more durable results than occasional large doses.
  • Older adults, pregnant or lactating people, those with higher body fat, and those with malabsorptive conditions need individualized targets, not a general-population number.

B12 (Cobalamin): Two Carriers, One Number

  • About 80% rides on haptocorrin, a buffer fraction most cells cannot use.
  • About 20% rides on transcobalamin, the fraction cells actually take up.
  • A standard test reports both fractions combined, so a normal total can mask a low usable fraction.
  • Risk factors for hidden shortage: autoimmune gastritis, long-term acid-suppressing medication or metformin, aging-related low stomach acid, and a diet low in animal products without supplementation.
  • A comfortably normal result lowers the likelihood of deficiency. It does not eliminate it.

Confirmation Layers

  • Methylmalonic acid (MMA): rises when B12-dependent methylmalonyl-CoA mutase slows. Fairly specific to B12, but also confounded by reduced kidney function.
  • Homocysteine: rises with B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, low B6, reduced kidney function, or an underactive thyroid. Sensitive, but not specific.
  • The grey zone (roughly 200 to 350 pg/mL serum B12) is exactly where ordering both markers together adds the most useful information.
  • Neither marker is a perfect verdict alone. Read them together, with confounders in mind.

The Methylfolate Trap and the CBC Tie-Back

  • Methionine synthase needs B12 to convert folate back into its usable form. When B12 runs short, folate gets stuck as 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, unusable for building new DNA, even though total folate can look normal.
  • Red cell precursors in the bone marrow keep growing without dividing on schedule, producing an elevated mean corpuscular volume (MCV): the macrocytic pattern from this course's complete blood count (CBC) chapter.
  • Folic acid can partially or fully normalize the blood count through a route that bypasses the trapped pathway, while an underlying B12 deficiency, and its risk to nerve tissue, keeps progressing unnoticed.
  • A flagged macrocytic anemia calls for B12 and folate to be evaluated together by a clinician, not treated with folic acid alone.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how vitamin D and B12 behave, not a license to diagnose or treat. Persistent fatigue, tingling or numbness, a flagged macrocytic anemia, or a grey-zone result alongside risk factors are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
