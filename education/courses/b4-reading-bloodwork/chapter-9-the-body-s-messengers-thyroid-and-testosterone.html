The Body's Messengers: Thyroid and Testosterone

Two feedback systems, read wrong more often than any other numbers on the page.

57 min read 13 cited sources

Open a typical blood panel and two numbers tend to sit quietly near the bottom, easy to skim past on the way to the numbers everyone already worries about. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). Testosterone. Both look like simple readouts, a single figure with a reference range printed beside it, unremarkable if the figure falls inside the box. Both are, in fact, something closer to a coded transmission from a supervisor reporting on someone else's output, and learning to read them well means learning to read the code, not just the number.

Start with the code inside TSH. It is tempting to assume a hormone with "stimulating" in its name simply tracks thyroid activity, so a higher number should mean a busier thyroid and a lower number a calmer one. The truth runs in the opposite direction. TSH is manufactured in the pituitary gland, not the thyroid, and its entire purpose is to demand more thyroid hormone when the brain senses too little circulating and to demand less when it senses too much. A high TSH is the brain asking loudly because the thyroid is not answering. A low TSH is the brain going quiet because the thyroid has already said more than enough. Read TSH as a demand signal instead of a supply signal and a great deal of the confusion around thyroid testing dissolves in one sentence. Testosterone carries a parallel trap of its own, though it is a trap of packaging rather than direction. Most testosterone circulating in blood travels bound to escort proteins, unable to act on any cell, and only a slim fraction rides free enough to do its job. A total testosterone number that looks reassuring can be quietly misleading if those escort proteins are unusually abundant, and a number drawn in the afternoon can look low for no reason other than the clock.

The learner who threads through this chapter is Owen, 22, who ordered a direct-to-consumer panel after a stretch of low energy and a foggy head that would not lift no matter how much sleep he tried to bank. His results came back with a small green checkmark next to nearly everything, including a TSH of 0.7, comfortably inside the laboratory's printed range of roughly 0.4 to 4.5, and a total testosterone that also cleared the bar. Owen closed the report feeling reassured, and slightly foolish for having worried. He was not wrong to feel confused, because the report handed him a verdict without giving him the tools to check it. He is not a patient in this chapter and no diagnosis is being made about him; he is a stand-in for an extremely common situation, a normal-looking report that is only normal if you stop reading at the first flag. By the end of this chapter you will be able to see what Owen's report was quietly not telling him, and you will understand why two of the most common numbers on a panel are also two of the most commonly misread.

TSH: the brain's demand signal, not the thyroid's supply gauge

Start with the axis that produces the number. The hypothalamus releases thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), which travels a short distance to the pituitary gland and prompts it to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) into the bloodstream. TSH then travels on to the thyroid gland itself, in the neck, and instructs it to manufacture and release thyroxine, known as T4, and a smaller amount of triiodothyronine, known as T3, the two hormones that do the actual work of thyroid signaling throughout the body, from resting metabolic rate to heart rate to temperature regulation. Once free thyroxine (free T4) and free T3, the unbound, biologically active fractions of those hormones, are circulating at a level the hypothalamus and pituitary judge sufficient, both organs throttle back, releasing less TRH and less TSH. This is a classic negative feedback loop, structurally similar to a thermostat regulating a room. According to Hoermann et al. (2015), TSH and free T4 exist in a homeostatic equilibrium that is somewhat unique to each person, a relationship in which small changes in free T4 produce comparatively large swings in TSH, which is exactly why TSH is such a sensitive early indicator of a thyroid axis drifting off its usual setting.

Here is the detail that trips up almost every learner encountering this system for the first time. Because TSH has "stimulating" in its name, it is tempting to read a rising number as a thyroid gland revving up and a falling number as a thyroid gland slowing down. The relationship runs the opposite direction. TSH is not the thyroid's output. It is the pituitary's demand for more output, and it rises precisely when the thyroid is falling short and falls precisely when the thyroid has more than met the request. A high TSH means the brain is asking loudly because the thyroid is not answering, the pattern seen in an underactive thyroid, or hypothyroidism. A low TSH means the brain has gone quiet because the thyroid has already said more than enough, the pattern seen in an overactive thyroid, or hyperthyroidism. Get this backwards, and every subsequent judgment about a thyroid panel gets built on an inverted foundation.

Owen's report illustrates the trap in its most common form. His TSH came back at 0.7, comfortably inside the laboratory's population reference range of roughly 0.4 to 4.0, sometimes printed as 4.5, and flagged with a small green checkmark that told him nothing was wrong. Two things are true at once here, and the report only showed him one of them. It is true that 0.7 clears the population floor and is not, by itself, a diagnosis of anything. It is also true that 0.7 sits close to the bottom of that range, well under the roughly 1 to 2 mid-range a healthy young adult commonly shows, and because a lower TSH points toward the overactive side of the axis rather than the underactive side, the responsible next step is not relief. It is to look at free T4 and, where the picture is still unclear, free T3, to see whether Owen's thyroid gland is quietly running hotter than his brain intended, or whether 0.7 is simply where his own baseline happens to sit. A single TSH value near the floor of a wide population range is a prompt to look further, not a verdict in either direction.

It is worth being precise about what that 1 to 2 figure is and is not. It is a commonly cited functional target, drawn from observational associations between TSH and outcomes like cardiovascular risk markers and symptom burden in healthy, non-pregnant adults, and it is a genuinely useful mental anchor for where a young, healthy thyroid axis typically sits. It is also, in the fullest honesty, an evidence-graded, still contested functional view, not a diagnostic line, and it is never a target to chase on your own with supplements or an unsupervised iodine regimen. The next section takes up that debate directly, because Owen's number cannot be read responsibly without it.

Think About It

Before reading further: a friend's TSH comes back at 6.5. Another friend's TSH also comes back at 6.5. The first friend's free T4 is normal; the second friend's free T4 is low. Are these two people in the same situation? If you can explain why not, you already understand why TSH is read together with free T4, never alone.

Why free T4 and free T3 are worth naming separately

T4 and T3 are frequently spoken of together, almost as a single idea, but they are not interchangeable, and the difference matters for reading a panel correctly. The thyroid gland manufactures mostly T4, along with a smaller, direct contribution of T3. T4 functions largely as a stable reservoir, a precursor hormone that tissues throughout the body convert into T3 on demand, using a family of enzymes called deiodinases. T3 is the more biologically potent form, the one that actually docks with receptors inside cells and switches gene expression on or off. This means a person can have a free T4 that looks entirely normal while their peripheral conversion to T3 is running poorly, a pattern that would be invisible to anyone reading TSH and free T4 alone. It is also why free T3 becomes genuinely useful, not as a routine screening test, but as a second-line tool when TSH and free T4 tell a story that does not match how someone feels, or when a clinician is trying to understand why symptoms of an underactive or overactive thyroid persist despite reassuring numbers on the first two tests.

None of this argues for ordering every test on every panel every time. TSH remains the correct first test precisely because of how sensitively it tracks small changes in thyroid output, and free T4 remains the correct second test when TSH raises a question. Free T3 and thyroid peroxidase antibodies earn their place further down the list, reserved for exactly the situations this chapter keeps returning to: a borderline number, a discordant symptom picture, or a life stage, like Owen's, where the population range was never built with that specific body in mind.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common misreading treats TSH as if it were a direct thermometer for the thyroid itself, so a rising number gets interpreted as the thyroid gland speeding up and a falling number as it slowing down. It is exactly backwards. TSH is the pituitary's request for more hormone, not the thyroid's own output. A climbing TSH means the thyroid is falling behind the request, not racing ahead of it, and a falling TSH means the request has already been more than satisfied.

The population range and the contested functional target

Labs commonly print TSH normal as roughly 0.4 to 4.0, sometimes 4.5, a range built from statistical percentiles of a reference population rather than from a proven boundary between health and disease. According to Wartofsky and Dickey (2005), the population historically used to establish that upper limit likely included people with unrecognized, mild thyroid dysfunction, which pulls the statistical ceiling higher than a genuinely thyroid-healthy population would produce; they argued for a narrower reference range on that basis, one that would flag disease earlier rather than later.

Surks et al. (2004), in a widely cited review for JAMA, offered the counterpoint: simply lowering the upper cutoff would reclassify a large share of the population, potentially millions of people, as having subclinical thyroid disease, many of them with no symptoms plausibly related to thyroid function, without clear evidence that treating them would help. Waise and Price (2009) add a distinction that is easy to miss and that clears up a good share of the confusion in this debate: the upper limit of a population reference range and the treatment cutoff a clinician uses to decide when subclinical hypothyroidism actually warrants treatment are two different numbers answering two different questions, and conflating them is where much of the argument goes sideways.

Layered underneath both positions is a more individual idea. Hoermann et al. (2015) describe TSH and free T4 as settling into a homeostatic set point that varies meaningfully from person to person, so a population range built from thousands of different individuals will always be wider than what is truly typical for any one of them. This is part of why some clinicians favor a narrower functional target, roughly 1 to 2 for a healthy, non-pregnant young adult, associated in some observational literature with favorable lipid and symptom profiles. Say this plainly: that functional target is an evidence-graded, still contested clinical view, not a diagnostic line, and it should never become a number a learner chases on their own through supplements, iodine, or an unsupervised prescription. It is a lens for asking better questions, not a target for self-treatment.

Owen's 0.7 sits below even the wide population range's midpoint and well below the contested functional target's floor, which is exactly why it deserves a second look through free T4 and free T3 rather than either panic or reassurance. Neither the wide range nor the narrow target settles his case by itself. Together, read as a starting point for better questions, they tell him what to ask for next.

It also helps to know why this debate exists at all rather than having been settled decades ago. TSH assays have become dramatically more sensitive since the reference ranges most labs still quote were first established, and the population samples used to build those older ranges were not always screened carefully for people with unrecognized thyroid nodules, family history of autoimmune thyroid disease, or borderline antibody status. A reference range is only as good as the population used to build it, and a population contaminated with a small number of people already drifting toward thyroid dysfunction will produce a wider, more permissive range than a population of confirmed, thoroughly screened healthy volunteers would. That is the technical seed of the entire narrower-range argument, and it is also why the debate has not simply resolved itself with more data: better screening keeps raising the question of how strict is strict enough, without ever producing a single number everyone agrees on.

Age changes the thermostat's setting

According to Surks and Hollowell (2007), who analyzed a large, nationally representative United States population sample, TSH rises with age in healthy people, without an accompanying fall in free T4, which indicates a genuine shift in the thyroid axis's own set point rather than a spreading disease. The pituitary in an older, healthy adult appears content asking a bit louder for the same amount of hormone. That is a recalibration, not a malfunction, and treating it as a malfunction is one of the more common ways bloodwork gets over-read in later life.

Consider a 75-year-old whose TSH comes back at 5.8, with a normal free T4 and no symptoms. Read against the standard adult ceiling of roughly 4.0 to 4.5, that number looks like it crossed a line. Read against what is typical and expectable at that age, it is unremarkable, and some of the literature on thyroid function in later life even associates a modestly elevated TSH in the oldest adults with neutral or favorable outcomes rather than harm. A young-adult functional target of roughly 1 to 2 was never built for this person and should not be pasted onto their chart. Many clinicians who work with older adults now favor age-adjusted reference ranges for exactly this reason, since over-diagnosing and over-treating subclinical hypothyroidism in later life carries its own real costs, including unnecessary hormone replacement in someone whose body was already doing the right thing for its age.

This point runs in both directions and deserves to be stated plainly: an older adult's thermostat is allowed to sit at a different setting than a young adult's, and that difference is not, by itself, evidence of disease. A single reference range printed on a lab report was built for a population that spans every decade of adulthood, which means it will always be a slightly wrong fit for any one age band. Age tells you which part of the range to trust.

The likely mechanism is not that the thyroid gland itself simply wears out. It is more that the pituitary's own sensitivity to circulating thyroid hormone appears to change gradually over a lifetime, so it takes a slightly larger TSH signal in later life to hold free T4 at the same setpoint it held decades earlier. That is a subtle, whole-system recalibration rather than a breakdown of any single part, and it is a useful reminder that aging bodies are not simply younger bodies with worse numbers. They are systems running to a different, still coherent, set of settings. This is exactly the same logic this chapter applies to testosterone later on, and it is worth carrying forward as a general habit: before treating any hormone value as a problem, ask what that value is supposed to look like at this particular age, not just in adulthood generally.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common mistake takes the same numeric target that makes sense for a healthy 25-year-old, roughly 1 to 2, and applies it as a scorecard for a person forty or fifty years older. Age changes the thermostat's set point in ways that are normal, not diseased. A TSH that would prompt a second look at 25 can be entirely ordinary, even reassuring, at 75. The right reference point moves with the person.

When the story does not add up: free T4, free T3, and thyroid peroxidase antibodies

TSH is a superb early warning system precisely because it is so sensitive to small changes in thyroid output, but a warning system is not the whole story. When the picture is unclear, a borderline value, a value that seems inconsistent with how a person feels, or a result like Owen's that sits near the floor of the range, the next step is to look directly at the hormones TSH is regulating. Free T4 is the unbound, active fraction of the thyroid's main hormone; free T3, the more biologically potent hormone, produced partly by the thyroid and partly by the conversion of T4 in tissues throughout the body, fills in the picture further when free T4 alone still leaves questions open.

The other test worth naming here is thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies. Thyroid peroxidase is an enzyme inside thyroid cells that is essential for building thyroid hormone, and in autoimmune thyroid disease the immune system produces antibodies against it. According to Pearce, Farwell, and Braverman (2003), TPO antibodies are a hallmark of chronic autoimmune, or Hashimoto, thyroiditis, the most common cause of hypothyroidism in regions with adequate dietary iodine, and their presence, even in someone whose TSH and free T4 currently sit inside the normal range, signals an autoimmune process already underway that raises the likelihood of thyroid dysfunction developing over time.

Put the two together and a borderline case reads very differently depending on whether antibodies are present. A TSH that is only mildly elevated, or even still technically inside the population range but climbing over successive tests, paired with positive TPO antibodies, tells a different story than the identical TSH without antibodies. The antibody result reframes a borderline number as an early chapter rather than noise. Rapid weight change, heat or cold intolerance, a resting heart rate that will not settle, a new swelling in the neck, or hair and skin changes alongside any of these lab patterns are signs that warrant medical evaluation, not a wait-and-see spreadsheet entry.

Think About It

A 29-year-old's TSH comes back at 3.9, technically inside the population range, with normal free T4 but strongly positive thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies. Is this the same situation as a TSH of 3.9 with negative antibodies? What does the antibody result add to the story that the TSH number alone cannot say?

Pregnancy resets the thermostat

Pregnancy changes thyroid physiology substantially, most notably because human chorionic gonadotropin, the hormone that rises steeply in early pregnancy, is structurally similar enough to TSH that it weakly stimulates the same thyroid receptor, nudging the thyroid to produce a bit more hormone and, through the feedback loop already described, pulling TSH down, especially in the first trimester.

According to Alexander et al. (2017), writing the American Thyroid Association's guidelines for thyroid disease during pregnancy and the postpartum period, this is exactly why a single, generic adult TSH range is the wrong yardstick during pregnancy; the guidelines recommend trimester-specific reference ranges, with the upper limit for TSH lowered in early pregnancy, commonly cited around 4.0, roughly half a point below the typical non-pregnant ceiling, before gradually returning toward the standard non-pregnant range across the second and third trimesters as the influence of rising pregnancy hormones fades.

A TSH that would be unremarkable outside pregnancy could be flagged as too high during the first trimester under trimester-specific ranges, and a TSH that looks low against the standard non-pregnant range may simply be an expected feature of early pregnancy rather than a sign of an overactive thyroid. The lesson generalizes well beyond pregnancy: whenever a learner reads bloodwork, the question is not only what the number is, but which reference range belongs to this specific body, at this specific life stage, on this specific day.

The second messenger: what testosterone on a panel is actually counting

Owen's report also included a testosterone result, and it is worth pausing on what that single number is actually built from before deciding what it means. Total testosterone, the figure most panels report by default, adds up every molecule of testosterone circulating in the blood regardless of whether that molecule is free to act on a cell or locked to a transport protein along for the ride. According to Rosner et al. (2007), writing an Endocrine Society position statement on the pitfalls of testosterone measurement, roughly 44 percent of circulating testosterone travels tightly bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), a carrier protein made mainly in the liver, while slightly more, on the order of 54 percent, travels more loosely attached to albumin, and only a slim 1 to 2 percent circulates fully unbound, or free.

The functional significance of this split is large. SHBG-bound testosterone is essentially locked away, unavailable to enter cells and activate the androgen receptor. Free testosterone, and to a meaningful extent the loosely albumin-bound portion sometimes grouped with it as bioavailable testosterone, is what tissue actually experiences. Two people can share an identical total testosterone and live in very different hormonal realities depending on how much of that total is escorted away by SHBG.

SHBG itself is not a fixed backdrop. According to Hammond (2011), sex hormone-binding globulin production responds to a wide range of influences, rising with age, with an overactive thyroid, with liver disease, and with certain medications, and falling with obesity, insulin resistance, and an underactive thyroid. Every time SHBG moves, often for reasons unrelated to testosterone production itself, it silently reshapes what a given total testosterone number actually means for that person on that day. A rising SHBG, common with age, can make a stable or even mildly increasing total testosterone quietly mask a falling free testosterone, while a falling SHBG, common with obesity, can make a modest total testosterone look more worrying on paper than the bioavailable reality actually is.

These are not rare edge cases. Rising body weight is common enough, and its effect on SHBG large enough, that a man carrying significant excess weight can show a total testosterone comfortably below a young, lean reference population while his free testosterone tells a considerably less alarming story, or the reverse can occur when weight loss itself raises SHBG and reshuffles the numbers again. This is precisely why a single total testosterone value, disconnected from SHBG, age, and the hour of the draw, is such a weak foundation for any conclusion, favorable or unfavorable. It is also why a learner should be skeptical of any online calculator or forum thread that treats total testosterone as if it were the whole story rather than one input among several.

One measurement caveat is worth carrying forward. Rosner et al. (2007) flagged that many widely used direct free testosterone immunoassays are unreliable, particularly outside the mid-normal range, and the most accurate method, equilibrium dialysis, is not something most routine panels use. Free testosterone is therefore often calculated from total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin using a validated formula rather than measured directly, a distinction worth knowing before treating a free testosterone result as a perfectly precise number rather than a well-informed estimate.

Think About It

Two 45-year-olds each have a total testosterone of 450 ng/dL, drawn at the same time of morning. One has ordinary sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG); the other's SHBG runs high because of a mild thyroid condition. Are these two men equally "normal"? Which one is more likely to notice symptoms despite an identical total number?

Diurnal rhythm: why the clock on the draw matters

Testosterone is not a flat number throughout the day. It follows its own daily rhythm, driven by the same hypothalamic clock that times cortisol and much of the rest of the endocrine system. According to Brambilla et al. (2009), in healthy young men testosterone typically peaks in the early morning, in the six to eight o'clock hour, and declines by roughly 20 to 25 percent by mid to late afternoon, a swing large enough to shift a single person's result across a meaningfully different part of a reference range depending purely on when the sample was drawn.

This is exactly why Bhasin et al. (2018), in the Endocrine Society's clinical practice guideline on testosterone therapy, recommend that adult men being evaluated for androgen deficiency have their initial testosterone drawn in the morning. A single afternoon value in a young man should not be read as equivalent to a morning value; a low-looking afternoon result in an otherwise well person often deserves a repeat morning draw before any conclusion is drawn, rather than an immediate worry.

Age modifies the rhythm as well. According to Bremner, Vitiello, and Prinz (1983), who sampled healthy young men and healthy older men hourly across a full day, the pronounced circadian rhythm seen in the younger group was markedly attenuated, in some cases nearly absent, in the older group; the older men showed lower testosterone across the entire 24 hour span rather than a sharp morning peak followed by an afternoon decline. The practical consequence is that timing matters most for younger men, whose rhythm is steep, and matters somewhat less, though still enough to prefer a morning draw as standard practice, for older men, whose rhythm has flattened.

Owen's testosterone was drawn at 4 in the afternoon, after a late shift, precisely the window where Brambilla and colleagues' data would predict the steepest decline from his true morning value. A number that looked unremarkable at 4 p.m. does not tell us what his 8 a.m. number would show, and in a 22-year-old that difference is not a rounding error.

There is a broader habit worth building here, one that applies well beyond testosterone. Any hormone with a meaningful daily rhythm, cortisol among the most familiar examples, carries an implicit instruction alongside its reference range: this range assumes a particular time of day. A number divorced from its collection time is an incomplete number, no matter how precisely the laboratory measured it. A learner reading their own report, or helping someone else make sense of theirs, should treat the collection time printed on the report, when it is printed at all, as being just as informative as the value itself, and should be genuinely cautious about any at-home kit or walk-in draw that does not specify or standardize when the sample was taken.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A testosterone value drawn at 3 or 4 in the afternoon is often read as if it carries the same meaning as a morning value, especially when a direct-to-consumer kit lets someone draw a sample whenever is convenient. In a young man, that afternoon draw can sit 20 to 25 percent below the same person's true morning level for no reason other than the clock. The fix is not a supplement. It is a repeat draw at the right hour.

Age bands change what "normal" means

According to Travison et al. (2017), a large, harmonized effort across four cohort studies in the United States and Europe established a reference range for total testosterone in healthy, non-obese young men, roughly age 19 to 39, of about 264 to 916 ng/dL using the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile, and a somewhat narrower 303 to 852 ng/dL using the 5th to 95th percentile. That is already a wide band for a single age group, underscoring how much individual variation exists even before age itself is introduced as a variable.

Bhasin et al. (2018) put the aging trend in plain terms: average testosterone declines on the order of 1 to 2 percent per year through mid and later adulthood, a gradual drift rather than a cliff, with substantial variation between individuals influenced by body composition, chronic illness, and medication use. Age alone predicts a general downward trend, not any one person's specific number. A total testosterone that would be unremarkable, even reassuring, for a man in his sixties can represent a meaningful, symptomatic decline for a man in his twenties, and the reverse mismatch, a young man's healthy number read as worrying because it happens to resemble what a man decades older might show, is just as common a misreading.

Bhasin et al. (2018) are also explicit about what a hypogonadism evaluation requires: consistently low, accurately measured testosterone, drawn correctly, alongside compatible symptoms, weighed by a clinician who accounts for age, comorbidity, and repeat testing. It is never a single number set against a single universal cutoff regardless of the age on the chart.

This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it cuts against a habit that direct-to-consumer testing tends to encourage: treating a single reference range, printed once at the bottom of a report, as if it were a fixed target for every adult male reader regardless of age. A 68-year-old chasing the harmonized reference range built for 19 to 39 year olds is chasing a number that was never meant to describe his stage of life, in much the same way a 75-year-old chasing a TSH of 1 to 2 is chasing a target built for a much younger thyroid axis. Symptoms, not age-inappropriate targets, are what should prompt the conversation with a clinician in either system.

The line we will not cross: education versus diagnosis

We need to name the frame that governs this chapter, and this entire course, because it shapes what a chapter like this one is for and what it is not. This is education, not diagnosis. We explain mechanisms so a learner can ask sharper questions and read a report with genuine understanding. We do not diagnose, treat, or manage disease, in a learner or in anyone else.

This matters especially for these two systems. Thyroid and testosterone panels sit at the center of a large direct-to-consumer testing and supplement industry, one that profits from confident verdicts delivered without context. A green checkmark next to a TSH of 0.7 or a total testosterone that clears a population floor is not the same thing as a clean bill of health, and a red flag on either panel is not, by itself, a diagnosis either. Both directions of overconfidence, false reassurance and false alarm, cause real harm: one delays a genuine workup, the other invites unsupervised self-treatment with hormones, supplements marketed as testosterone boosters, or iodine regimens that can push a stable thyroid axis in the wrong direction.

Owen's situation is a good example of why the line matters. Nothing in this chapter diagnoses Owen with anything. His TSH near the floor of the range and his afternoon-drawn testosterone are, at most, reasons to ask better questions and to repeat testing at the right time, under a clinician's guidance. The responsible use of everything in this chapter is to make Owen a sharper, better-informed partner in someone else's expertise, not a substitute for it.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Case study: Owen and the panel that looked fine

Case

Owen is 22, a few months into his first full-time job, running on inconsistent sleep and a diet mostly assembled from what is nearby. A stretch of low energy and a fog that would not lift led him to order a direct-to-consumer panel that included TSH and total testosterone. His results arrived with green checkmarks next to nearly everything: TSH at 0.7, inside the lab's printed range of roughly 0.4 to 4.5, and total testosterone inside its printed range as well, drawn at 4 in the afternoon after a late shift. Reassured but still tired, Owen wanted to know whether the report was actually telling him he was fine.

Run Owen's TSH through this chapter's framework. His 0.7 clears the population floor, so it is not, on its own, evidence of an overactive thyroid. But it sits well below the roughly 1 to 2 mid-range a healthy young adult commonly shows, and because a low TSH points toward the overactive side of the axis rather than the underactive side, the honest next step is to look at free T4 and free T3 before assuming the green checkmark closes the question. This is not alarm. It is simply reading the number as a demand signal rather than a verdict.

Run his testosterone through the same lens. A total testosterone drawn at 4 p.m. in a 22-year-old is being read close to the daily low point of a rhythm that, according to Brambilla et al. (2009), commonly drops 20 to 25 percent from its early-morning peak by that hour. Before Owen, or anyone reading his report, treats that number as his baseline, the responsible move is a repeat draw in the morning, ideally alongside sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), so that total and free testosterone can both be read against the right clock and the right carrier-protein context.

What this chapter does not do, and cannot do, is tell Owen whether his fatigue and brain fog trace back to his thyroid axis, his testosterone, his sleep, his diet, or something else entirely. That judgment, weighing symptoms against repeat, correctly timed labs, belongs to a clinician, not a self-ordered panel or a course chapter. What the chapter can do, and did, is replace two false reassurances, a TSH flagged normal and a testosterone flagged normal, with a sharper, more honest set of questions and a clear next step: repeat the relevant tests at the right time, add the tests that fill in the missing part of the story, and bring the whole picture, not just the flags, to someone qualified to interpret it.

What Owen leaves this chapter with is not a diagnosis and not a supplement stack. It is a working model of two feedback systems: a thyroid axis where the number that sounds like it measures the thyroid is actually a request, read against a population range wide enough to hide real signal, and reshaped again by age and, where relevant, pregnancy; and a testosterone system where the headline number is a sum of bound and free hormone, reshaped by sex hormone-binding globulin, by the hour of the draw, and by age, long before it ever reaches a clinician's judgment. That model travels a great deal further than a green checkmark ever could.

Key Takeaways

  • Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) is a demand signal, not a supply gauge: it moves opposite to free thyroxine (free T4) and free triiodothyronine (free T3), so a low TSH points toward an overactive thyroid and a high TSH points toward an underactive one.
  • The population TSH reference range, roughly 0.4 to 4.0 or 4.5, is wide enough to include people with early or subclinical thyroid change. A commonly cited functional target of roughly 1 to 2 for a healthy young adult is an evidence-graded, contested view, not a diagnostic line, and never a self-treat target.
  • TSH naturally drifts upward with age; a value that would prompt a closer look in a young adult can be normal, even benign, in someone decades older, and a young-adult target should never be pasted onto an older adult's chart. Pregnancy resets the reference range again, trimester by trimester.
  • When the TSH story is unclear, free T4, free T3, and thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies fill in what TSH alone cannot say, especially for catching autoimmune thyroid disease before it fully declares itself.
  • Total testosterone is a sum of bound and free hormone; sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) decides how much of that total is actually bioavailable, and the diurnal rhythm means the hour of the draw, morning versus afternoon, can change the story as much as the number itself.
  • "Good" values for both systems are age-banded and context-dependent, never universal. This remains education, not diagnosis; a discordant panel, or persistent symptoms, are signs that warrant medical evaluation.

Thyroid and testosterone are two feedback systems read through a handful of numbers each. The next and final chapter in this class steps back to the harder, more common task: reading an entire panel at once, where a metabolic marker, a lipid value, an iron study, and an endocrine result all sit on the same page and quietly inform one another. Chapter 10 brings every system from this class together and asks what the whole story looks like when no single number is read in isolation.
