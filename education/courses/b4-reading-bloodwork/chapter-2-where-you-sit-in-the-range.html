Where You Sit in the Range

Position, not pass/fail — and why "optimal" moves with age and life stage.

60 min read 8 cited sources

Two people walk out of the same lab on the same morning. Both are handed a ferritin result. One reads 32 µg/L; the other reads 210 µg/L. Both results print with the same reassuring word beside them — normal — because the laboratory's reference interval runs from roughly 30 to 300. The flag is identical. The story is not. One of these people is standing at the very edge of a cliff that the reference interval politely declines to mention; the other is comfortably inland. Same flag. Different lives.

Chapter 1 argued that "in range" is not the same as "optimal." This chapter hands you the two instruments you need to act on that claim for a specific person: reading position within a range, and adjusting that reading for age and life stage. Everything else in this course is these two moves, repeated on panel after panel.

The flag is the least informative thing on the page

A reference interval is a summary of where most apparently healthy reference individuals fell. By long-standing convention it captures the central 95% of that reference population — the values between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles — and discards the top and bottom 2.5% as "outliers" (Ozarda, 2016). That is a statistical decision, not a biological one. Nothing magical happens to your physiology as you cross from the 97th percentile to the 98th. The interval simply draws a fence and sorts every result into two bins: inside or outside.

Binary sorting throws away almost everything useful. The difference between a result at the 5th percentile of the interval and one at the 90th is enormous — for how you feel, how you recover, and in many cases how your risk trends over the next decade — yet both wear the same "normal" badge. The professional skill this chapter builds is refusing the badge and asking instead: whereabouts in this band does the number sit, and is that a good place for a person like this one to be?

From flag to position: percentiles and zones

Start with a mental reframe. Instead of "normal / abnormal," picture the reference interval as a ruler and your result as a mark on that ruler. Two vocabularies help you describe the mark:

Percentile answers "what fraction of the reference population sits below this value?" A result at the 10th percentile means 90% of healthy reference individuals had a higher number. That framing instantly tells you a "normal" value can still be unusual — a 10th-percentile result is, by definition, lower than nine out of ten of your peers.

Zones are a coarser, more practical partition of the same ruler. A workable default is to split the reference interval into thirds — low-normal, mid-normal, and high-normal — and to note when a value hugs either fence. Zones are how you talk to a non-specialist; percentiles are how you reason precisely. You will use both.

Think About It

Two thyroid results, TSH 0.6 and TSH 3.9, both fall inside a 0.4–4.0 reference interval and both flag "normal." Without looking anything else up, what can you already say about their positions? Which one is hugging a fence, and which sits mid-range?

Worked example: the ferritin cliff

Return to our two ferritin results, because ferritin is the cleanest illustration of why position beats flag — and why the fence itself is sometimes in the wrong place.

A 2024 systematic review traced where common ferritin reference intervals actually came from and found the foundations alarmingly thin (Truong et al., 2024). Lower limits of "normal" varied wildly between laboratories, frequently landing well below 30 µg/L — and many reference populations were assembled without excluding iron-deficient people. Read that twice: the "healthy" reference group used to define the bottom of normal often included individuals who were themselves iron-depleted. The fence was built on sand.

The practical consequence is structural underdiagnosis. A ferritin of 15 µg/L can print as "normal" against a lab interval whose lower limit is 10 or 12, even though a substantial body of clinical reasoning treats values under ~30 µg/L as consistent with depleted iron stores. Our person at 32 µg/L is not comfortably normal; they are perched one bad month — a heavy period, a growth spurt, a training block, a pregnancy — away from frank deficiency. The flag said "relax." Position says "watch this."

This is the moment to state the course's safety frame plainly. Reading position is not diagnosing. A low-in-range ferritin is a sign that warrants medical evaluation and a conversation, not a self-assigned diagnosis or a cue to start supplementing blindly. Your job as a reader — whether you're a coach, a credentialed clinician, or someone reading your own results — is to notice the position, understand why it matters, and route it to the right professional context. The skill is interpretation; the diagnosis belongs to a clinician.

Think About It

If a reference population for ferritin quietly included iron-deficient individuals, which end of the interval gets distorted — the top or the bottom? And which patients pay the price when clinicians trust that end?

Why one broad interval is really several bands stacked

So far we have treated the reference interval as a fixed ruler. It is not. In Chapter 1 you saw how an interval is constructed from a reference population. The deeper truth is that a single "adult" interval printed on your report is frequently a crude average of several narrower, more specific bands that have been flattened together to fit one line of a page.

Ozarda (2016) describes this formally as partitioning: when a marker's distribution differs meaningfully by age, sex, or physiological state, best practice is to split the reference interval into separate sub-intervals rather than report one blurred range. The catch is that partitioning multiplies the work — each sub-group needs its own properly sampled reference population — so many labs, for cost and convenience, report the broad version anyway. The broad interval is not wrong so much as lossy. It compresses several stories into one.

The clearest proof: children are not small adults

Nowhere is the flattening more obvious than in pediatrics. The CALIPER program spent a decade measuring more than 100 biomarkers in healthy Canadian children and adolescents, and the headline finding is blunt: pediatric biomarker concentrations change continuously with development and simply cannot be mapped onto adult norms (Adeli et al., 2017). Alkaline phosphatase, driven by bone growth, soars during the pubertal growth spurt to levels that would look frankly alarming against an adult interval — and then falls. A single adult band applied to a 13-year-old would generate false alarms; a single pediatric band applied across all childhood would hide real change.

The methodological response has been to abandon the fence metaphor entirely for a smoother one. Rather than chop childhood into a handful of discrete age boxes — each with a hard edge where the "normal" range jumps overnight — researchers now fit continuous centile curves that flow across age (Higgins & Adeli, 2016). Using the Lambda-Mu-Sigma (LMS) method, the full distribution — 2.5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 97.5th percentiles — is modeled as a set of smooth curves across every year of age (Bohn et al., 2023). A result is then read not as "in the 6–12 box" but as a percentile at that child's exact age, trackable over time. This is the same percentile thinking from the first half of this chapter, now made continuous.

Optimal moves across the lifespan

Adults are not exempt from this — the change just slows down. Consider total testosterone in men, a marker routinely misread because most reports carry one wide adult interval. A validated normative model pooling 13 studies across ages 3 to 88 produced centile curves for every year of age (Kelsey et al., 2014). The findings are more interesting than the usual "testosterone declines with age" headline. Mean total testosterone peaks around age 19, declines to about age 40, and then — contrary to popular belief — roughly plateaus rather than continuing to fall. What genuinely changes after 40 is the variance: the spread of normal values widens dramatically.

Sit with what widening variance does to interpretation. A given numeric value can be middling for a 25-year-old and comfortably above-average for a 65-year-old, because the whole distribution has spread out beneath it. The number didn't move; the band moved. Read against one broad adult interval you would miss this entirely — you would call the same value "normal" for both men and stop, when in fact it occupies two very different positions. This is the marriage of the chapter's two skills: position only means something once you have chosen the right, age-appropriate band to read it against.

Think About It

A testosterone value sits at the 50th percentile for a 25-year-old man. If variance widens with age but the median plateaus after 40, would that same value read as roughly mid-range, clearly high, or clearly low for a 65-year-old? Why does the spread of the band, not just its center, decide the answer?

Sex and physiological state redraw the map

Age is one axis; sex is another, and the two interact. Higgins and Adeli (2016) documented that several pediatric biomarker trajectories are indistinguishable in early childhood and then diverge sharply at puberty as sex hormones reshape physiology. From that point the "optimal" band for many markers is genuinely different by sex, not as a rounding convenience but as biology.

And then there is pregnancy — the most dramatic single example of a life stage that rewrites nearly every reference interval at once.

Pregnancy: a different physiology, a different ruler

Pregnancy is not "adult physiology plus a baby." It is a distinct, rapidly shifting metabolic state, and applying non-pregnant reference intervals to it is one of the most common interpretive errors in laboratory medicine. Bohn and Adeli (2022) reviewed how pregnancy-induced adaptations move markers across virtually every panel — thyroid, hepatic, renal, hematologic, cardiac, inflammatory — and found that most laboratories nonetheless still report against non-pregnant adult intervals. The result is predictable: entirely normal pregnancy changes get flagged as pathology, and occasionally real problems hide inside a band that no longer applies.

The scale of the shifts is striking. A large cross-sectional study of 13,656 healthy pregnant women in China built gestational-age-specific reference intervals for 15 biochemical markers and documented dramatic, trimester-by-trimester movement: lipids rise substantially, renal markers fall as plasma volume expands and filtration increases, and liver enzymes shift in ways that would look abnormal against a non-pregnant ruler (Dai et al., 2017). Crucially, these intervals are gestational-age-specific — not one "pregnancy band" but a moving band that changes across the three trimesters. The ruler itself is in motion.

The takeaway for a reader of bloodwork is not to memorize pregnancy intervals. It is the habit: before you read position, confirm you are holding the right ruler. Age, sex, and physiological state each answer the question "which band?" — and only after you've answered it does percentile position mean anything.

A working procedure you can reuse on every panel

Distill the chapter into a four-step read you can run on any value, on any report, for the rest of the course:

1. Choose the right ruler. Before anything else, ask "good for whom?" Confirm the reference interval matches this person's age, sex, and physiological state. If the report carries one broad adult band and the person is a teenager, an older adult, or pregnant, treat that band as approximate at best.

2. Locate the position. Where in the band does the value sit — low-normal, mid, high-normal — and roughly what percentile? Hugging a fence is a signal even when the flag says normal.

3. Ask whether the fence is trustworthy. Some intervals, like ferritin's lower limit, are known to be poorly founded (Truong et al., 2024). A value that is "normal" against a shaky fence deserves more scrutiny, not less.

4. Route, don't diagnose. A striking position is a reason for evaluation and conversation with a qualified clinician — not a self-diagnosis, and not a cue to intervene on your own. Interpretation is your lane; diagnosis is theirs.

Run those four steps and you have replaced a one-bit flag with a rich, person-specific read. That is the entire interpretive posture of this course, compressed to a checklist.

The central 95% is a convention of the statistician, not a promise of the body. Where you sit inside it — and whether "it" was even built for someone like you — is where the meaning lives.

Key Takeaways

  • A reference interval captures the central 95% of a reference population by convention — a statistical choice, not a biological target (Ozarda, 2016).
  • Read position, not the flag: express a value as a percentile and a zone (low-, mid-, high-normal). Two "normal" results can carry very different meaning.
  • A single broad interval is often several narrower, age- and sex-specific bands flattened together for convenience; partitioning restores the lost detail (Ozarda, 2016; Adeli et al., 2017).
  • Pediatric markers change continuously with development; continuous LMS centile curves model the full distribution across age better than discrete boxes (Higgins & Adeli, 2016; Bohn et al., 2023).
  • Optimal shifts across the lifespan: male testosterone plateaus after 40 but its variance widens, so the same number changes percentile with age (Kelsey et al., 2014).
  • Pregnancy is a distinct, trimester-shifting physiology; applying non-pregnant intervals misreads normal changes as pathology (Bohn & Adeli, 2022; Dai et al., 2017).
  • Some fences are poorly founded — ferritin's lower limit chief among them — so a "normal" value near a shaky limit warrants extra scrutiny (Truong et al., 2024).
  • Reading position is interpretation, not diagnosis. Striking values are signs that warrant medical evaluation, routed to a qualified clinician.
Looking Ahead
