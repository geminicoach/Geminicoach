Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Warren, 58
Consider Warren, who has had his 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) checked twice this year: 32 ng/mL in November, and 34 ng/mL in June. The two numbers are close enough that Warren figures his vitamin D status is "basically stable" and stops thinking about it. He has taken metformin for type 2 diabetes for several years. A routine complete blood count (CBC) this month flagged a mildly elevated mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and Warren has noticed some fatigue and occasional tingling in his hands that he has chalked up to a busy stretch at work. He read online that folic acid "is for anemia" and is considering picking some up before his next appointment. He has not yet had his vitamin B12 (cobalamin) checked this year.

Your Tasks

  1. Using the seasonal curve concept from the chapter, explain why Warren's close November and June 25(OH)D readings do not necessarily mean his vitamin D status is stable across the full year.
  2. Explain why Warren's metformin use and the elevated MCV flag on his CBC are reasons to evaluate his B12 status, not just folate, and describe what methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine could add if his B12 result comes back in the grey zone.
  3. Explain the risk in Warren's plan to simply start taking a folic acid supplement to address the MCV flag, using the methylfolate trap to explain what could happen to an underlying B12 problem if it gets masked this way.
  4. Give one practical, general-education next step Warren could raise with his clinician, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains that Warren's near-matching November and June readings sit on opposite slopes of the same seasonal curve, and are not proof of a stable status.
  • Connects metformin use and the elevated MCV flag to a reason for evaluating B12, not folate alone, and explains what MMA and homocysteine could add in a grey-zone result, including the kidney-function caveat on MMA.
  • Explains the methylfolate trap: folic acid could normalize the blood count while an underlying B12 shortage, and its risk to nerve tissue, keeps progressing unnoticed.
  • Frames "normal" or closely matching numbers as not automatically reassuring, for both vitamin D and B12.
  • Offers a practical, general-education next step and clearly states where general education ends and clinical care begins, without diagnosing.
