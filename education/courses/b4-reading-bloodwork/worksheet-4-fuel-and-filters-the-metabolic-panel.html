Part A · Two Timescales, One Story: Fasting Glucose and HbA1c

  1. In two or three sentences, explain the difference between a reference range and a decision limit, and say which one fasting glucose and HbA1c both are.
  2. The estimated average glucose (eAG) formula translates an HbA1c percentage into familiar glucose units: eAG (mg/dL) equals 28.7 times HbA1c minus 46.7. Part B · The Quiet Signal: Fasting Insulin and HOMA-IR

Fasting insulin and the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) are the chapter's flagship example of a marker that can drift for years while glucose and HbA1c still look normal.

  1. Explain, mechanically, what happens to insulin secretion when cells become less responsive to insulin, and why fasting glucose and HbA1c can both stay normal for years while that process is underway.
  2. The HOMA-IR formula is: fasting glucose in mg/dL, multiplied by fasting insulin in microunits per milliliter, divided by 405. Calculate HOMA-IR for a fasting glucose of 95 mg/dL and a fasting insulin of 18 microunits per milliliter. Show your work.
  3. Explain why HOMA-IR does not have one single, internationally agreed decision limit the way a diabetes diagnosis does, and what that means for how a reader should treat a high or rising result.
  4. In your own words, explain why a rising fasting insulin or HOMA-IR, while glucose and HbA1c both read normal, is described as the clearest "in range is not optimal" case on the whole metabolic panel.

Part C · Electrolytes and Hydration

Sodium and potassium are dissolved minerals that govern hydration, nerve firing, and muscle contraction, and both are unusually sensitive to how a person drank and trained in the day or two before the draw.

  1. Explain why sodium is described as "a hydration signal read backward" rather than a measure of how much salt a person ate.
  2. Distinguish hyponatremia from hypernatremia: what direction does each move sodium, and what typically drives each one in an exercise setting?
  3. Explain why "drinking to thirst," rather than drinking as much as physically possible, is the safer default for endurance athletes. Name the mechanism involved.
  4. Sweat sodium concentration varies roughly tenfold between individuals. Explain why that variation matters for how a person should think about electrolyte replacement, and why a single flagged value right after a hot workout is not automatically a red flag.

Part D · The Filters: Creatinine, BUN, and eGFR

Creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) track how well the kidneys clear waste, and this is where muscle mass and recent exercise can make a healthy person look temporarily "off."

  1. Explain, mechanically, why creatinine is described as both a waste product and a "muscle signal."
  2. Name the two separate confounders that can raise creatinine, and lower a creatinine-based eGFR, without any real change in how well the kidneys are filtering. Explain what causes each one.
  3. Explain why BUN is not a reliable stand-alone kidney marker, and why clinicians often look at BUN and creatinine together rather than at either one alone.
  4. Explain why eGFR is described as "estimated" rather than measured, name its inputs, and explain why the same eGFR value can mean something different in a healthy 25-year-old than it does in a healthy 80-year-old.

Part E · Reason Through a Confounder

Use the "read the context before reading the flag" logic from the chapter.

  1. A well-trained, muscular person gets a mildly low eGFR flag the week after a heavy squat cycle. Walk through the reasoning: what two things would you want to know before drawing any conclusion, what single retest condition would most cleanly separate a training artifact from a real signal, and why?
  2. A person's sodium reads 134 mmol/L, drawn two hours after finishing a hot half marathon during which they drank generously. Is this the kind of value that would be expected to resolve with rest and normal hydration, or the kind that should be treated as an urgent concern? Explain your reasoning, and describe what a sensible next step looks like.

Where education ends
This worksheet builds a sharper reader of the metabolic panel, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the system works, treat any single flag as a prompt to ask why rather than a verdict, and treat a value that stays abnormal after rest and normal hydration, or a marker that keeps trending in one direction across repeated panels, as a sign that warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
