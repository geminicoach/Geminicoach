The Scenario

Scenario · Cole, mid-season endurance athlete
Consider Cole, who has been training for an upcoming triathlon and had an annual physical land in the middle of the heaviest week of the training block. Cole's complete blood count (CBC) came back with a hemoglobin of 12.9 g/dL, comfortably inside the reference interval printed on the report and carrying no flag, but sitting in the lower half of the range. The mean corpuscular volume (MCV) came back at 90 fL, and the red cell distribution width (RDW) was unremarkable. A training partner glanced at the number, said Cole "has anemia," and suggested cutting back on training right away. Cole isn't sure whether to be concerned or whether the training partner's read of the report is even accurate.

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, what a hemoglobin value actually measures, and why the chapter describes it as a concentration rather than a straightforward count of red cells.
  2. Explain what dilutional pseudoanemia is, why endurance training produces it, and why it is not the same thing as true anemia.
  3. Use Cole's MCV and RDW to reason through whether this pattern looks more consistent with dilutional pseudoanemia or with a developing iron deficiency, and name the one follow-up test that would settle the question.
  4. Decide whether the training partner's reaction was a responsible read of the report, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains that hemoglobin and hematocrit are concentrations, and that plasma volume expansion dilutes them without changing true red-cell mass.
  • Explains dilutional pseudoanemia as a mechanism tied to endurance training, and distinguishes it from true anemia.
  • Uses MCV and RDW to reason toward the benign pattern, while naming ferritin as the real tie-breaker against iron deficiency.
  • Evaluates the training partner's "anemia" claim as overreach, and explains why a single number read without context cannot support a diagnosis.
  • Offers a practical, general-education framing and states clearly where general education ends.
  • Identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose.
