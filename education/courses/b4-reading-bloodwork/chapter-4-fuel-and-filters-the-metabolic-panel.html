Fuel and Filters: The Metabolic Panel

How your body manages sugar, water, and waste — and why the same lifestyle that keeps you healthy can make a marker look "off."

60 min read 9 cited sources

Two people get the identical result on their annual panel: fasting glucose of 98 mg/dL, sitting quietly inside the reference range. The first is sedentary, sleeps five hours a night, and is drifting slowly toward a problem the single number can't yet see. The second lifted heavy that morning, slept eight hours, eats well — and the 98 is just a normal day. Same digit, two entirely different stories. The metabolic panel is full of these traps, and the whole skill of this chapter is learning to read past the number to the story underneath it.

Think of the metabolic panel as your body's fuel-and-plumbing report. It answers three practical questions: How well are you managing your fuel (blood sugar)? Is your fluid and mineral balance where it should be (electrolytes)? And are your filters clearing waste properly (kidney markers)? We'll take each in turn — and along the way you'll meet a confounder that will follow you into the liver chapter: muscle and recent training can nudge these numbers in ways that look alarming but aren't.

Before we start: "in range" is not "optimal"

Recall the distinction from Chapter 1 between a reference range — the statistical spread of a general population — and a decision limit — a threshold tied to actual outcomes. The metabolic panel is where that distinction stops being academic and starts changing behavior. Glucose and HbA1c thresholds are decision limits: lines drawn by researchers because on one side of them, the risk of developing diabetes and its complications climbs measurably. That means the interesting territory isn't "flagged vs. not flagged." It's the wide, unflagged band between genuinely optimal and the pre-diabetes cutoff — the exact zone where diet, sleep, and training changes show up first.

One rule frames everything below: a genuinely out-of-range value is a sign that warrants medical evaluation, not a diagnosis you make at your desk. Your job as a reader — whether you're a lay learner, a coach, or an RD earning CE — is to interpret position and context well enough to know when a number is telling a real story and when it's an artifact of how you lived the day before the draw.

Story one: glucose regulation on two timescales

Blood sugar is the panel's headline act, and it's reported through two markers that measure the same thing over wildly different windows. Understanding why they sometimes disagree is the single most useful thing in this chapter.

Fasting glucose: the snapshot

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) is exactly what it sounds like — the concentration of sugar in your blood after roughly 8–12 hours without food. It's a snapshot of one moment: this morning, in this state of hydration, sleep, and stress. The conventional decision limits are worth memorizing because they anchor every conversation:

  • Below ~100 mg/dL — considered normal fasting glucose.
  • 100–125 mg/dL — impaired fasting glucose, the "pre-diabetes" band.
  • 126 mg/dL or above (confirmed on a second test) — the diabetes threshold.

Notice the granularity. Optimal isn't just "under 126" — many clinicians and most metabolic-health frameworks would rather see fasting glucose comfortably in the 80s to low 90s. A value of 98 is "in range" and simultaneously worth watching, especially if it's been creeping upward year over year. That upward drift, invisible in any single result, is often the earliest readable signal on the whole panel.

But the snapshot has a weakness: it's noisy. A poor night's sleep, an argument on the way to the lab, an intense workout the day before, even the stress of the needle can nudge a fasting reading. That's precisely why we don't rely on it alone.

HbA1c: the long memory

HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) solves the noise problem by measuring a slow, cumulative process. Glucose in your blood sticks to hemoglobin inside red blood cells, and it stays stuck for the life of that cell — roughly two to three months. So the percentage of your hemoglobin that's glycated reflects your average glucose over the preceding ~8–12 weeks, not any single morning. It can't be gamed by fasting the night before; it's a running average with a long memory.

The landmark work here is the ADAG trial (Nathan et al., 2008), which enrolled 507 people across ten centers — type 1, type 2, and nondiabetic — and pinned down the linear relationship between A1c and average glucose. Their equation lets you translate an A1c percentage into an estimated average glucose (eAG) in the same mg/dL units you see on a fasting result:

eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × A1c − 46.7

Nathan et al. (2008), the ADAG study equation

Run the numbers and the thresholds line up intuitively. An A1c of 5.7% — the low edge of pre-diabetes — corresponds to an average glucose of about 117 mg/dL. An A1c of 6.5% — the diabetes line — maps to roughly 140 mg/dL average. Below 5.7% is considered normal, and metabolic-health enthusiasts often target the low-to-mid 5s.

A recent systematic review sharpens the pre-diabetes band into actionable tiers rather than one blunt cutoff. Karczewski et al. (2026) propose treating the range as a gradient: 5.7–5.9% as a prompt for low-intensity prevention, 6.0–6.1% for structured risk-factor modification, and 6.2–6.4% for intensive lifestyle intervention. Their central message echoes this whole course: risk is continuous, thresholds are conveniences, and the same A1c means different things depending on ethnicity, BMI, and family history.

Why the two markers disagree — and why that's the point

Here's the crux. Because fasting glucose is a snapshot and A1c is a running average, they capture different physiological signals, and they routinely classify the same person differently. In a population study of older adults, Khambatta et al. (2017) found only about 30% overlap between the two tests in flagging pre-diabetes — with formal agreement so poor (κ = 0.22–0.34) that the authors concluded the tests are simply measuring different things. A1c caught more cases overall, and the mismatch was especially pronounced in some subgroups.

Practically, this gives you a two-timescale view of metabolic health:

  • Fasting glucose normal, A1c elevated? Your mornings look fine, but your average day — post-meal spikes, afternoon snacking, overnight highs — is running warmer than the snapshot suggests. This is a common pattern and a strong argument for the A1c's value.
  • Fasting glucose elevated, A1c normal? Possibly a noisy fasting draw (stress, a hard prior-day workout, short sleep), or a "dawn phenomenon" where the morning number runs high while the rest of the day is well-controlled. Worth a recheck before you worry.
  • Both elevated? Now the signal is consistent across timescales — the strongest case that a real change is underway and a conversation with a clinician is warranted.

This is also why a two-week diet experiment can be so misleading if you only look at one marker. Cut carbs sharply and your daily glucose trace drops within days — but your A1c, dragged by the two-month memory of red blood cells, will barely have budged. The snapshot cheers; the long memory shrugs. Neither is lying. The widget below lets you feel this lag directly.

Story two: electrolytes and the water you swim in

If glucose is fuel, electrolytes are the chemistry of the fluid that fuel travels in. Sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), chloride, and bicarbonate are dissolved minerals that carry electrical charge, and together they govern hydration, blood pressure, nerve firing, and muscle contraction — including the biggest muscle you own, the heart. Because your body defends these concentrations fiercely, the numbers are usually stable — which means when they do move, they move for a reason, and they're exquisitely sensitive to how you drank, sweated, and trained recently.

Sodium: it's about water, not salt

The most common misconception is that a sodium result reports how much salt you ate. It doesn't. Blood sodium is a concentration — sodium relative to water — so it's really a hydration signal. Drink far more than you sweat and you dilute your sodium; lose water faster than you replace it and you concentrate it. Normal sits around 135–145 mmol/L.

Exercise makes this vivid. In their review of exercise-associated electrolyte disorders, Hew-Butler, Smith-Hale, and Sabou (2019) describe both directions: hyponatremia (plasma Na⁺ below 135) from overzealous hydration, and hypernatremia from severe fluid restriction. The 2017 update on exercise-associated hyponatremia (Hew-Butler et al., 2017) is emphatic about the mechanism: the dangerous drops come from overdrinking beyond thirst, compounded by non-osmotic release of the water-retaining hormone AVP. The counterintuitive lesson for endurance athletes is that drinking to thirst — not drinking as much as possible — is the safer default.

Potassium: small window, big consequences

Potassium lives in a narrow range (roughly 3.5–5.0 mmol/L) and matters enormously because the heart's rhythm depends on it. Both directions have exercise links. Prolonged endurance work with heavy sweating can pull potassium down (hypokalemia), while unaccustomed high-intensity training severe enough to damage muscle — rhabdomyolysis — can dump potassium into the blood (hyperkalemia), as Hew-Butler et al. (2019) note. A meaningfully abnormal potassium is one of the results on this panel you should never sit on; it's a straightforward prompt for prompt medical attention.

Why sweat makes electrolytes so personal

Here's a subtlety that trips up even experienced athletes: how much sodium you lose in sweat varies enormously from person to person. In her comprehensive review, Baker (2017) documents sweat sodium concentrations ranging from about 10 to over 100 mmol/L across individuals — a tenfold spread — driven by intensity, heat acclimation, aerobic fitness, sex, diet, and genetics. That's the scientific basis for personalized electrolyte replacement: a "salty sweater" finishing a hot session has genuinely different needs than someone whose shirt stays white. It also means a post-workout electrolyte panel reflects not just how hard you trained but the particular chemistry of your sweat.

The takeaway for reading your own results: an electrolyte value slightly off after a hard, sweaty session in the heat, or after a day of poor drinking, often reflects that context rather than disease. Rehydrate sensibly, retest in a normal state, and see whether it settles. A value that stays abnormal when you're rested and normally hydrated is the one that warrants evaluation.

Story three: the filters — kidney markers

Your kidneys clear metabolic waste from the blood, and the panel tracks how well they're doing it through three linked numbers: creatinine, BUN, and the estimated filtration rate calculated from them. This is also where the chapter's central confounder — muscle and recent training — does its most dramatic work, so read this section with your skeptic's hat on.

Creatinine: waste, but also a muscle signal

Creatinine is a waste product of normal muscle metabolism. Your muscles produce it at a fairly steady rate, your kidneys filter it out, and the blood level reflects the balance. When kidneys filter poorly, creatinine rises — which is why it's a workhorse kidney marker. But here's the catch that makes it treacherous: creatinine also depends on how much muscle you have and what your muscles have been doing.

The muscle-mass effect is real and measurable. Using more than 8,000 adults from the PREVEND cohort, Groothof et al. (2022) showed that muscle mass is a key determinant of creatinine independent of kidney function — and that people with high muscle mass were often misclassified when their creatinine-based filtration estimate was compared against a muscle-mass-independent marker (cystatin C). In plain terms: a muscular person can have a creatinine that looks mildly elevated purely because they carry more muscle, not because their kidneys are struggling.

The recent-exercise effect is even sharper. Beunders et al. (2020) had subjects exercise and watched serum creatinine jump from about 58 to 71 µmol/L, with the estimated filtration rate appearing to fall by roughly 34 mL/min — a drop that on paper could mimic acute kidney injury. Crucially, the mechanism wasn't failing kidneys at all; it was creatinine released from working muscle cells. A control biomarker that doesn't come from muscle (PENK) stayed flat, proving the point. The nephrology core curriculum by Becerra and Mansour (2026) extends this to the extreme case: ultramarathon runners routinely show transient creatinine spikes from muscle breakdown that complicate — and can falsely suggest — a kidney-injury diagnosis.

So a muscular person, or a recently-trained person, may look "off" on creatinine while being perfectly healthy. This is the exact same lesson you'll meet again in the liver chapter, where muscle-derived enzymes play a parallel trick. Reading position and context, not the flag alone, is what protects you from a false alarm.

BUN: protein and hydration in the mix

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is another waste product the kidneys clear, but it reflects protein metabolism and hydration as much as filtration. A high-protein diet raises it; dehydration concentrates it; overhydration dilutes it. Because it's so context-sensitive, BUN is most useful alongside creatinine rather than alone — clinicians often look at the BUN-to-creatinine ratio to distinguish, say, dehydration from a true kidney problem.

eGFR: a formula, not a measurement

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is the summary statistic most people fixate on — it's meant to express how many milliliters of blood your kidneys filter per minute, standardized to body size. But the word to underline is estimated. Standard eGFR isn't measured directly; it's calculated from creatinine, plus age and sex. That means every confounder that inflates creatinine — high muscle mass, a recent hard workout, a big steak dinner — flows straight through the formula and lowers your apparent eGFR, even when your actual filtration is fine.

This is why both Groothof et al. (2022) and Becerra and Mansour (2026) point toward cystatin C, a filtration marker that doesn't come from muscle, when muscle mass is unusual or variable. For your purposes as a reader, the actionable rule is simpler: if a lean-to-muscular, well-trained person sees a mildly reduced eGFR flag right after a heavy training block, the smart first move is to retest after several rested, well-hydrated days — not to conclude anything about kidney disease. A value that stays low under those calm conditions, or any value with a large or worsening change, is a genuine sign that warrants medical follow-up.

Putting it together: three worked examples

The panel earns its keep when you read the markers as a system, in context. Walk through these.

Example 1 — The "creeping" fasting glucose

A 44-year-old desk worker has fasting glucose of 101 mg/dL (just over the line) and A1c of 5.6% (just under it). Last year both were slightly lower. Neither number is a diagnosis, but the trend and the fact that both timescales agree in direction make this a textbook "act now, while it's cheap" moment — the pre-diabetes territory where diet, sleep, and zone-2 training reliably show up. The right move isn't alarm; it's a conversation and a re-test after a genuine lifestyle change, expecting fasting glucose to respond first and A1c to confirm months later.

Example 2 — The salty-sweat electrolyte scare

A recreational marathoner has blood drawn two hours after a hot long run and shows sodium at 134 mmol/L — flagged low. Given Baker's (2017) documentation of huge sweat-sodium variability and Hew-Butler et al.'s (2017) work on exercise-associated hyponatremia, the leading explanation is context: heavy sweat losses plus generous fluid intake diluting the sample. The response is to rehydrate sensibly and retest in a rested, normally hydrated state. If it normalizes, story solved. If 134 persists at rest, that's the version that warrants evaluation.

Example 3 — The muscular "kidney problem"

A 30-year-old strength athlete, tested the morning after a heavy session, shows creatinine of 1.35 mg/dL and an eGFR flagged mildly low. This is the Beunders et al. (2020) and Groothof et al. (2022) pattern almost exactly: high muscle mass plus recent muscle work inflating creatinine, which then drags the creatinine-based eGFR down — with no actual filtration problem. The intelligent path is a rested, hydrated retest and, if uncertainty remains, a cystatin C–based estimate that sidesteps the muscle confounder entirely. What you don't do is diagnose kidney disease from a single post-training draw.

Across all three, the discipline is identical: identify which timescale or context each marker reflects, ask what you did in the days before the draw, and treat a persistent, rested, well-hydrated abnormality — not a single flag — as the real prompt for medical follow-up.

The flag tells you a number crossed a line. It never tells you why — and on the metabolic panel, "why" is usually hiding in how you slept, drank, and trained the day before.

The reading-in-context principle, applied to fuel and filters

Key Takeaways

  • Fasting glucose is a noisy snapshot of "right now"; HbA1c is a smoothed ~8–12 week average. They measure the same thing on different timescales and often disagree — that disagreement is informative, not an error (Nathan et al., 2008; Khambatta et al., 2017).
  • Use eAG = 28.7 × A1c − 46.7 to translate A1c into familiar glucose units, and treat the pre-diabetes band (A1c 5.7–6.4%) as a graded call to action, not a single wall (Karczewski et al., 2026).
  • Blood sodium is a hydration ratio, not a salt-intake tally; overdrinking beyond thirst — not underdrinking — is the main driver of dangerous exercise-associated low sodium (Hew-Butler et al., 2017).
  • Sweat sodium losses vary up to tenfold between people, so electrolyte needs and post-exercise values are genuinely personal (Baker, 2017).
  • Creatinine reflects muscle and recent exercise as well as kidney function; high muscle mass and hard workouts can inflate it and falsely lower creatinine-based eGFR (Groothof et al., 2022; Beunders et al., 2020; Becerra & Mansour, 2026).
  • eGFR is estimated from creatinine, so every creatinine confounder flows into it. When muscle mass is unusual, a rested retest or a cystatin C–based estimate clarifies the picture.
  • "In range" is not "optimal," and a genuinely out-of-range value is a sign that warrants medical evaluation — read after rest and normal hydration before drawing conclusions.
Looking Ahead

Next we turn to the liver panel — and you'll meet the muscle confounder again, wearing a different disguise. The enzymes we use to read liver health also leak from working muscle, so a hard leg day can light up a "liver" flag that has nothing to do with your liver. Same principle, new organ: read position and context before you read alarm.
