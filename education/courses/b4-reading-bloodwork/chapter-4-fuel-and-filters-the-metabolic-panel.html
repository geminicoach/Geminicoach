Fuel and Filters: The Metabolic Panel

How your body manages sugar, water, and waste, and why the same lifestyle that keeps you healthy can make a marker look off.

60 min read 14 cited sources

Two people get back the identical result on their annual panel: fasting glucose of 98 mg/dL, sitting quietly inside the reference range. The first is sedentary, sleeps five hours a night, and is drifting slowly toward a problem the single number cannot yet see. The second trained hard that same morning, sleeps eight hours most nights, and eats with real intention, and for that person the 98 is just an ordinary Tuesday. Same digit, two entirely different stories. The metabolic panel is full of traps exactly like this one, and the whole skill of this chapter is learning to read past the number to the story underneath it.

Think of the metabolic panel as your body's fuel and plumbing report. It answers three practical questions. How well are you managing your fuel, meaning blood sugar and the insulin that quietly manages it behind the scenes? Is your fluid and mineral balance where it should be, meaning the electrolytes? And are your filters clearing waste properly, meaning the kidney markers? We will take each of these three stories in turn, and along the way you will meet a confounder that follows you into the liver chapter later in this course: muscle and recent training can nudge these numbers in ways that look alarming and are not.

Throughout this chapter you will follow one learner, Elena, a 34-year-old who trains for sprint triathlons three mornings a week and treats her annual bloodwork the way she treats a race split, as a number to beat. Her results came back with three things that made her sit up. Her fasting insulin was flagged high-normal even though her glucose and hemoglobin A1c looked fine. Her sodium read a touch low after a hot Saturday long run. And her creatinine nudged her estimated filtration rate downward the week after a heavy squat cycle. Nothing on the page was dramatically abnormal, and nothing was clearly nothing either. Elena is not a patient in this course and she is not a case to be solved. She is a stand-in for an honest, common experience: a panel that reads as almost entirely in range and still leaves a careful reader with real questions. By the end of this chapter you will be better equipped to answer those questions than she was on the day she opened the results.

Before we start: "in range" is not "optimal"

Recall the distinction introduced earlier in this course between a reference range, the statistical spread of a general population, and a decision limit, a threshold tied to actual health outcomes. The metabolic panel is where that distinction stops being academic and starts changing behavior. Glucose and hemoglobin A1c thresholds are decision limits: lines drawn by researchers because on one side of them, the risk of developing diabetes and its complications climbs measurably. That means the genuinely interesting territory is not flagged versus not flagged. It is the wide, unflagged band between what is truly optimal and the pre-diabetes cutoff, the exact zone where diet, sleep, and training changes show up first, long before a lab report would ever say anything about it.

Two further wrinkles complicate a simple in-range verdict on this particular panel, and both will come back later in concrete form. First, a healthy target is not always the same width across a life span. An estimated filtration rate or a glucose target that makes sense for a healthy 28-year-old is not automatically the right yardstick for a healthy 78-year-old, and treating every age as if it should hit an identical number is its own kind of error. Second, some of the panel's most informative markers, fasting insulin chief among them, do not come with a single, universally agreed decision limit the way glucose does. They are read as trends and patterns, not as a pass or fail line, which makes them easy to underrate and easy to misread in both directions.

One rule frames everything below: a genuinely out-of-range value is a sign that warrants medical evaluation, not a diagnosis you make at your own kitchen table. Your job as a reader, whether you are working through this material for your own understanding or supporting someone else's, is to interpret position and context well enough to know when a number is telling a real story and when it is an artifact of how you lived the day before the draw.

Story one: glucose regulation on two timescales

Blood sugar is the panel's headline act, and it is reported through markers that measure the same underlying story over wildly different windows, from a single morning to several years. Understanding why they sometimes disagree, and why one of them can be moving long before the others notice, is the single most useful thing in this chapter.

Fasting glucose: the snapshot

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) is exactly what it sounds like, the concentration of sugar in your blood after roughly 8 to 12 hours without food. It is a snapshot of one moment: this morning, in this state of hydration, sleep, and stress. The conventional decision limits are worth memorizing because they anchor every conversation that follows, and they are consistent with the most recent American Diabetes Association Standards of Care (American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee, 2026):

  • Below about 100 mg/dL, considered normal fasting glucose.
  • 100 to 125 mg/dL, impaired fasting glucose, the "pre-diabetes" band.
  • 126 mg/dL or above, confirmed on a second test, the diabetes threshold.

Notice the granularity that a simple in-range verdict misses. Optimal is not just "under 126." Many clinicians and most metabolic-health frameworks would rather see fasting glucose comfortably in the 80s to low 90s. A value of 98 is in range and simultaneously worth watching, especially if it has been creeping upward year over year. That upward drift, invisible in any single result, is often the earliest readable signal on the whole panel.

These decision limits are written for healthy adults in general, but they genuinely bend for one important group, which is the first of this chapter's age bands. For older adults, especially those managing several chronic conditions or with a limited life expectancy, professional guidance relaxes glucose and A1c targets on purpose, because for that population the real risk of driving blood sugar too low, dangerous hypoglycemia, can outweigh the marginal benefit of very tight control. A target that looks lenient on paper for an 80-year-old with multiple health conditions is not a lower standard of care. It is the same underlying goal, minimizing net harm, applied to a body where the balance of risks has genuinely shifted. Age-banding a target is not a courtesy. It is what taking outcomes seriously actually requires.

The snapshot also has a real weakness: it is noisy. A poor night of sleep, an argument on the way to the lab, an intense workout the day before, even the simple stress of the blood draw itself can nudge a fasting reading up or down. That noise is exactly why we do not rely on a single fasting value alone.

HbA1c: the long memory

Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) solves the noise problem by measuring a slow, cumulative process instead of a single moment. Glucose circulating in your blood sticks to hemoglobin inside red blood cells, and it stays stuck for the life of that cell, roughly two to three months. So the percentage of your hemoglobin that is glycated reflects your average glucose over the preceding eight to twelve weeks, not any single morning. It cannot be gamed by fasting the night before a test. It is a running average with a long memory.

The landmark work here is the A1c-Derived Average Glucose trial (Nathan et al., 2008), which enrolled 507 people across ten centers, spanning type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and people without diabetes, and pinned down the linear relationship between A1c and average glucose. Their equation translates an A1c percentage into an estimated average glucose (eAG), expressed in the same mg/dL units you see on a fasting result:

eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × A1c - 46.7

the A1c-Derived Average Glucose (ADAG) study equation, Nathan et al. (2008)

Run the numbers and the thresholds line up intuitively. An A1c of 5.7%, the low edge of pre-diabetes, corresponds to an average glucose of about 117 mg/dL. An A1c of 6.5%, the diabetes line, maps to roughly 140 mg/dL average. Below 5.7% is considered normal, and many metabolic-health-minded clinicians and patients target the low-to-mid 5s.

A recent systematic review sharpens the pre-diabetes band into actionable tiers rather than one blunt cutoff. Karczewski, Karczewski, and Olsen (2026) propose treating the range as a gradient: 5.7 to 5.9% as a prompt for low-intensity prevention, 6.0 to 6.1% for structured risk-factor modification, and 6.2 to 6.4% for intensive lifestyle intervention. Their central message echoes the theme of this whole course: risk is continuous, thresholds are conveniences, and the same A1c means something different depending on a person's ethnicity, body composition, and family history.

The insulin signal hiding underneath

If an earlier chapter on the thyroid gave you the clearest example of a value that can sit inside a wide reference range while a person's actual physiology has drifted from its own optimum, fasting insulin and the calculation built from it give you the second clearest example, and arguably the most consequential one on this entire panel.

Here is the mechanism worth sitting with. Insulin's job is to move glucose out of the blood and into cells. When cells become less responsive to that signal, a state called insulin resistance, the pancreas does not simply let glucose drift upward. It compensates, secreting more insulin to force the same amount of glucose into cells that used to respond to less. For years, this compensation can work. Glucose stays normal. Hemoglobin A1c stays normal. And underneath both of those reassuring numbers, fasting insulin is quietly climbing, because the pancreas is working harder and harder to produce the same normal-looking result.

This is not a hypothetical sequence. Tabák and colleagues (2009), analyzing nearly a decade of repeated testing in more than 6,500 participants in the Whitehall II study, tracked the years leading up to a type 2 diabetes diagnosis and found that insulin sensitivity had already been declining, and insulin secretion already rising to compensate, three to six years before diagnosis, well before fasting and post-meal glucose showed the kind of change that would trigger a flag. By the time glucose itself crosses a decision limit, the underlying insulin story has usually been unfolding for years. Glucose and A1c are the smoke alarm. Insulin resistance is the fire that has already been burning quietly for a long time before the alarm goes off.

Homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) is the calculation researchers built to put a number on that quiet process using only two fasting measurements. Developed by Matthews and colleagues (1985) from mathematical modeling of the feedback loop between glucose and insulin, the formula multiplies fasting glucose by fasting insulin and divides by a constant that scales the result to a familiar range: HOMA-IR equals fasting glucose in mg/dL multiplied by fasting insulin in microunits per milliliter, divided by 405.

A lower HOMA-IR reflects a body that needs relatively little insulin to keep glucose in check, meaning good insulin sensitivity. A higher HOMA-IR reflects a body working harder, secreting more insulin, to achieve the same result, meaning insulin resistance. Unlike glucose and A1c, HOMA-IR does not have a single, internationally agreed decision limit the way a diabetes diagnosis does, partly because fasting insulin assays are not standardized the way glucose assays are, so absolute cutoffs vary somewhat between labs and populations. That lack of a bright line is a genuine limitation, not a reason to ignore the marker. Meigs and colleagues (2020) found that combining HbA1c with a HOMA-IR-based measure of insulin resistance meaningfully improved prediction of who would go on to develop type 2 diabetes, over and above what HbA1c could tell you alone, precisely because the two markers are catching different parts of the same slow story at different points along its timeline.

For a reader without a clinician's toolkit, the practical takeaway is not to memorize a specific HOMA-IR cutoff and self-diagnose. It is to treat a fasting insulin or HOMA-IR result that runs persistently high, particularly if it is trending upward across repeated annual panels while glucose and A1c still look normal, as exactly the kind of sign that warrants medical evaluation, even though nothing else on the page is flagged. This is the single clearest illustration in the whole metabolic panel of why "in range" is not the same claim as "optimal." A person can be years into a real physiological drift while every headline number on their report reads clean.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common belief holds that as long as fasting glucose and hemoglobin A1c look normal, blood sugar regulation is a closed question. But glucose and A1c are late indicators precisely because a healthy pancreas is very good at compensating for early insulin resistance by secreting more insulin, which is exactly what keeps glucose looking normal for years. A rising fasting insulin or HOMA-IR while glucose and A1c hold steady is not a false alarm. It may be the earliest honest signal on the entire panel, arriving well before the markers that get all the attention.

Why the timescales disagree, and why that is the point

Because fasting glucose is a snapshot, hemoglobin A1c is a running average, and fasting insulin or HOMA-IR trend even more slowly across years, these markers routinely tell a person different things about the same underlying process, and that disagreement is informative rather than a flaw. In a population study of older adults, Khambatta and colleagues (2017) found only about 30% overlap between fasting glucose and A1c in flagging pre-diabetes, with agreement so poor statistically that the authors concluded the two tests are simply measuring different things. A1c caught more cases overall, and the mismatch was especially pronounced in some subgroups.

Practically, this gives you a layered view of metabolic health, fast to slow:

  • Fasting glucose normal, A1c elevated? Your mornings look fine, but your average day, meaning post-meal spikes, afternoon snacking, and overnight highs, is running warmer than the snapshot suggests.
  • Fasting glucose elevated, A1c normal? Possibly a noisy fasting draw from stress, a hard prior-day workout, or short sleep, or a "dawn phenomenon" where the morning number runs high while the rest of the day is well controlled. Worth a recheck before concern.
  • Glucose and A1c both normal, fasting insulin or HOMA-IR elevated and rising? The earliest and quietest pattern of the three, worth raising with a clinician even though nothing else on the page is flagged.
  • All three elevated together? The signal is now consistent across every timescale, the strongest case that a real change is underway and a conversation with a clinician is genuinely warranted.

This layering is also why a two-week diet experiment can be so misleading if a person only looks at one marker. Cut carbohydrates sharply and a daily glucose trace can drop within days, but A1c, dragged by the two-month memory of red blood cells, will barely have moved, and a HOMA-IR calculated from a fasting insulin drawn that same week may not have budged either, since real improvement in insulin sensitivity tends to build over months of sustained change, not days. None of the three markers is lying. They are simply reporting from different points on the same timeline. The widget below lets you feel that layered lag directly.

Story two: electrolytes and the water you swim in

If glucose and insulin are fuel, electrolytes are the chemistry of the fluid that fuel travels in. Sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), chloride, and bicarbonate are dissolved minerals that carry electrical charge, and together they govern hydration, blood pressure, nerve firing, and muscle contraction, including the biggest muscle a person owns, the heart. Because the body defends these concentrations fiercely, the numbers are usually stable, which means that when they do move, they move for a reason, and they are exquisitely sensitive to how a person drank, sweated, and trained in the day or two before the draw.

Sodium: it is about water, not salt

The most common misconception is that a sodium result reports how much salt a person ate. It does not. Blood sodium is a concentration, sodium relative to water, so it is really a hydration signal read backward. Drink far more than you sweat and you dilute your sodium. Lose water faster than you replace it and you concentrate it. Normal sits around 135 to 145 mmol/L.

Exercise makes this vivid. In their review of exercise-associated electrolyte disorders, Hew-Butler, Smith-Hale, and Sabou (2019) describe both directions: hyponatremia, plasma sodium below 135, from overzealous hydration, and hypernatremia from severe fluid restriction. The 2017 update on exercise-associated hyponatremia (Hew-Butler et al., 2017) is emphatic about the mechanism behind the more dangerous of the two directions: the sharp drops come from overdrinking beyond thirst, compounded by a non-osmotic release of the water-retaining hormone arginine vasopressin (AVP). The counterintuitive lesson for endurance athletes is that drinking to thirst, not drinking as much as physically possible, is the safer default.

Older adults are a special case worth naming here, because the usual defenses against both dehydration and overhydration can be blunted by reduced thirst sensation and by certain common medications, which is one more reason to read a borderline sodium value in the context of the whole person rather than the number alone.

Potassium: small window, big consequences

Potassium lives in a narrow range, roughly 3.5 to 5.0 mmol/L, and matters enormously because the heart's rhythm depends on it staying there. Both directions have exercise links. Prolonged endurance work with heavy sweating can pull potassium down, a state called hypokalemia, while unaccustomed high-intensity training severe enough to damage muscle tissue, a condition called rhabdomyolysis, can dump potassium into the blood, a state called hyperkalemia, as Hew-Butler and colleagues (2019) note. A meaningfully abnormal potassium is one result on this panel that a reader should never sit on. It is a straightforward prompt for prompt medical attention rather than a wait-and-see situation.

Why sweat makes electrolytes so personal

Here is a subtlety that trips up even experienced athletes: how much sodium a person loses in sweat varies enormously from one individual to the next. In her comprehensive review, Baker (2017) documents sweat sodium concentrations ranging from about 10 to over 100 mmol/L across individuals, a tenfold spread, driven by training intensity, heat acclimation, aerobic fitness, sex, diet, and genetics. That tenfold spread is the scientific basis for personalized electrolyte replacement. A "salty sweater" finishing a hot session has genuinely different needs than someone whose shirt stays essentially white. It also means a post-workout electrolyte panel reflects not just how hard a person trained but the particular chemistry of that person's own sweat.

The takeaway for reading your own results: an electrolyte value slightly off after a hard, sweaty session in the heat, or after a day of poor drinking habits, often reflects that context rather than disease. Rehydrate sensibly, retest in a normal state, and see whether the number settles back. A value that stays abnormal when a person is rested and normally hydrated is the one that warrants evaluation.

Story three: the filters, kidney markers

The kidneys clear metabolic waste from the blood, and the panel tracks how well they are doing that job through three linked numbers: creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and the estimated filtration rate calculated from them. This is also where the chapter's central confounder, muscle and recent training, does its most dramatic work, so read this section with a healthy amount of skepticism toward any single flagged value.

Creatinine: waste, but also a muscle signal

Creatinine is a waste product of normal muscle metabolism. Muscle produces it at a fairly steady rate, the kidneys filter it out, and the blood level reflects the balance between the two. When kidneys filter poorly, creatinine rises, which is why it functions as a workhorse kidney marker. But here is the catch that makes it treacherous to read in isolation: creatinine also depends on how much muscle a person has and what that muscle has been doing recently.

The muscle-mass effect is real and measurable. Using more than 8,000 adults from the PREVEND cohort, Groothof and colleagues (2022) showed that muscle mass is a key determinant of creatinine independent of actual kidney function, and that people with high muscle mass were often misclassified when their creatinine-based filtration estimate was compared against a muscle-mass-independent marker called cystatin C. In plain terms, a muscular person can show a creatinine that looks mildly elevated purely because they carry more muscle, not because their kidneys are struggling at all.

The recent-exercise effect is even sharper. Beunders and colleagues (2020) had subjects exercise and watched serum creatinine jump measurably, with the estimated filtration rate appearing to fall by roughly 34 mL per minute on paper, a drop that could mimic acute kidney injury if read without context. Crucially, the mechanism was not failing kidneys at all. It was creatinine released from working muscle cells. A control biomarker that does not originate from muscle stayed flat across the same exercise bout, confirming the source of the change. Aponte Becerra and Mansour (2026), writing the nephrology field's most recent core curriculum review, extend this to an extreme case: ultramarathon runners routinely show transient creatinine spikes from muscle breakdown that complicate, and can falsely suggest, an acute kidney injury diagnosis.

So a muscular person, or a recently trained person, may look off on creatinine while being perfectly healthy. This is nearly the same lesson you will meet again in the liver chapter, where muscle-derived enzymes play a parallel trick on a different panel. Reading position and context, not the flag alone, is what protects a person from a false alarm here.

BUN: protein and hydration in the mix

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is another waste product the kidneys clear, but it reflects protein metabolism and hydration as much as filtration. A high-protein diet raises it. Dehydration concentrates it. Overhydration dilutes it. Because it is so context-sensitive on its own, BUN is most useful alongside creatinine rather than in isolation. Clinicians often look at the ratio between BUN and creatinine to help distinguish something like simple dehydration from a genuine kidney problem.

eGFR: a formula, not a direct measurement, and one that shifts with age

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is the summary statistic most people fixate on. It is meant to express how many milliliters of blood the kidneys filter per minute, standardized to body size. But the word to underline is "estimated." Standard eGFR is not measured directly. It is calculated from creatinine, plus age and sex. That means every confounder that inflates creatinine, high muscle mass, a recent hard workout, a large protein-heavy meal, flows straight through the formula and lowers the apparent eGFR, even when actual filtration is completely fine.

Age deserves its own explanation here, because eGFR is genuinely age-dependent in a way that glucose is not. Kidney function declines gradually across a normal life span, even in people with no kidney disease at all. Astley and colleagues (2025), pooling data from more than 2.5 million adults across nine population studies in seven European countries, mapped what a healthy eGFR actually looks like at each age. In healthy 20-year-olds, the middle of the range sits close to 99 to 101 mL per minute per 1.73 m². By age 80, that same healthy middle has drifted down to roughly 63 to 66, simply as a feature of normal aging, not disease. A value that would be a genuine concern in a healthy 25-year-old can be an entirely unremarkable, age-typical reading in a healthy 80-year-old, which is exactly why applying one fixed cutoff identically at every age is an active and reasonable debate within nephrology rather than a settled matter.

This is also why both Groothof and colleagues (2022) and Aponte Becerra and Mansour (2026) point toward cystatin C, a filtration marker that does not come from muscle, as a useful second opinion when muscle mass is unusual or when age makes a standard cutoff hard to interpret cleanly. For a reader's purposes, the actionable rule is simpler than the underlying formula: if a lean-to-muscular, well-trained person sees a mildly reduced eGFR flag right after a heavy training block, the sensible first move is to retest after several rested, well-hydrated days, not to jump to conclusions about kidney disease. If an older adult sees a moderately reduced eGFR that sits close to what is age-typical for a healthy person their age, that context matters too. A value that stays low under calm, rested, age-adjusted conditions, or any value with a large or worsening change over time, is the genuine signal that warrants medical follow-up.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common assumption treats any reduced eGFR flag as evidence of kidney disease, full stop. But eGFR is a calculation built on creatinine, age, and sex, and every one of those inputs can shift the number without a single glomerulus actually working any worse. A muscular frame, a hard workout the day before, and simple older age can each nudge eGFR down on paper while true filtration stays completely normal. The flag is a prompt to ask why, not a verdict.

Where education ends

Everything in this chapter is built to make a person a sharper reader of their own panel, not a substitute for the clinician who actually orders, interprets, and acts on one. That boundary matters more here than it might first appear, because this particular panel is an easy one to over-interpret confidently at home.

Consider fasting insulin and HOMA-IR again, since it is the marker on this panel most likely to be misused in both directions. Wellness marketing has increasingly discovered these numbers, selling home tests and supplement protocols aimed at "optimizing" a HOMA-IR score, sometimes with real enthusiasm and very little acknowledgment that fasting insulin assays are not standardized the way glucose assays are, that a single value is noisy, and that meaningful interpretation depends on trend, timing, and a person's full clinical picture, not a single number pulled off a chart and compared to an internet cutoff. Overreacting to one borderline HOMA-IR is not more scientific than ignoring one. Both skip the step where an actual clinician looks at the whole picture.

The same discipline applies to a low sodium after a hot race, or a creatinine that ticks up after a heavy training block. Context can explain a great deal of what shows up on this panel, and understanding that context is genuinely useful. But context is a tool for asking better questions, not a tool for closing the file yourself. When a value stays abnormal after the obvious explanations have been ruled out through rest, hydration, and a sensible retest, the correct next step is the same every time: a conversation with a clinician who can order follow-up testing and interpret it against a person's actual history.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

Case study: Elena and the panel that was almost all in range

Case

Elena is 34, trains for sprint triathlons three mornings a week, and eats with real intention. Her annual metabolic panel came back with fasting glucose at 91 mg/dL and hemoglobin A1c at 5.4%, both comfortably normal, alongside a fasting insulin flagged high-normal by her lab. Drawn two hours after a hot Saturday long run, her sodium read 134 mmol/L, one point under the lab's cutoff. And a creatinine drawn the week after a heavy squat cycle nudged her estimated filtration rate into a mildly low flag. Nothing was dramatically wrong. Nothing was clearly nothing either. She wanted to know which of the three, if any, deserved a real conversation with her doctor.

Run Elena's results through this chapter and two of the three questions resolve into confident, low-stakes explanations, while the third deserves genuine follow-through, not because it is alarming but because it is exactly the kind of pattern this chapter warned a reader not to wave off.

Start with the sodium. A 134 drawn two hours after a hot long run, in someone who reports drinking generously during the event, fits precisely the pattern Baker (2017) and Hew-Butler and colleagues (2017) describe: real, individually variable sweat losses combined with fluid intake that outpaced them in the short window right after finishing. The sensible response is not alarm. It is a retest in a normal, rested, well-hydrated state, days away from any hard training. If the number settles back above 135, the story is solved, and it was never really about her kidneys or her heart. It was about two hours in the heat.

Move to the creatinine and the eGFR flag. Elena trains seriously and had just come off a heavy squat cycle. This is close to the exact pattern Beunders and colleagues (2020) and Groothof and colleagues (2022) describe: real muscle mass plus recent, demanding muscle work, both of which genuinely raise creatinine without any change in how well the kidneys are actually filtering. The intelligent path here, too, is a rested, hydrated retest well clear of a training block, and if any uncertainty remains after that, a cystatin C-based estimate that sidesteps the muscle confounder entirely. There is no reason, on this evidence alone, to suspect a kidney problem.

Now the fasting insulin, and this is the one worth sitting with. Elena's glucose and A1c both look genuinely good, which is real and worth acknowledging. But a fasting insulin flagged high-normal, especially if it turns out to be part of a rising trend across her last few annual panels rather than an isolated blip, is exactly the pattern Tabák and colleagues (2009) traced for years before a glucose or A1c value would ever move. It is not evidence that anything is currently wrong. It is a legitimate prompt to ask a clinician a more specific question than "in range or not": is this number stable, or is it trending, and if it is trending, what would be worth doing about it now, while the intervention options are simplest and the stakes are lowest. That is a very different, and far more useful, question than panicking over a single flagged value, and it is also a very different question than dismissing it because the rest of the page looks clean.

Notice what Elena actually walks away with, because it is the real deliverable of this chapter. Not a diagnosis, which was never available to her from a chart she read on her own couch. Not a verdict on any of the three flagged numbers. What she has instead is a working model: know which timescale or context each marker reflects, ask what happened in the days before the draw, treat a persistent, rested, well-hydrated abnormality as the real signal rather than a single flag, and take the one marker that does not have an easy contextual explanation to the person qualified to interpret a trend. That is not a smaller outcome than a definitive answer would have been. It is a sturdier one, because it does not depend on Elena, or anyone else reading a panel at home, guessing correctly.

The flag tells you a number crossed a line. It never tells you why. On the metabolic panel, why is usually hiding in how a person slept, drank, and trained the day before.

Key Takeaways

  • Fasting glucose is a noisy snapshot of "right now"; HbA1c is a smoothed roughly 8 to 12 week average; fasting insulin and HOMA-IR trend even more slowly, sometimes years ahead of either (Nathan et al., 2008; Khambatta et al., 2017; Tabák et al., 2009).
  • Use eAG = 28.7 × A1c - 46.7 to translate A1c into familiar glucose units, and treat the pre-diabetes band, A1c 5.7 to 6.4%, as a graded call to action rather than a single wall (Karczewski et al., 2026).
  • A rising fasting insulin or HOMA-IR while glucose and A1c still look normal is one of the clearest "in range is not optimal" signals on any panel, because a healthy pancreas can compensate for early insulin resistance for years before glucose moves at all (Matthews et al., 1985; Meigs et al., 2020).
  • Blood sodium is a hydration ratio, not a salt-intake tally; overdrinking beyond thirst, not underdrinking, is the main driver of dangerous exercise-associated low sodium (Hew-Butler et al., 2017).
  • Sweat sodium losses vary up to tenfold between people, so electrolyte needs and post-exercise values are genuinely personal (Baker, 2017).
  • Creatinine reflects muscle and recent exercise as well as kidney function; high muscle mass and hard workouts can inflate it and falsely lower creatinine-based eGFR (Groothof et al., 2022; Beunders et al., 2020; Aponte Becerra & Mansour, 2026).
  • eGFR is estimated from creatinine and declines gradually with normal aging even in healthy kidneys, so the same number can mean different things at different ages (Astley et al., 2025).
  • "In range" is not "optimal," and a genuinely out-of-range or persistently trending value is a sign that warrants medical evaluation, read after rest and normal hydration before drawing conclusions.

Next this course turns to the liver panel, and the muscle confounder returns wearing a different disguise. The enzymes used to read liver health also leak from working muscle, so a hard leg day can light up a liver flag that has nothing to do with the liver at all. Same principle, new organ: read position and context before reading alarm.
