Chapter 6 : The Liver's Alarm Bells, and False Alarms

Leakage enzymes report recent cellular stress, not organ function. Read the pattern, the timing, and the reference range together, before reading the number alone.

The Two Leakage Enzymes

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT)Shuttles an amino group between alanine and pyruvate. Concentrated mainly in the liver, so it is the more trustworthy signal when the question is simply, is this the liver. Half-life in blood roughly 36 hours.
Aspartate aminotransferase (AST)Shuttles an amino group between aspartate and oxaloacetate. Abundant in the liver, but also in skeletal muscle, heart muscle, red blood cells, kidneys, and brain, so a high AST alone is a question, not an answer. Half-life in blood roughly 18 hours.
Both enzymesAre leakage markers, not function tests. They rise because stressed or dying cells spill more of what was already inside them, not because an organ started making more enzyme. "Liver function test" is a misnomer for what they capture; bilirubin, albumin, and the international normalized ratio come closer to a true function report.

The AST-to-ALT Ratio

  • Also called the De Ritis ratio. Carries information the two raw numbers do not, because AST and ALT clear the blood at different speeds.
  • Ratio below roughly 1 (ALT higher): points toward the categories of liver stress common with metabolic risk factors, often a slow, chronic build.
  • Ratio at or above roughly 2 (AST much higher): classically linked to alcohol-related liver injury, and also the exact signature produced by recent hard muscle exertion.
  • The ratio narrows the question. It does not answer it on its own.

The Muscle Confound

  • Pettersson et al. (2008): a single hard weightlifting session in untrained men raised AST, ALT, lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase, and myoglobin, elevated for at least seven days.
  • Bilirubin, gamma-glutamyl transferase, and alkaline phosphatase stayed normal throughout, a fingerprint that favors a muscle source over a liver source.
  • Because ALT is scarcer in muscle, a hard workout pushes AST up more than ALT, inflating the AST-to-ALT ratio for reasons unrelated to the liver.

Pre-Draw Timing Rules

  • The 48 to 72 hours before a draw is the single highest-yield window to ask about.
  • Novelty and intensity matter more than routine, familiar training.
  • Recent hard training pushes the AST-to-ALT ratio up, in the direction that looks more concerning, not less.
  • For a clean baseline read, skip hard or novel training in the 48 to 72 hours before a draw. If that is not possible, record the date, type, and intensity of the last hard session.

Creatine Kinase (CK): The Tie-Breaker

  • Creatine kinase (CK) is concentrated in skeletal and heart muscle, and is considerably more muscle-specific than AST.
  • AST up with CK also up: the muscle explanation gets stronger.
  • AST up with CK unremarkable: the muscle explanation gets weaker, and further liver-specific questions become more warranted.
  • CK is not on a standard liver panel, but it is a useful marker to know exists.

Reference Ranges: Sex and Age

  • True healthy ALT ceilings, from carefully screened populations (Kwo, Cohen, & Lim, 2017): roughly 29 to 33 IU/L for men, 19 to 25 IU/L for women, both lower than many labs' traditional cutoff of 40 IU/L.
  • Older, broader reference populations quietly included people with undiagnosed fatty liver changes and excess visceral fat, pushing the printed ceiling higher than a truly healthy one.
  • Pooling wide age ranges into one interval can make a range too lax for a young, lean, metabolically healthy adult.
  • In range is a statement about a lab's reference population, not a personal verdict of optimal health.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how ALT and AST behave, not a license to diagnose or treat. Jaundice, dark urine, pain in the upper right abdomen, markedly high values, persistent elevation without a training explanation, or an ALT-predominant pattern alongside metabolic risk factors are signs that warrant evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
