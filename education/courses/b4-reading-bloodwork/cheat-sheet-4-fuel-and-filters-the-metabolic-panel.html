Chapter 4 : Fuel and Filters

Three stories, one panel: fuel (glucose and insulin), fluid chemistry (electrolytes), and filters (kidney markers). Read position and context before reading alarm.

Fasting Glucose & HbA1c: Two Timescales

Fasting glucoseSnapshot of right now. Below 100 mg/dL is normal; 100 to 125 mg/dL is impaired fasting glucose; 126 mg/dL or above, confirmed, is the diabetes threshold. Many metabolic-health frameworks prefer the 80s to low 90s. Noisy: sleep, stress, and a hard prior-day workout can nudge it.
Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)Running average of roughly the last 8 to 12 weeks, since glucose sticks to hemoglobin for a red cell's life span. Below 5.7 percent is normal; 5.7 to 6.4 percent is pre-diabetes, graded rather than one wall; 6.5 percent or above is the diabetes line.
eAG formulaEstimated average glucose (mg/dL) = 28.7 × HbA1c − 46.7. Converts an HbA1c percentage into familiar glucose units.
Why they disagreeDifferent timescales, not contradiction. A two-week diet change can move a daily glucose average fast while HbA1c, dragged by the 8 to 12 week red-cell memory, barely responds yet.

The Insulin Signal

  • Mechanism: cells resist insulin's signal, so the pancreas secretes more to force the same glucose into cells. Glucose and HbA1c can stay normal for years while this compensation runs.
  • Homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR): fasting glucose (mg/dL) × fasting insulin (µU/mL), divided by 405.
  • No single universal decision limit; read as trend, not pass or fail.
  • Rising fasting insulin or HOMA-IR while glucose and HbA1c are normal: the flagship "in range is not optimal" case on this panel.

Electrolytes & Hydration

  • Sodium: a hydration ratio, not a salt tally. Normal about 135 to 145 mmol/L.
  • Hyponatremia: overdrinking beyond thirst is the main driver, not underdrinking.
  • Potassium: narrow window, about 3.5 to 5.0 mmol/L; the heart's rhythm depends on it. A meaningfully abnormal result is a prompt for prompt medical attention, not a wait-and-see item.
  • Sweat sodium loss varies up to tenfold between individuals, so electrolyte needs are genuinely personal.

The Filters: Creatinine, BUN & eGFR

CreatinineA muscle-metabolism waste product cleared by the kidneys. Also rises with higher muscle mass and with recent hard exercise, neither of which reflects worse kidney function.
Blood urea nitrogen (BUN)Reflects protein intake and hydration as much as filtration. Most useful alongside creatinine, often as a ratio, rather than read alone.
Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)Calculated, not directly measured, from creatinine, age, and sex. Any confounder that inflates creatinine lowers the apparent eGFR even when true filtration is fine. Declines gradually with normal aging in healthy kidneys.
The confounderHigh muscle mass and a recent hard training block both raise creatinine and lower apparent eGFR without any real drop in filtration. Fix: retest rested and well hydrated; a cystatin C-based estimate sidesteps the muscle effect.

Reading a Flag: The Quick Check

  • Ask what happened in the day or two before the draw: sleep, stress, hydration, and training all move this panel.
  • A value that is context-driven usually settles back on a rested, well-hydrated retest.
  • A value that stays abnormal after that retest, or that keeps trending in one direction across repeated annual panels, is the one that is a sign that warrants medical evaluation.
  • Age matters for eGFR and, at the margins, for glucose and HbA1c targets. The same number is not always the same story at every age.
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how the panel works, not a license to diagnose or treat. A genuinely out-of-range value, or one that stays abnormal after rest and normal hydration, is a sign that warrants evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role, whatever it is.
