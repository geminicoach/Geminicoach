Take the leakage-enzyme model and use it to explain a real result, in plain language, within the bounds of general education.

Name: ____________________________ Date: __________ Est. time: 25 min

The Scenario

Scenario · Dahlia, 29
Consider Dahlia, who gets routine bloodwork done as part of an annual check-in and finds her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) flagged at 91 IU/L, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 48 IU/L. She feels completely well, with no symptoms of any kind, no nausea, no unusual fatigue, no pain, and no change in the color of her urine. Two days before the blood draw she took a hard, unfamiliar strength class for the first time in months, the kind of session that left her sore for days afterward. She has never had a liver problem, drinks rarely, and takes no medications. Seeing the word "liver" attached to a flagged red number, she starts to worry. Dahlia wonders: "Does this mean something is wrong with my liver? Should I be worried?"

Your Tasks

  1. Explain, mechanically, what ALT and AST actually measure, and why a single elevated AST does not by itself mean liver damage.
  2. Work out the AST-to-ALT ratio for Dahlia's result, and explain why that ratio pattern alone cannot tell you whether the source is muscle or liver.
  3. Use the timing of Dahlia's strength class and the 48 to 72 hour window logic from the chapter to explain the most plausible source of her elevated AST, and describe what additional marker could serve as a tie-breaker and why.
  4. Give one practical, general-education suggestion for what Dahlia might reasonably do next, and state clearly where general education ends and clinical care begins.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not diagnosing. A responsible response explains the mechanism, identifies what if anything warrants professional evaluation, and does not diagnose. Diagnosis and treatment belong to a qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Identifies ALT and AST as leakage markers rather than function tests, and explains why a single elevated AST does not by itself indicate liver damage.
  • Correctly works out the AST-to-ALT ratio and explains why the ratio alone cannot distinguish a muscle source from a liver source.
  • Connects the timing of the workout to the 48 to 72 hour window and gives a mechanistically sound muscle-source explanation.
  • Identifies creatine kinase (CK) as a more muscle-specific tie-breaker marker and explains how its result would sharpen the interpretation.
  • Offers a practical, general-education next step and clearly states where general education ends and clinical care begins, without diagnosing.
