Part A · The Seasonal Signal

In two or three sentences each, answer the following about 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D).

  1. Explain why 25(OH)D is better described as a hormone built from sunlight than a vitamin consumed at a table, and name the organ that produces the stable, circulating form that labs actually measure.
  2. List three factors, besides diet, that predictably shift a person's 25(OH)D value across the year or across people, and explain how each one works.
  3. Two draws from the same person, one in March and one in September, both return 26 ng/mL. Explain why this match does not prove the person's vitamin D status is stable, using the idea of a seasonal curve.

Part B · Sufficient by Decision Limit, Optimal by Open Debate

This chapter presented a genuine, unresolved disagreement rather than a single settled answer. Explain it honestly.

  1. Describe, in your own words, the population-level target set by the Institute of Medicine and the higher clinical target set by the Endocrine Society. What question was each group actually trying to answer?
  2. The VITAL trial gave a substantial daily dose of vitamin D3 to thousands of adults who were mostly not deficient to begin with. What did it find about pushing an already-adequate level higher, and why does that matter for someone chasing an "optimal" number rather than a sufficient one?
  3. Why is it more honest to present sufficiency and optimal targets as a genuine debate rather than to simply pick one number and call it settled science?

Part C · How Supplementation Moves the Number

Use the bathtub model from the chapter.

  1. Describe the shape of the dose-response curve seen after vitamin D supplementation begins. Does the level rise in a straight line, or does it behave differently, and why?
  2. Explain why a single large dose of vitamin D produces a temporary spike rather than a permanent elevation, and why steady, moderate dosing tends to produce a more durable result.
  3. Name two groups for whom a general-population target should not be applied without individualized guidance, and explain why each group's target moves.

Part D · The B12 Problem: A Normal Number That Is Not Always Reassuring

  1. A standard serum vitamin B12 (cobalamin) test reports two carrier fractions combined into one number. Name both fractions, and explain which one is actually taken up and used by cells.
  2. List three common situations that can leave a functional B12 shortage hiding behind a technically normal serum result.
  3. Explain, in a full sentence, what "a comfortably normal result lowers the likelihood of deficiency, it does not eliminate it" means in practice for someone reading their own lab report.

Part F · Folate, Macrocytic Anemia, and the CBC Tie-Back

Where education ends
This worksheet builds literacy in reading vitamin D and B12 markers, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. Explain how the seasonal curve, the sufficiency debate, and the confirmation layers work, and treat anything that looks clinical, including persistent fatigue, tingling or numbness, a flagged macrocytic anemia, or a grey-zone result alongside risk factors, as signs that warrant medical evaluation by a qualified clinician. Work within your role: a coach refers, a clinician works within their license, and a self-learner does not self-diagnose.
