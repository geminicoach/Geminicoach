Chapter 1 : The Number Is Not the Verdict

A reference range describes a population, not a target for you. A decision limit is a different kind of line entirely.

How a Reference Range Is Built (Three Steps)

1. Choose a populationA laboratory recruits people it screens as "healthy" for the marker in question, typically a minimum of 120 people per partition (for example, adult men and adult women measured separately) per Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidance.
2. Measure and plotEveryone in that population is measured, and the results are plotted as a distribution, often bell-shaped, sometimes skewed and mathematically adjusted.
3. Trim the tailsBy convention, the lower limit is set at the 2.5th percentile and the upper limit at the 97.5th percentile. The middle 95 percent becomes "the reference range."

Why 1 in 20 Falls Out of Range

  • The bottom and top 2.5 percent of a healthy population, by definition, land outside the range, that is where the boundary was drawn.
  • "Out of range" for a single marker means statistically unusual for that population, not diseased.
  • Roughly 1 in 20 healthy people will be flagged on any single marker, purely by construction.

Cumulative Probability Across a Panel

  • Each additional independent test lowers the odds of a fully clean panel.
  • At 95 percent per test, roughly 60 percent odds of a completely clean sweep across ten tests; the odds keep dropping as more markers are added.
  • A single isolated flag on a large panel is close to the statistically expected outcome, not an alarm.
  • A cluster of flags, a large deviation, or a value drifting the same direction across repeated tests is a different, more informative signal.

Reference Range vs. Decision Limit

  • Reference interval: is this value typical for a healthy population? Boundary set at the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
  • Decision limit: does this value carry enough risk, or enough diagnostic weight, to act on? Boundary set by outcome and diagnostic research, not percentiles.
  • Fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1c (glycated hemoglobin), and LDL cholesterol (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol) cutoffs are decision limits, not reference intervals.
  • A value can sit inside the population range and above the decision limit at the same time, ordinary for the population, yet genuinely worth acting on.

The Core Literacy Point

  • "In range" answers "is this typical for a group?" It is not a promise of "optimal for you."
  • Age, sex, training status, pregnancy, ethnicity, and medications can all make a printed range fit a person poorly.
  • Read a range by age band and life stage where possible, and treat a broad adult range as a starting approximation, not a finish line.
  • "Out of range" is a prompt to look closer and involve a professional. It is not a self-issued diagnosis.

The Four-Question Read

  • 1. Which kind of line is this, reference interval or decision limit?
  • 2. Is it one isolated flag or part of a pattern?
  • 3. Does the range actually fit this person, age, sex, training, pregnancy, ethnicity, medications?
  • 4. What is the trend across repeated tests, not just the single snapshot?
Where education ends   This builds understanding of how reference ranges and decision limits are built and what they actually claim, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. A flag is a prompt for evaluation with a qualified clinician, never a self-issued verdict. Work within your role, whatever it is.
