The Scenario

Scenario · Noemi, 45
Noemi already uses a firming eye serum listing "copper tripeptide-1" on the ingredient label. At a wellness expo, a booth for a different clinic offers "GHK-Cu injections for whole-body regeneration," describing the treatment as "backed by decades of research" on the same molecule in her serum. Noemi is not using either product beyond her existing serum and wants to understand whether the injectable claim deserves the same confidence as her serum before she thinks any further about it.

Your Tasks

  1. Explain GHK-Cu's proposed mechanism at the skin level, including how copper delivery connects to specific copper-dependent enzymes and to collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycan synthesis.
  2. Place the topical claim and the injectable claim separately on the evidence ladder. What does the human evidence actually support for each route?
  3. Evaluate the clinic's "decades of research" framing as applied to the injectable product. What does that research history actually show, and where does it stop short of supporting the injectable claim?
  4. Address copper safety honestly: is unbound copper a genuine concern, and does that concern look the same for a topical serum as for an injectable product? State where general education ends and a dermatologist or clinician's input would be needed.

Where education ends
You are demonstrating understanding and plain-language communication, not recommending either product to Noemi. A responsible response explains the mechanism and the route-specific evidence honestly, and identifies that the injectable product, or any unusual skin reaction, belongs in a conversation with a dermatologist or qualified clinician.
Instructor / self-check rubric
  • Explains the copper-chelation, cofactor-enzyme, and collagen/elastin/GAG synthesis mechanism accurately.
  • Distinguishes topical evidence (human trial-supported) from injectable evidence (animal-only, thin) and places each on the evidence ladder correctly.
  • Explains why "decades of research" overstates the injectable claim, correctly identifying the 1973 finding and later reviews as hypothesis-generating, not clinical proof.
  • Addresses copper safety reasoning without alarmism, noting the route-dependent uncertainty.
  • Does not recommend either product; notes where a dermatologist or clinician consult applies.
