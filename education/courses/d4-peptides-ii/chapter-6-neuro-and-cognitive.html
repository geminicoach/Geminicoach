Peptides II · Chapter 6

Neuro and Cognitive Peptides

Four peptides promise a sharper mind, a calmer nervous system, deeper sleep, and a brain that heals itself, but the evidence behind those four promises is not remotely the same size, and pretending otherwise is where this category goes wrong.

41 min read 7 cited sources

Devon found the first forum thread by accident, looking for something entirely unrelated, and ended up three hours deep in a subculture built around nasal sprays. Not decongestant sprays. Small glass vials of a peptide called semax, described in one post as "a legal nootropic that actually does something," alongside a second peptide called selank, described as "the anxiety compound that doesn't knock you out," a third called DSIP, short for delta sleep-inducing peptide, described as "exactly what the name says," and a fourth, cerebrolysin, described as "what stroke patients get in Eastern Europe, and what a lot of biohackers are now using off-label for brain recovery." Every post had a confident tone. Every claim was stated as settled fact. And every single one of the four compounds, on closer inspection, had almost nothing in common with the other three except that they all, in some loose sense, act on the brain.

This chapter is about untangling exactly that bundle, because "peptides for the brain" is not a coherent category the way "growth hormone secretagogues" or "anti-inflammatory peptides" are coherent categories elsewhere in this course. Semax and selank are short, precisely defined peptide chains developed by Soviet and Russian pharmacology institutes decades apart from each other but by the same research lineage, one built from a fragment of a stress hormone, the other built from a fragment of an immune peptide. Delta sleep-inducing peptide, or DSIP, is a still-older nonapeptide identified in the 1970s whose entire reputation rests on one very specific finding that has never been cleanly replicated at scale. And cerebrolysin is not a single peptide at all. It is a manufactured mixture of peptide fragments and free amino acids extracted from pig brain tissue, used as a prescribed clinical drug in several countries and evaluated in the single largest, most rigorous body of trial evidence of anything else covered in this chapter, evidence that, as you will see, does not say what its use pattern implies it says.

Our thread through this chapter is Devon, a learner in their late twenties who works a demanding, deadline-driven job and, like a lot of people who land in this part of the internet, was drawn in by the word "focus." Devon is not here to be talked into or out of anything, and this course is not going to hand Devon a verdict on any of these four compounds. What Devon needs, and what this chapter is built to give every learner working through it, is the actual biology underneath four very different marketing pitches, stated plainly next to an honest account of how much, or how little, evidence actually backs each pitch up. That is the discipline this whole chapter practices: intended use, named plainly; mechanism, explained as far as it is actually understood; evidence grade, stated without softening. Nothing here is dosing guidance, a stack, or a protocol, and nothing here is a recommendation to use, or not use, any of these four compounds. It is the biochemistry and the honest evidence picture that should sit underneath any decision a person eventually makes about their own brain, made by that person and, where it matters clinically, by a qualified physician.

Four peptides, one loose category

Before looking at any single compound, it helps to see why these four keep getting grouped together at all, because the grouping itself is a source of confusion. Semax and selank share a genuine common ancestry: both were developed at Russian research institutes descended from the same mid-twentieth-century tradition of studying small fragments of larger, better-known hormones and finding that the fragment sometimes retained a narrow slice of biological activity while losing the parent hormone's broader effects. Semax is derived from a fragment of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), the pituitary hormone that in its intact form drives the adrenal glands to produce cortisol. Selank is derived from a fragment of tuftsin, an immune-system peptide with no direct relationship to ACTH at all. Both are short, structurally defined peptide chains, both were developed with a specific clinical indication in mind, and both have a real regulatory history in Russia that this chapter will describe honestly rather than either dismissing it or inflating it.

DSIP belongs to a different, older story. It was isolated from the blood of rabbits in the 1970s during research into what circulating factor might be responsible for inducing a specific stage of sleep, the deep, slow-wave stage sometimes labeled "delta sleep" on an electroencephalogram. That single finding generated real scientific interest for about two decades, produced a modest cluster of small human studies, and then largely faded from mainstream sleep pharmacology without ever becoming an approved medication anywhere. Cerebrolysin is different again, and arguably does not belong in the same sentence as the other three at all except that grey-market marketing has decided it does: it is not a peptide in the singular sense but a manufactured extract, a defined mixture of low-molecular-weight peptide fragments and free amino acids derived from porcine (pig) brain tissue, produced under pharmaceutical manufacturing standards and sold as a prescription drug in Russia, parts of Eastern Europe, China, and other Asian and post-Soviet markets for stroke and dementia care.

What actually unites this chapter, then, is not a shared mechanism, because there is not one. It is a shared marketing pattern: each of these four compounds is presented online as a low-risk, precision tool for a specific mental state, focus for semax, calm for selank, sleep for DSIP, brain repair for cerebrolysin, and each pitch borrows credibility from the fact that a real, named institute or a real clinical-trial literature exists somewhere behind the compound. The honest work of this chapter is separating what that literature actually shows from what the marketing implies it shows, compound by compound, and being precise about which claims rest on a real regulatory approval, which rest on a thin scatter of small trials, and which rest on almost nothing newer than a 1980s abstract.

Think About It

Before reading further, notice that three of these four compounds were developed inside a specific national research tradition, largely published in Russian-language journals, with limited replication by researchers outside that tradition. That fact alone does not make the underlying biology false. But it does mean a learner has to ask a second question beyond "is there a clinical trial," namely "has this finding been independently replicated by a different research group, in a different country, published in a venue most of the world's pharmacologists actually read." Hold that question through the rest of this chapter.

Semax: a hormone fragment marketed as a focus peptide

Semax is a synthetic heptapeptide, a chain of seven amino acids, built from the first few residues of ACTH, specifically the ACTH(4-10) fragment, with a short added tail (Pro-Gly-Pro) intended to slow its breakdown once in circulation. This structural fact matters enormously and is the single most common point of confusion in the communities Devon had been reading. Semax is not synthetic ACTH, and it does not meaningfully activate the receptors that make intact ACTH drive cortisol production. The corticotropic, hormone-releasing part of the ACTH molecule sits in a different region of the full sequence. What semax retains is a small piece of ACTH that, in the intact hormone, appears to contribute to effects on the central nervous system distinct from adrenal stimulation, and it is that narrow slice of activity, not adrenal-axis activity, that the compound is built around and marketed for.

The intended use stated plainly, the one Devon kept encountering online, is semax as a nootropic for focus, taken as a neurocognitive agent intended to sharpen attention and support learning and memory, alongside a second, related claim of neuroprotection, meaning a proposed ability to limit neuronal damage after an insult like ischemic stroke. In Russia, where semax has the longest institutional history, it has been used clinically for exactly this second purpose, as a supportive treatment following ischemic stroke and in some cognitive-impairment contexts, developed and studied primarily out of Russian pharmacological and neurological research institutes over several decades.

Proposed mechanism

The mechanism most often cited for semax's cognitive and neuroprotective claims centers on brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a signaling protein that supports the survival of existing neurons and encourages the growth of new synaptic connections, alongside proposed effects on the brain's dopaminergic system and other monoamine neurotransmitter pathways involved in attention and motivation. According to Shadrina et al. (2010), administration of semax to rats produced measurable, time-dependent shifts in the expression of BDNF and a related neurotrophin, nerve growth factor (NGF), across several brain regions, including an increase in BDNF expression in the frontal cortex roughly ninety minutes after dosing. That is a real, published finding, and it is the closest thing semax has to a defined molecular mechanism: a peptide fragment of a stress hormone that appears able to modulate the expression of neurotrophic signaling factors in specific brain regions on a specific time course, at least in rodent tissue. The proposed effects on dopaminergic and other monoamine systems come from a separate, older thread of Russian pharmacology literature and are considerably less precisely mapped than the BDNF finding, more a general pattern observed across multiple small studies than a single well-characterized receptor mechanism.

Evidence grade

Here the honesty this course insists on has to be applied directly. The BDNF and neurotrophin data behind semax comes almost entirely from rodent studies conducted by a small number of closely linked Russian laboratories, described in reviews such as Umnov, Lin'kova, and Khavinson (2013), which surveys semax alongside other short peptide neuroprotectants developed within the same research tradition. Human clinical experience with semax is real and dates back decades in Russia, primarily in the stroke and cognitive-impairment context, but the controlled trial literature behind that clinical use has not been meaningfully replicated by independent laboratories outside Russia, has rarely appeared in the major Western neurology or psychopharmacology journals, and has not gone through the kind of large, multi-site, double-blind trial process that would be required for regulatory approval in the United States, the European Union, or most of the rest of the world. Semax is not approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration or by European regulators for any indication. For a learner evaluating the "focus" claim specifically, rather than the older stroke-recovery claim, the honest summary is that the proposed BDNF and monoamine mechanisms are biologically plausible and supported by real, if narrow, rodent data, while the specific claim of improved focus and cognitive performance in a healthy adult using semax off-label rests on considerably thinner and less independently verified ground than the marketing tone around it suggests.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A recurring claim in online discussion treats semax as "basically a legal version of ACTH" or, confusingly, as a steroid-adjacent hormone booster. It is neither. Semax is built from a fragment of ACTH specifically chosen because it appears to lack the parent hormone's corticotropic, adrenal-stimulating activity. Whatever central nervous system effects semax has, they are proposed to run through neurotrophic signaling and monoamine pathways, not through the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis that intact ACTH drives.

Selank: an immune peptide's cousin, marketed as calm

Selank is a synthetic heptapeptide as well, but its lineage runs through a completely different part of the body's signaling systems. It is derived from tuftsin, a naturally occurring peptide fragment cleaved from an antibody molecule (immunoglobulin G) that plays a role in activating cells of the immune system, particularly macrophages. Selank takes tuftsin's core sequence and extends it with the same Pro-Gly-Pro stabilizing tail used in semax, a shared design choice from the same research lineage that lengthens the peptide's activity in circulation. The immune-system ancestry is not incidental trivia. It is the reason selank's earliest described activity in the literature includes antiviral and immune-modulating effects alongside its now more heavily marketed anxiolytic (anxiety-reducing) profile, described by Kolomin et al. (2011) in work examining how selank and its breakdown fragments shift the expression of genes for chemokines and cytokines, the signaling molecules immune cells use to coordinate with each other.

The intended use that dominates current marketing, and the one Devon had specifically bookmarked, is selank for anxiety, with the pitch built heavily around a particular claim: that selank calms without sedating, working by way of its effect on the sympathetic nervous system, the branch of the autonomic nervous system responsible for the body's fight-or-flight arousal response, rather than by broadly suppressing central nervous system activity the way a benzodiazepine does. That specific framing, an anxiolytic that shifts autonomic balance rather than blanketing the brain in sedation, is exactly the intended-use claim this course asks to be stated plainly next to its mechanism and its evidence grade, and it deserves that treatment here.

Proposed mechanism

Selank's proposed anxiolytic mechanism is described in the Russian pharmacology literature as multi-part, rather than a single clean receptor story, and being honest about that multiplicity matters more than picking one clean-sounding explanation. Part of the proposed action involves gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), the brain's principal inhibitory neurotransmitter, with selank proposed to enhance GABAergic tone, meaning it may increase the net calming, inhibitory signal GABA circuits provide, through mechanisms distinct from how benzodiazepines directly bind the GABA-A receptor. A second proposed piece involves the balance of the autonomic nervous system itself, with selank described as shifting activity away from sympathetic (arousal, fight-or-flight) dominance and toward a calmer parasympathetic tone, which is the specific mechanistic claim behind the "calms without sedating" pitch. A third proposed piece involves enkephalins, the body's own opioid-family peptides involved in mood and pain regulation, with selank proposed to inhibit the enzymes that normally break enkephalins down, prolonging their natural calming activity rather than introducing an external opioid signal. According to Kolik et al. (2019), selank administration in rats also produced measurable changes in BDNF content in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex under conditions of chronic ethanol exposure, tying selank's mechanism back to the same neurotrophic signaling theme that runs through semax, even though the two peptides come from unrelated parent molecules.

Evidence grade

Selank has a real regulatory history in Russia, where it has been studied through a formal, multi-phase clinical trial process as a selective anxiolytic, described by Sarkisova, Kozlovskiy, and Kozlovskaya (2008) as having "completed the third phase of clinical testing" in that country's regulatory system by the time of their review. That is a genuinely more advanced clinical development pathway than semax's off-label cognitive-enhancement use, and it is worth stating plainly rather than dismissing. What has not happened is independent replication of that Russian trial program by researchers or regulatory bodies outside Russia, publication of the pivotal trial data in venues most Western psychiatrists or pharmacologists routinely read, or evaluation by agencies like the United States Food and Drug Administration or the European Medicines Agency, neither of which has approved selank for any indication. The GABAergic and autonomic-balance mechanisms described above are biologically coherent and consistent with known anxiolytic pathways in general, but the specific evidence tying selank to those mechanisms in humans, rather than in rats, remains considerably thinner than the phrase "completed phase three" implies to a Western reader used to a different regulatory context. Additional animal work, such as Konstantinopolsky, Chernyakova, and Kolik (2022) on selank's effects in a morphine-withdrawal model, continues to extend the mechanistic picture in rodents without resolving the underlying replication gap.

Case

Devon's specific interest was the sympathetic nervous system claim: a forum post had described selank as "turning down the volume on fight-or-flight without turning down the volume on you," and Devon found that framing appealing precisely because it sounded more precise than a generic "it's calming" pitch. Precision in language is not the same as precision in evidence. The autonomic-balance mechanism is a real, specific, testable claim, which is exactly why it is worth separating from the strength of the human data actually supporting it in the case of selank.

DSIP: the sleep peptide that never quite delivered

Delta sleep-inducing peptide, or DSIP, has the oldest and, in an important sense, the most singular origin story of the four compounds in this chapter. It was isolated in the early 1970s from the cerebral venous blood of rabbits that had been electrically stimulated into a sleep-like state, on the hypothesis that some circulating factor in that blood was responsible for inducing slow-wave, or "delta," sleep, the deepest, most physically restorative stage of the human sleep cycle. The peptide identified, a short nine-amino-acid chain, was named for the specific brainwave pattern researchers hoped it would reliably produce. That naming choice has driven a great deal of the confusion around DSIP ever since, because a compound named after its proposed effect tends to get treated as though the name were an established, guaranteed property rather than a hypothesis the subsequent decades of research only partially supported.

The intended use stated plainly is sleep regulation, specifically the promotion of deep, slow-wave sleep and, in some of the older literature, a broader claimed role in stress adaptation and normalizing disrupted circadian and physiological rhythms. According to Schneider-Helmert and Schoenenberger (1983), a set of controlled human studies using intravenous DSIP in insomniac and healthy volunteer populations found real effects on sleep architecture, including in some cases a described "complete normalization of disturbed sleep" after repeated dosing in people with insomnia, alongside reported effects on daytime alertness, stress tolerance, and coping ability that the authors themselves described as extending well beyond a simple sleep-inducing action, into what they called modulation of psychological functioning more broadly.

Proposed mechanism

Unlike semax and selank, DSIP does not have a well-mapped receptor mechanism even in the proposed sense, and this chapter should not pretend otherwise. Early research explored possible interactions with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal stress axis and effects on autonomic balance in cardiac tissue, including work by Ul'ianinskii et al. (1990) describing DSIP-related peptide effects on the balance between vagal (parasympathetic) and sympathetic influence on heart rhythm in animal models, an autonomic theme that echoes, without being identical to, the mechanism proposed for selank. Malyshenko and Eliseev (1993) placed DSIP within a broader review of how neuropeptides and adrenal-cortex hormones interact to regulate central nervous system function under ordinary living conditions, treating it as one signaling factor among several rather than a peptide with a single, clean site of action. No specific DSIP receptor has ever been definitively identified and characterized in the way the androgen receptor or the GABA-A receptor have been, and much of the mechanistic language used to describe DSIP remains descriptive of observed effects rather than explanatory of a molecular pathway.

Evidence grade

DSIP deserves the most cautious evidence grade of any compound in this chapter, and that caution should be stated without hedging. The human studies behind it are old, several of them dating to the early-to-mid 1980s, conducted in small samples using intravenous administration under research conditions very different from how the peptide circulates in current grey-market marketing. The findings, while genuinely published and genuinely showing measurable effects on sleep architecture in some cohorts, were never followed by the kind of large-scale, modern, double-blind replication that would let a learner treat "DSIP reliably induces deep sleep" as an established fact rather than a promising but unconfirmed lead from four decades ago. DSIP has never been approved as a sleep medication by any major regulatory body, has largely fallen out of active pharmacological research since its initial wave of interest in the 1970s and 1980s, and the honest summary for a learner encountering DSIP online today is that its evidence base is old, thin, and not meaningfully extended by anything resembling the newer trial infrastructure that surrounds cerebrolysin, the next compound in this chapter, despite DSIP's name promising something far more specific and reliable than the underlying science can currently back up.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A peptide named for the exact brainwave state it was hypothesized to produce sounds, by construction, like a guarantee. DSIP's name describes a 1970s research hypothesis, not a confirmed pharmacological property. The controlled human evidence that exists is real but decades old, drawn from small samples using a delivery route unlike what current grey-market marketing describes, and it has not been meaningfully replicated since. Evaluate the name and the evidence separately.

Cerebrolysin: a mixture from pig brain, and the largest trial base in this chapter

Cerebrolysin is the outlier of this chapter in every structural sense, and understanding why matters as much as understanding what it does. It is not a single defined peptide sequence the way semax, selank, and DSIP are. It is a manufactured biological drug product, a standardized mixture of low-molecular-weight peptide fragments and free amino acids enzymatically derived from porcine (pig) brain tissue, produced under pharmaceutical manufacturing controls by a specific commercial manufacturer and sold as a prescription-only clinical drug rather than as a grey-market research peptide in the countries where it is approved. According to Ziganshina et al. (2023), cerebrolysin is "widely used in the treatment of acute ischaemic stroke in Russia, Eastern Europe, China, and other Asian and post-Soviet countries," a clinical footprint considerably larger and more institutionally embedded than any of the other three compounds in this chapter.

The intended use stated plainly is neuroprotection and recovery support, specifically in acute ischemic stroke and in dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, where it is used with the goal of limiting neuronal damage after an ischemic event and supporting cognitive function in the setting of neurodegenerative decline. This is a squarely clinical use case, administered by intravenous or intramuscular injection in a medical setting under a prescribing clinician, not a self-directed wellness or nootropic use, and that distinction is worth holding onto through the rest of this section.

Proposed mechanism

Because cerebrolysin is a mixture rather than a single molecule, its proposed mechanism is necessarily broader and less precise than a single-receptor story. The peptide fragments it contains are proposed to mimic some of the activity of naturally occurring neurotrophic factors, including BDNF-like and nerve-growth-factor-like signaling, supporting neuron survival and limiting the cascade of cell death that follows an ischemic injury, alongside proposed anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic (cell-death-limiting) effects in damaged brain tissue. This is the same general neurotrophic-support theme that runs through semax's proposed mechanism, which is part of why cerebrolysin, semax, and even DSIP get grouped together in reviews like Umnov, Lin'kova, and Khavinson (2013), which explicitly compares "polypeptide complexes" like cerebrolysin against "short peptides" like semax as two different structural strategies aimed at a broadly similar neuroprotective goal.

Evidence grade: larger, and more mixed, than the marketing implies

Cerebrolysin is where this chapter's honesty standard is tested hardest, because unlike the other three compounds, cerebrolysin has actually been through the kind of rigorous, independent, systematic evaluation this course treats as the gold standard, and the result of that evaluation is considerably less favorable than its continued widespread clinical use in several countries would suggest. According to Ziganshina et al. (2023), an update of a Cochrane systematic review first published in 2010, seven randomized controlled trials covering 1,773 participants were assessed for cerebrolysin's effect on acute ischemic stroke outcomes, and the review found moderate-certainty evidence that cerebrolysin "probably results in little to no difference" in preventing all-cause death compared to placebo, moderate-certainty evidence of little to no difference in the total number of people experiencing serious adverse events overall, but also moderate-certainty evidence of an increase in non-fatal serious adverse events among people receiving cerebrolysin, an increase the review found to be more pronounced at higher cumulative dosing schedules. None of the trials included in that review reported on poor functional outcome, meaning death or long-term dependence, a gap the reviewers themselves flagged as a significant limitation of the existing trial record.

This is a genuinely important, and genuinely uncomfortable, evidence picture to sit with. Cerebrolysin is not a thinly studied grey-market curiosity the way DSIP is. It has a real trial base, real regulatory approval in multiple countries, and a real place in clinical stroke and dementia care in parts of the world. And the most rigorous, independent synthesis of that trial base, run by an international group under Cochrane's methodology rather than by the manufacturer, does not find clear evidence that it improves the outcomes it is used for, while flagging a signal of increased non-fatal serious adverse events at higher doses. The honest evidence grade for cerebrolysin, then, is neither "unproven" in the thin sense that applies to DSIP nor "clearly effective" in the sense its continued clinical use in several health systems might imply to an outside observer. It is "extensively studied, and the most rigorous synthesis of that evidence does not support the benefit its use pattern assumes," which is a meaningfully different, and more useful, statement than either extreme.

Think About It

Cerebrolysin is derived from pig brain tissue and manufactured under pharmaceutical controls specifically to standardize its peptide content batch to batch. Before assuming that a rigorous manufacturing process settles every safety question, consider what the Cochrane review actually found: a real, measurable signal of increased non-fatal serious adverse events at higher cumulative doses, inside a manufactured, regulated product with decades of clinical use behind it. Rigor in manufacturing and rigor in efficacy evidence are two separate questions.

Reading the evidence grade honestly, side by side

Laid next to each other, the four evidence pictures in this chapter form a genuine spectrum rather than a uniform "peptides for the brain" category, and seeing that spectrum clearly is the actual point of this chapter. Semax sits on real, but narrow and largely rodent-derived, mechanistic data, layered under decades of Russian clinical use for stroke recovery that has not been independently replicated in Western trial literature at the scale its cognitive-enhancement marketing implies. Selank sits on a somewhat more advanced regulatory pathway within Russia, having reportedly completed a formal multi-phase trial process there as an anxiolytic, while facing the identical replication gap outside that regulatory system, and its specific sympathetic-nervous-system mechanism remains considerably better developed as a hypothesis than as a confirmed human finding. DSIP sits on the thinnest and oldest evidence of the four, real controlled human studies from the 1980s that were never meaningfully extended or replicated at modern scale, wearing a name that implies far more certainty than the underlying science supports. Cerebrolysin sits, counterintuitively, on the largest and most rigorously reviewed evidence base of the four, and that rigorous review does not find the benefit its widespread clinical use would suggest, while flagging a real safety signal at higher doses.

None of these four grades are static facts pulled from nowhere. Each one reflects a genuinely different stage of the evidence-generation process: basic mechanistic biology, animal pharmacology, small early human studies, formal national regulatory trials, and independent international systematic review are five different rungs on a ladder, and a compound's marketing pitch almost never specifies which rung it is actually standing on. A learner's job, the actual skill this chapter is teaching, is to ask that question directly of any claim about any of these four compounds, or any compound like them: which rung is this specific claim standing on, who generated the evidence, has it been independently replicated, and does the trial design used actually match the outcome being claimed. That question does more real protective work than any specific fact about BDNF, GABA, or autonomic balance covered above, because it is the question that will still be useful the next time a new peptide with a confident-sounding pitch and an unfamiliar evidence base shows up in the same online spaces Devon was reading.

It is also worth being explicit about what remains genuinely unknown across all four compounds, beyond the specific efficacy questions already covered. Long-term safety data in healthy people using any of these four peptides outside a supervised clinical or research setting is limited or absent; interaction effects with other medications, including psychiatric medications a learner might already be taking, have not been systematically studied for semax, selank, or DSIP; and grey-market sourcing of any of these compounds outside an approved clinical supply chain introduces the same contamination, purity, and mislabeling risks this course covers in its dedicated chapter on the grey market, risks that exist entirely independent of whatever a compound's proposed mechanism happens to be.

Here Is Where People Get This Wrong

A common assumption treats "there is a real clinical trial literature" as a single yes-or-no fact about a compound, present or absent. It is not. Semax, selank, DSIP, and cerebrolysin all have some form of published trial or clinical-use history, and yet they sit at four meaningfully different points on the evidence ladder once independence, replication, trial design, and outcome reporting are actually examined. Treat "there is a trial" and "the trial evidence is strong, independent, and outcome-relevant" as two entirely separate claims that both need checking.

Case study: Devon and the stack that promised three different brains

Case

Devon is 28, works a high-pressure job in project management, and does not have a diagnosed anxiety or sleep disorder, though Devon describes ordinary work stress and occasional trouble winding down at night. After the forum deep-dive that opened this chapter, Devon had assembled a mental shopping list: semax for daytime focus, selank for the anxious afternoon crash before a big deadline, and DSIP for the nights sleep would not come easily. Cerebrolysin, described in the same threads as something people were self-administering off-label for "cognitive enhancement" despite its clinical, injectable, prescription-only status in the countries where it is actually approved, gave Devon pause, and that instinct turned out to be the right one to bring into this course.

Running Devon's list back through this chapter resolves it piece by piece, and the pattern of resolution matters more than any single verdict. The semax piece rests on real rodent neurotrophin data and a long Russian clinical history for stroke recovery, but the specific "sharper focus in a healthy adult" claim Devon found most appealing is exactly the part of the semax story with the least independent human evidence behind it, borrowing credibility from the compound's older, more established stroke-related use without actually being the same claim.

The selank piece rests on a more advanced formal trial history within Russia and a coherent, multi-part proposed mechanism involving GABAergic tone, autonomic balance, and enkephalin signaling, but "calms without sedating, by way of its effect on the sympathetic nervous system" is a mechanistic hypothesis with real biological plausibility, not a settled finding independently confirmed outside the research tradition that produced it. Devon's attraction to the precision of that pitch was understandable and even a reasonable instinct, precise claims are more falsifiable and more useful than vague ones, but precision in a claim's wording says nothing about how well-supported the claim actually is.

The DSIP piece is where this chapter asks Devon to be most skeptical. A name that describes the exact desired outcome, paired with decades-old, small, unreplicated human data and no defined receptor mechanism, is close to the textbook shape of a compound whose reputation has outrun its evidence. And the cerebrolysin instinct Devon already had, hesitation in the face of a clinical, prescription-only, injectable drug being described casually as a self-administered cognitive enhancer, was exactly right, reinforced by the fact that the most rigorous evidence available on cerebrolysin does not clearly support the benefit its off-label enthusiasts assume it delivers, while flagging a real signal of harm at higher doses.

What Devon actually needed from this chapter was not a verdict on any of the four compounds. It was the discipline to hold "this has an intended use," "this has a proposed mechanism," and "this has this specific amount and quality of evidence" as three separate, independently checkable claims about any compound Devon encounters, in this chapter or in the next unfamiliar peptide that shows up in the same forum threads later. That discipline, not a yes-or-no answer about semax, selank, DSIP, or cerebrolysin, is the actual skill this chapter was built to transfer.

Where this chapter stops: understanding is not a prescription for your own brain

Everything in this chapter has been descriptive: what semax, selank, DSIP, and cerebrolysin are structurally, what they are marketed and used for, what their proposed mechanisms are, and how strong or weak the evidence actually is behind each specific claim. None of it is dosing guidance, none of it is a stack or protocol recommendation, and none of it is an endorsement of using, or not using, any of these four compounds. That boundary matters more in a chapter about the brain than almost anywhere else in this course, because the tissue in question is the one doing the deciding, and claims about cognition, mood, sleep, and neuroprotection carry a specific kind of persuasive pull that claims about, say, tendon repair generally do not.

It also matters because three of the four compounds in this chapter are not approved medications in most of the world, sit outside any regulated clinical supply chain in the markets where they circulate as research chemicals, and carry the same sourcing, purity, and mislabeling risks this course addresses directly in its dedicated grey-market chapter, risks that exist regardless of how compelling a given mechanism sounds on paper. And even where a compound in this chapter is a genuine, regulated clinical drug, as cerebrolysin is in several countries, its own most rigorous evidence review does not straightforwardly support the benefit its use pattern assumes, which is precisely the kind of nuance a learner needs a qualified clinician, not a forum thread, to walk through in the context of an individual person's actual health history, current medications, and diagnosis.

Where education ends

This course builds understanding, not a license to diagnose or treat, in yourself or anyone else. When a situation calls for diagnosis or treatment, that belongs to a qualified clinician.

For Devon, and for any learner working through this chapter, that boundary is not a disappointing place for the chapter to end. It is the most useful thing this chapter can hand off. Anxiety, attention difficulty, and disrupted sleep are all real, sometimes serious, clinical concerns that deserve an actual diagnostic workup by a qualified clinician when they are significant or persistent, not a self-selected peptide chosen from a forum thread based on which mechanism sounded most precise. Understanding what semax, selank, DSIP, and cerebrolysin actually are, and how much or how little evidence stands behind each one, gives a learner the vocabulary to ask a clinician better questions and to recognize an overconfident claim when one shows up online. It does not, and was never meant to, replace the judgment that only a clinician looking at a specific person's actual history can provide.

Key Takeaways

  • Semax, selank, DSIP, and cerebrolysin are not a single coherent mechanistic category. They share a marketing pattern, precision tools for specific mental states, more than they share biology, and each deserves its own evidence assessment.
  • Semax is a short peptide fragment of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), intended as a nootropic for focus and neuroprotection, proposed to act through brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and dopaminergic and other monoamine pathways, with real rodent mechanistic data but thin, largely unreplicated Western human evidence for its off-label cognitive-enhancement use.
  • Selank is a short peptide fragment of tuftsin, an immune-system peptide, intended for anxiety by way of a proposed effect on the balance of the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system, alongside gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic and enkephalin-related mechanisms. It has a more advanced Russian regulatory trial history than semax, but the same independent-replication gap.
  • Delta sleep-inducing peptide (DSIP) is intended for sleep regulation. Its name describes a 1970s research hypothesis about a specific sleep stage, not a confirmed, well-mapped receptor mechanism, and its human evidence is old, small, and has not been meaningfully replicated at modern scale.
  • Cerebrolysin is a manufactured mixture of peptide fragments and amino acids from porcine (pig) brain tissue, used clinically in several countries for stroke and dementia neuroprotection and recovery. It carries the largest and most rigorously reviewed human trial base of the four, and a Cochrane systematic review finds little to no benefit on survival, alongside a signal of increased non-fatal serious adverse events at higher doses.
  • Evaluating any claim about these compounds means separating three distinct questions: what is the intended use, what is the proposed mechanism, and what specific tier of evidence, animal, small human, national regulatory trial, or independent systematic review, actually backs the claim. None of this chapter's content is dosing guidance or a recommendation to use any compound discussed.
Looking Ahead

This chapter closed on a genuine tension: the compound with the largest, most rigorous evidence base did not deliver the clearest good news. Chapter 7 turns to a different family entirely, the melanocortins, PT-141 (bremelanotide) and melanotan II, where the marketed benefit is real and receptor-confirmed, but the risk picture, blood pressure effects, pigmentation changes, and a genuine melanoma concern, is exactly as serious as this chapter's honesty standard requires naming plainly.
