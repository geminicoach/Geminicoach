Working Around Common Injuries

Not every ache is a stop sign, and not every stop sign is optional. Learn to tell the difference, and know exactly where your job ends.

~15 min readInteractive: Train around it, or refer out?Not medical advice

Sooner or later, almost every client you train will show up with something hurting. Most of the time it is nothing more than the honest cost of hard work, muscle that is rebuilding itself and will settle in a day or two. Sometimes it is a genuine injury that needs to be worked around, and occasionally it is something that has no business being managed in a gym at all. Reading the difference between those three situations, and knowing what to do with each one, is one of the most valuable and most dangerous skills a coach can develop. Valuable, because a trainer who panics at every twinge cannot build anyone. Dangerous, because a trainer who explains away every red flag can turn a manageable problem into a serious one.

Before anything else

This chapter teaches pattern recognition for a personal trainer, not diagnosis or treatment. A personal trainer is not a physician and not a physical therapist, and nothing here changes that. The goal is to help you make sensible, conservative decisions about training load, and to help you recognize, quickly and without hesitation, when a client needs to see someone qualified to diagnose and treat them instead of you.

Soreness, injury, and the ambiguous middle

Start by separating three things that clients tend to lump together under the single word "pain."

Normal training soreness

Delayed onset muscle soreness, usually shortened to DOMS, is the dull, generalized ache that shows up eight to twenty four hours after a session, often peaks around the second day, and then fades on its own over the following two or three days. It is most common after a new exercise, a jump in volume, or extra emphasis on the lengthening phase of a lift, and it tends to feel like stiffness and tenderness spread across a whole muscle belly rather than a single point. A client with DOMS can usually still move the joint through a full range, just with some discomfort, and the feeling is symmetrical with whatever they trained, both hamstrings after a heavy deadlift day, both sets of calves after a week of sprint work. DOMS is not a sign that something is wrong. It is a sign that a tissue was asked to do more than it is used to, and it is adapting.

Acute injury

An acute injury looks and feels different, and usually announces itself. It is typically sudden rather than gradual, often tied to one identifiable moment, a rep, a step, a landing, and it is frequently sharp rather than dull. It may come with visible or palpable swelling, bruising that develops over the following day, an audible pop or tearing sensation at the time it happened, or an immediate and obvious loss of function, a knee that will not bear weight normally, a shoulder that will not lift past a certain point, a back that locks up and will not let the person stand up straight. Acute injury is localized. A client can usually put one finger on the spot, unlike the diffuse ache of DOMS.

The ambiguous nagging pain

The hardest category to read is the one in between, a nagging ache or twinge that does not fit either pattern cleanly. It might have crept in gradually over several sessions rather than appearing all at once, it might come and go depending on the exercise or the day, and it might sit right at a joint rather than in the middle of a muscle belly. This is the pain that most tests a coach's judgment, because it is not obviously an emergency and it is not obviously nothing either. Most of this chapter is about how to manage that ambiguous middle safely, and about knowing when even the ambiguous cases need to stop being your call to make.

A simple pain-monitoring idea

Once you have ruled out an acute injury, you still need a working rule for the ambiguous middle, because "does it hurt" is too blunt a question to build a session around. A useful, simple standard that many coaches use informally is this: mild, localized discomfort that does not worsen during the session and settles down within a day or so can often be trained around with sensible adjustments. Sharp pain, pain that worsens as you go, or pain that sits inside a joint rather than a muscle is a stop signal.

In practice that means asking a client a few concrete questions before and during a set. Where exactly is it, one point or a general area? Is it the same, better, or worse than the last set? Does it stay put or does it travel down a limb? A client who says the ache is a general two out of ten in the muscle belly and stays a two out of ten for three sets is a very different case from a client who starts at a two and is at a six by the third rep, or who says it now shoots down toward the knee. The first case is a candidate for continued, adjusted training. The second is not, and no amount of technique cueing changes that.

This is a monitoring habit, not a clearance. It helps you decide, set by set, whether to keep going with an exercise, adjust it, or stop it. It does not tell you what is structurally wrong, and it is not a substitute for a professional evaluation when something does not add up.

Load management: adjust the dial, do not just flip the switch

The most common mistake with a nagging ache is treating training as all-or-nothing, either grind through at full weight or scrap the session entirely. Almost always there is a middle path, because pain in a movement is usually sensitive to three separate dials, and you rarely need to turn all three down at once.

  • Volume. Fewer sets or fewer total repetitions for the affected pattern, while keeping everything else in the program intact.
  • Intensity. Lighter load relative to what the client normally handles, which reduces tissue stress while keeping the movement pattern and the training habit alive.
  • Range of motion. A shorter, less provocative range, stopping a squat above the point where a knee complains, or hinging to a higher position rather than a full range deadlift.

Adjusting one or two of these dials is usually enough to keep a client training productively around a minor issue. Dropping all three at once, or abandoning the exercise pattern altogether, should be reserved for cases where even a reduced version still reproduces the warning signs described above.

Substitution logic: same pattern, different tool

When an exercise itself is the problem, rather than just the load, the fix is usually not to skip the movement pattern, it is to find a pain-free variation that still trains it. Losing an entire pattern, all pressing, all hinging, all squatting, for weeks at a time is rarely necessary and often costs a client more than the original irritation would have. A few reliable swaps:

  • Back squat, painful, to box squat or split squat. A box squat shortens and controls the range and changes the loading angle at the hip and knee, while a split squat reduces the load on any single joint and lets a client work around a side that is bothering them while still loading the front of the leg.
  • Barbell bench press, painful shoulder, to a neutral-grip dumbbell press. The neutral grip changes the position of the humerus in the socket, often reducing the impingement-type discomfort a straight bar can create, while still training a horizontal pressing pattern.
  • Conventional deadlift, painful low back, to a trap bar deadlift or a lighter-range hip hinge. A trap bar shifts the load closer to the body's center and reduces shear at the spine, and a hip hinge trained through a shorter, higher range still builds the pattern without asking for full lumbar flexion under load.

The principle behind every one of these swaps is the same: keep the movement pattern the client's program is built around, squat, hinge, press, pull, and change the specific tool, angle, or range until you find a version the client can perform without reproducing the warning signs. That is training around an issue. It is not treating it, and it is not a diagnosis of what is actually wrong.

Three common regions, briefly

Low back

A dull, generalized low-back ache after deadlifts or a new hinge pattern is common and usually responds well to trimming range, reducing load, and emphasizing hip hinge mechanics over spinal flexion. Favor trap bar or rack-pull style work over a full conventional pull, keep the load closer to the body, and watch for the pain staying local to the low back rather than reaching into a leg.

Shoulder

Overhead pressing and heavy bench work are the usual triggers for a cranky shoulder. Neutral-grip pressing, a lower incline angle, or trimming the top of an overhead range where impingement-type pinching tends to occur are all reasonable first adjustments. Rows and pulling work are often untouched and can stay in the program.

Knee

Deep knee flexion under load, the bottom of a squat or a lunge, is the most common trigger. Reducing depth, moving to a box squat, or shifting volume toward hip-dominant work such as hip thrusts or Romanian deadlifts while the knee settles are all sensible, conservative adjustments that keep a lower-body session productive.

In every one of these three regions, the same boundary applies. Working around mild, stable, local discomfort is within a coach's scope. Anything that matches the red flags below is not.

When to refer out, no exceptions

A personal trainer is not a diagnostician and not a therapist, and the following symptoms are outside a coach's scope regardless of how mild the client insists they are. Refer the client to a physician or a physical therapist, and do not continue loading the affected area in the meantime, if you see or hear about any of these:

  • Numbness or tingling, anywhere, especially in a hand or foot.
  • Radiating pain that travels down an arm or leg rather than staying local to the muscle or joint.
  • A joint that gives way, locks, or catches, a knee that buckles, a shoulder that clunks and sticks.
  • Night pain, an ache that wakes the client up or is worse lying down than moving around.
  • Pain following an identifiable trauma, a fall, a collision, a specific rep where something happened.
  • Anything that is not improving over one to two weeks of sensible load management, or that is getting worse despite it.

None of these require you to guess what is wrong. They only require you to recognize that it is time to hand the client to someone qualified to find out.

Why this pays off on the floor

Clients trust a coach more, not less, when that coach says "this is outside what I should be managing, let's get it looked at." Referring out at the right moment protects the client's health, protects your working relationship with them, and protects you professionally. It is not an admission of failure, it is the job done correctly.

The takeaway

Most of what walks through your door is ordinary soreness, and most of the rest is manageable discomfort that responds to sensible adjustments in volume, intensity, range, or exercise choice. Your job in that space is real, useful work, keeping a client training productively while a tissue settles. But that job has a firm edge. Numbness, radiating pain, a joint that gives way or locks, night pain, pain after a trauma, or anything that simply is not getting better belongs with a physician or a physical therapist, not with you. Knowing that edge, and respecting it every time, is what makes it safe for you to do everything else this course teaches.
