Programming Basics

Anatomy tells you what a muscle does. Programming is the separate skill of deciding how much, how heavy, how often, and in what order, so that a client actually changes.

~17 min readInteractive: Match the goal to its setupPractical, not academic

Everything in Part 1 was about reading the body. This chapter is about writing for it. A program is nothing more than a set of decisions repeated over time: what exercise, how heavy, how many reps, how many sets, how long to rest, how fast to move, how often to repeat it, and in what order. Get those decisions roughly right and pointed at a clear goal, and almost any reasonable exercise selection will produce results. Get them wrong, and the fanciest exercise selection in the world will not.

The one principle everything else sits on top of

If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember this: the body only adapts to a demand it does not already meet. Lift the same weight for the same reps forever, and the body has no reason to change, it is already capable of that task. This is progressive overload, the gradual, planned increase in the demand you place on the body so it keeps having a reason to adapt.

Overload does not only mean adding weight to the bar. A coach has several honest ways to increase demand over time, and rotating through them is how you keep progressing a client for years instead of months:

  • More load. The same exercise, reps, and sets, with a heavier weight.
  • More reps or sets at the same load. The classic way to build work capacity before adding weight.
  • Less rest between sets. The same work, done in less time, which raises the difficulty without touching the weight.
  • Better range of motion or technique. A deeper squat or a more controlled tempo is a harder version of the same lift, even at an identical load.
  • More frequency. Training a pattern or muscle group an extra day a week, so total weekly work rises.
  • A harder variation. Moving from a supported exercise to a free one, or from a machine to a barbell, once the simpler version stops being a challenge.

Every method below, every rep range, every periodization scheme, is just a structured way of applying progressive overload without breaking the client down faster than they can recover. That is the whole game.

Start with the goal, because it decides everything else

A coach who sets sets and reps before deciding a goal is guessing. The goal comes first, and it tells you where to sit on every one of the variables you will read about next. Four goals cover almost every client you will see.

Hypertrophy: building muscle size

Hypertrophy training uses moderate loads, roughly 6 to 15 repetitions per set, and leans on a relatively high total training volume, meaning more total sets across the week for a muscle group. The mechanism is mostly about accumulating enough hard sets close to fatigue, not about lifting the heaviest possible weight. Training is often taken close to failure, the point where another clean repetition is not possible, but not always to failure on every set, since that adds fatigue without much extra benefit. Rest periods are moderate, commonly one to two minutes, enough to keep density in the session without leaving big reserves of strength unused between sets.

Strength: building maximum force output

Strength training uses heavier loads, typically 1 to 5 repetitions per set, with longer rest, often three to five minutes, so the nervous system can recover enough to move a genuinely heavy weight again. Total volume is lower than a hypertrophy phase, because heavy singles, doubles, and triples are far more taxing per repetition. A meaningful part of a strength phase is simply skill practice, the squat, the deadlift, and the bench press are technical lifts, and lifting near-maximal loads well is itself a trainable skill that needs repetition under load, not just muscle growth.

Muscular endurance and general fitness

Here the load drops and the reps climb, often 15 to 25 or more repetitions per set with lighter weight, shorter rest, and an emphasis on repeatable, sustainable effort rather than grinding toward failure. This range suits clients whose goals are day-to-day stamina, general health markers, or simply staying active and consistent, and it is also a sensible choice early in a new client's training history while they are still learning to move well under fatigue.

Mobility and movement quality

This goal is not primarily about a rep range at all, it is about training a joint through a fuller, controlled range of motion under some load, rather than chasing pure flexibility with no load behind it. A client working on mobility benefits more from showing up consistently and doing loaded range work, think a slow, controlled deep squat or a controlled overhead reach, than from any single intense session. For this goal, consistency over time matters more than intensity in any one workout.

A field shortcut for choosing a rep range

When a new client cannot articulate a specific goal, ask what they actually want changed in daily life. "I want to look and feel more built" points toward hypertrophy ranges. "I want to be genuinely stronger for my sport or my job" points toward strength ranges. "I just want to move well and not get hurt" points toward mobility and lighter general fitness work. The rep range follows from the answer, you rarely need to guess.

The acute variables: the dials a coach actually turns

Underneath every goal sits the same short list of variables. Programming, at its core, is deciding where each of these sits for a given client on a given day.

  • Intensity, or load. How heavy the weight is, usually described as a percentage of the heaviest weight the person could lift once, called a one repetition maximum, or described in plain terms as light, moderate, or heavy.
  • Volume. The combination of sets and reps, often summarized as total sets per muscle group per week. Volume is the main driver of size gains and a major driver of fatigue, so it needs to rise and fall deliberately, not by accident.
  • Rest. How long between sets. Short rest, under a minute, builds work capacity and conditioning. Long rest, three to five minutes, protects strength and power on the next set.
  • Tempo. The speed of each phase of a lift, commonly described as the lowering phase, any pause, and the lifting phase. Slowing a lift down increases time under tension and control, which is useful for hypertrophy, technique correction, and mobility work.
  • Frequency. How often a muscle group or movement pattern is trained per week. More frequency spreads the same weekly volume across more, smaller sessions, which many clients recover from and adhere to better than fewer, huge sessions.
  • Exercise selection and order. Which movements are chosen, and the sequence they are performed in within a session. This is the subject of the next section, and it is where most of a coach's day-to-day judgment actually lives.

Notice that none of these variables works alone. Raise volume and you usually need to lower intensity or add rest to compensate. Shorten rest and intensity usually has to drop. A program is a balance of these dials, not a single number to chase.

Main lifts and accessories: two different jobs in the same session

Every well-built session has a structure, not just a list of exercises. Main lifts are the big, compound, multi-joint patterns, the squat, the deadlift and its variations, the bench or overhead press, and the row or pull-up family. They train the most muscle mass at once, allow the heaviest loading, and are placed first in a session while the client is freshest and technique matters most.

Accessories come after the main lift. These are smaller, often single-joint or corrective movements, a biceps curl, a calf raise, a face pull, a glute bridge, chosen to target a specific muscle the main lift did not fully load, to correct an imbalance, or to add extra volume to a lagging area without the fatigue cost of another heavy compound lift. Accessories are typically done at a lighter load and a higher rep range than the main lift that precedes them, regardless of the session's overall goal.

The practical rule is simple: fatigue is a limited resource in every session, so spend the freshest part of it on the movement that gives the most return, the main lift, and spend the remaining budget on the accessories that round out the work.

Periodization, kept usable

Periodization is simply the plan for how a program changes over weeks and months, so that overload keeps happening without burning the client out. You do not need the academic version to coach well. Three practical ideas cover almost every situation a personal trainer meets.

Linear progression

The simplest model: keep the exercises and structure roughly the same, and add a small, steady amount of weight, reps, or sets each week. This works extremely well for beginners, whose bodies adapt quickly to almost any reasonable stimulus, and it is the natural starting point for most new clients.

Undulating, or day to day, variation

Instead of one steady climb, the load and rep range shift from session to session within the same week, for example a heavier, lower rep day early in the week and a lighter, higher rep day later in the week for the same lift. This suits intermediate clients whose progress on a purely linear plan has stalled, since it varies the stimulus enough to keep adapting while still hitting the same movement patterns often.

Blocks and the deload

A block is simply a chunk of weeks, commonly four to six, built around a shared emphasis, for example a block focused on building work capacity, followed by a block focused on heavier strength work. Stringing a few blocks together over months is a practical way to organize a longer plan without overcomplicating it. A deload is a planned, lighter week, usually dropping volume or intensity noticeably, placed every four to eight weeks depending on how hard the preceding blocks were. Its job is to let accumulated fatigue clear before it turns into a stall or an overuse injury. A deload is not a reward and not a sign of weakness, it is a scheduled maintenance stop that keeps long-term progress from breaking down.

You do not need a perfect system

Coaches new to programming often worry they need to pick the one correct periodization model. In practice, linear progression for a beginner, undulating variation once progress slows, and a deload every month or two when fatigue builds up will carry the overwhelming majority of clients for years. The model matters far less than actually tracking what a client did last time and nudging it up.

Laying out one session

A single session built around a goal generally follows the stack in the figure above: a brief warm up that raises tissue temperature and rehearses the movement pattern at light load, the main lift for that day performed first, two to four accessory exercises that support the main lift or address a weak point, and a short cool down or mobility piece to close. A hypertrophy-focused session might run a squat as the main lift for four sets of eight to twelve reps, followed by accessories like a lunge variation, a leg curl, and calf raises at higher reps. A strength-focused session on the same pattern might run the squat for five sets of three to five reps with longer rest, followed by lighter accessory work that does not compete with recovery from the main lift.

Laying out one week

At the week level, the job is to make sure the major movement patterns, squat, hinge, push, pull, and carry, all get trained without any single joint or muscle group being hit so often that it cannot recover. A common, simple structure for a general client trains three or four days a week, alternating an emphasis, for example a lower body and a upper body day repeated twice, or a push, pull, and legs rotation. Whatever the split, the same questions apply every time: does each major pattern appear at least once, is there enough rest between sessions that hit the same joint hard, and does weekly volume trend upward gradually rather than jumping around at random.

Education, not medical advice

Everything in this chapter is general programming education for coaching healthy adult clients. It is not medical advice, and it does not replace an individualized assessment. A client with a current injury, a diagnosed condition, or pain that does not resolve with normal training modification should be referred to a physician, physical therapist, or other qualified provider before you keep programming around it.

The takeaway

Programming is not mysterious once you separate the pieces. Progressive overload is the engine, the goal decides where the acute variables sit, main lifts claim the freshest effort while accessories fill in the gaps, and periodization is just the plan for changing the demand over weeks so the client keeps adapting without breaking down. You do not need a perfect system on day one. You need a goal, a sensible starting point on load, reps, rest, and volume, and the discipline to track what happened last session so you know what to nudge next time.
